## 2. Candidate book passage — draft material for later page writing

### Could a World Begin Again?

A history of Earth eventually reaches a boundary that is not a date. It is the boundary between what evidence can presently tell us and what we would still like to know. Crossing it does not mean abandoning science. It means becoming more careful about the difference between an explanation, an analogy and a question.

One question behind this book came from its author, Trev: could a catastrophe that drives matter inward also prepare the conditions for another world? He imagined a giant planet, its moons, rapidly increasing rotation, and an unseen interaction that might help organise the aftermath. The suggestion grew into a further question about dark matter and dark energy. Could collapse connect them in a way that produces an additional force?

These are proposals to investigate, not events this book claims have happened.

Some of the ingredients have familiar physical foundations. For a system losing little angular momentum, contraction can increase its rotation rate. Matter that cannot fall directly inward may instead remain in orbit, exchange angular momentum, collide or escape. What survives depends on the initial conditions and on how energy is transported. A dramatic collapse does not, by itself, guarantee a rebound, a disc, a planet or a habitat.

There is also an important constraint on the Jupiter example. In the spherical, fixed-mass Newtonian approximation, reducing a body's radius leaves its gravitational field at a fixed exterior distance unchanged. Its moons do not automatically lose their sideways motion and plunge inward. A proposed catastrophe therefore needs a real mechanism for changing their orbits, not just a smaller central body.

The dark component requires its own distinction. Dark matter is inferred through gravitational effects, while dark energy names the unknown cause or description of accelerated cosmic expansion in the standard cosmological account. Their similar names do not establish that they combine during an implosion. [S17-P01, S17-P02]

Published calculations do provide a narrower connection. In a particular model of a supermassive star forming and collapsing at the centre of a dark-matter halo, the dark matter develops a density distribution different from an idealised black-hole-growth calculation. That is a model of existing dark matter responding to gravity, not a detection of a new force and not a calculation of an imploding Jupiter. [S17-P05]

The author's stronger suggestion therefore has a clear task ahead of it. It must identify something that conventional gravity, gas dynamics and known observational uncertainties would not already explain. A debris disc would not suffice. Nor would merely finding more gravitating mass than first estimated. The additional interaction would need to predict a particular, measurable difference under stated conditions.

Even a successful mechanism for making a rocky body would leave other questions. Can material cool and remain bound? Can an atmosphere and suitable environment persist? How would chemistry become biology? The route to a planet, the route to a habitat and the origin of life are separate investigations.

For now, the honest conclusion is neither that the proposal has been proved nor that asking it was pointless. It is that the question has been preserved in a form that can be examined. The next step is to decide what should happen without the proposed interaction, then specify what should happen differently with it. An idea becomes stronger when it risks a precise failure, not when every possible outcome is treated as support.

## 5. Make the question capable of a test

The following is a design checklist, not a completed simulation or a set of confirmed predictions.

### A. Specify a physically possible system

Choose one object class and one trigger at a time: for example a giant-planet disruption, stellar collapse, or supermassive-star formation inside a halo. Do not transfer a result between those scales without new calculations. A Jupiter experiment must identify a physical disruption mechanism; spontaneous black-hole collapse of present-day Jupiter is not a demonstrated starting condition.

### B. Define the comparison model

Record mass, composition, radius, rotation, orbital architecture and any assumed dark-matter distribution. Account for mass-energy and angular momentum carried away by radiation, ejecta and escaping bodies. In a spherical, fixed-mass weak-field limit, g(r) = GM/r^2 at fixed exterior r; shrinking the source alone does not supply an extra distant inward force. With negligible external torque, L = I omega constrains spin-up but does not determine a unique final state.

A Newtonian baseline for appropriate slow, weak-field systems can include ordinary and dark-matter mass densities in Poisson's equation, with gas dynamics and a collisionless description where applicable. Relativistic collapse requires a relativistic treatment. Simply writing a dark-matter density into an equation does not demonstrate that it is large enough to matter in the selected system.

### C. Specify the additional interaction rather than naming it

Before Version B yields quantitative predictions, define its fields or particles, coupling, trigger, range, strength, time dependence, energy exchange and conservation behaviour. Demonstrate that the comparison model is recovered in a stated limit. None of those choices has yet been made here; do not invent a force law and attribute it to the author as something already established.

### D. Define a discriminating observable

A possible comparison quantity is a residual acceleration, a_res(r,t) = a_observed(r,t) - a_baseline(r,t), or a difference in an inferred density profile. Those expressions define a measurement comparison; they do not predict that a nonzero signal exists or identify its cause.

A useful test needs a predicted sign, magnitude and spatial or temporal pattern from the additional model, a specified parameter range, and a measurement with adequate sensitivity. Modelling errors, unaccounted ordinary matter, known dark-matter restructuring, gas effects and instrument systematics are competing explanations. For remnant waveforms, first check what published gravitational-wave models predict and whether the necessary data and detector sensitivity exist.

### E. State what would rule out that version

A null result can exclude a particular parameter range only if the experiment or observation could have detected the signal that version predicted. Not finding a planet or a disc does not disprove every conceivable recycling route. Finding one does not establish the dark-sector interaction. Agreement with conventional models limits additions; it is not a licence to keep changing the prediction after observing the result.
