## Evidence boundaries
- Living relatives are not unchanged ancestors, and a fossil near a branching point is not necessarily the direct common ancestor.
- A fossil's first preserved occurrence, its taxonomic identification and its lineage's origin are different claims.
- Molecular relationships and divergence dates retain sampling, calibration and rate-model assumptions.
- A stem-group assignment is not crown membership; names must not silently imply a closer relationship than the analysis establishes.
- Isolated bones attributed by size, anatomy and faunal association are not the same evidence as an associated skeleton.
- Anatomy constrains possible function without preserving every behaviour, sensory experience or ecological interaction.
- Grasping, visual organisation, locomotion and feeding capacities can change at different times; one fossil is not a universal ancestral package.
- Scholarly reviews record attributed arguments and do not add new primary anatomical observations merely by restating them.
- Climate, forest structure and a population's resources are distinct conditions; a warming interval does not establish a continuous forest dispersal corridor.
- Correlation of occurrences is not the measured duration of an individual journey; a revised diagnosis can change an inferred geographical history.
- A call to revise Archicebus diagnosis is not presented as universally accepted synonymy with Teilhardina.
- Toe bones from Wyoming are not attached to the Chinese Archicebus specimen; bony support and inferred grooming behaviour remain distinct.
- No exact origin, first-ever ranking or inevitable human endpoint is imposed on incomplete evidence.
- Continental affinity and a proposed dispersal route do not preserve an actual journey; nearby attributed fossils do not necessarily represent one individual.
- Detrital maximum ages, original burial ages and lineage arrival dates differ; reworked Ashaninkacebus material does not supply a secure original stratum.
- Catarrhine branch assignments constrain minimum presence without identifying the actual common ancestor or a hard maximum age from a stem relative.
- Masripithecus geographical implications remain a hypothesis; the Rusinga and Moroto environmental reconstructions concern different local settings.
- Missing tail bones alone do not establish taillessness; calibrated anatomy, gene regulation and adaptive advantage are separate questions.
- Danuvius interpretations retain the published challenge and reply; upright posture, clambering and habitual human walking are not equivalent.
- Genomic lineage sorting and subsequent gene flow are distinct processes; a regional genealogy is not a whole-species tree or an exact divergence date.
- Anatomical diagnosis, locomotor inference, fossil age and direct ancestry are separate claims; proximity to a divergence date does not establish a direct ancestor.
- “Intermediate” morphology describes comparative anatomy or model position, not a literal halfway organism or an inevitable evolutionary step.
- An isolated toe or other single element can support a locomotor hypothesis without reconstructing the whole gait.
- Habitual terrestrial bipedality and meaningful arboreal behaviour can coexist; neither category is treated as all-or-nothing.
- Temporal overlap weakens a simple no-overlap anagenetic sequence but does not by itself exclude every branching ancestor-descendant relationship.
- A footprint preserves a locomotor event, not a secure species identity; body size, sex, social grouping and taxonomic attribution require additional inference.
- New comparative studies can strengthen an interpretation without erasing earlier published disputes or the limits of incomplete fossils.
