# CHAPTER SIX — THE OXYGEN REVOLUTION
## Page 178 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### THE SULFUR FINGERPRINT OF AN ANOXIC SKY

Sulfur has several stable isotopes.

The most abundant is sulfur-32.

There is sulfur-33.

Sulfur-34.

And sulfur-36.

Most physical and chemical processes separate isotopes according to their mass.

A heavier isotope behaves slightly differently from a lighter one.

The effects scale in predictable relationships.

This is **mass-dependent fractionation**.

Archean sulfur often refuses to follow those rules.

Its isotopes contain anomalies that cannot be explained by ordinary mass-dependent chemistry.

This is called **mass-independent fractionation of sulfur**:

S-MIF.

The leading explanation begins in the atmosphere.

Volcanoes release sulfur gases.

Ultraviolet radiation breaks and transforms those molecules through photochemical reactions.

Under an atmosphere with extremely little oxygen and no substantial ozone shield, different sulfur-bearing products acquire unusual isotope combinations.

Those products fall to Earth's surface.

Some end up as sulfide.

Others as sulfate or elemental sulfur.

Sediments preserve the anomaly.

A rock therefore carries an atmospheric photochemical fingerprint.

Why does oxygen erase it?

As atmospheric oxygen rises, several things change.

Sulfur gases are oxidised more efficiently.

An ozone layer begins screening ultraviolet wavelengths involved in the photochemistry.

Atmospheric and oceanic sulfur reservoirs mix and cycle differently.

The unusual atmospheric isotope products can no longer be produced and preserved in the same globally visible way.

The disappearance of large S-MIF anomalies from the sedimentary record is therefore one of the strongest indicators that atmospheric O₂ crossed a critical threshold.

How high was that threshold?

Older literature often quoted around **10⁻⁵ of the present atmospheric level**.

More recent photochemical work commonly places the relevant threshold closer to **10⁻⁶ PAL**, although the exact value remains model-dependent.

Either number is tiny compared with today's atmosphere.

That is the crucial point.

The disappearance of S-MIF does not mean oxygen instantly rose to modern levels.

It means the atmosphere became oxidising enough to change sulfur photochemistry fundamentally.

This makes S-MIF unusually powerful.

Red iron in a rock can form locally.

A trace metal can respond to local seawater.

S-MIF is tied to atmospheric photochemistry and can therefore constrain the global atmosphere.

But the rock record complicates the simple story.

Sulfur bearing an old MIF signature can be stored in sedimentary rocks.

Later, after atmospheric oxygen has risen, oxidative weathering can release that ancient sulfur.

It can then be transported and redeposited into younger sediment.

A younger rock may therefore contain an **old atmospheric memory**.

This is called a crustal or sedimentary memory effect.

Some sequences also show S-MIF disappearing and reappearing across the transition.

That could reflect actual atmospheric oscillations.

It could reflect recycling of old sulfur.

Different basins need not record the same mixture.

So geologists no longer treat the youngest grain carrying S-MIF as an automatic date for the last anoxic atmosphere.

They combine sulfur isotopes with radiometric ages, sedimentology, trace metals and other redox proxies.

Still, the broad message remains exceptionally robust.

For much of the Archean, the atmosphere was oxygen-poor enough for S-MIF to be generated and preserved.

Across the Great Oxidation transition, that atmospheric regime ended.

And once the old signal stops returning as a primary atmospheric product, Earth has crossed a threshold it never permanently crosses back.

## Research / fact-check notes
- S-MIF arises from atmospheric sulfur photochemistry under very low-O₂ conditions and is one of the strongest global proxies for an Archean anoxic atmosphere.
- 2025–2026 reviews commonly cite a disappearance threshold near ~10⁻⁶ PAL, with model dependence and older ~10⁻⁵ PAL estimates retained in the literature.
- Izon et al. (2018) and later work demonstrate regional/asynchronous records and crustal-memory effects from recycling older MIF-bearing sulfur.
- The last preserved S-MIF in a sediment is not automatically the exact date of atmospheric oxygenation.

**Decision:** explain S-MIF as a global atmospheric proxy while keeping threshold and preservation complexities explicit.
