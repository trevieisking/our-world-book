# CHAPTER SIX — THE OXYGEN REVOLUTION
## Page 177 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### THE GREAT OXIDATION EVENT IS NOT THE FIRST OXYGEN

The last chapter ended with a paradox.

Microbes were making oxygen.

The atmosphere was still almost oxygen-free.

That paradox lasted for hundreds of millions of years.

The **Great Oxidation Event**, or GOE, is the interval when the balance finally changed enough for free molecular oxygen to accumulate persistently in Earth's atmosphere and surface environments.

It is not the origin of oxygenic photosynthesis.

That metabolism is older.

It is not the first molecule of O₂ ever produced.

Local oxygen oases existed earlier.

It is not the moment the entire ocean became oxygenated.

Large parts of the ocean remained anoxic long afterwards.

And it is not a single clean date.

The word **event** makes the transition sound abrupt.

Some researchers increasingly prefer to think in terms of a prolonged oxidation **episode**.

Current reviews commonly place the first major atmospheric rise somewhere around the Archean–Proterozoic transition, beginning roughly **2.5 to 2.4 billion years ago**.

Different proxies record different thresholds.

Some atmospheric signals change first.

Shallow marine environments respond later or unevenly.

Deep waters can remain reducing.

Oxygen may rise, fall and spread geographically before a new planetary state becomes persistent.

A 2026 review of the Great Oxidation Event emphasises how wide the remaining range of defensible atmospheric oxygen histories still is.

Researchers agree on the broad transition from an essentially anoxic atmosphere to one containing persistent free oxygen.

They disagree over the detailed trajectory and absolute O₂ concentrations.

That uncertainty is not surprising.

No ancient air sample survives in a bottle.

We reconstruct the atmosphere from reactions it caused.

Before persistent oxygen, river systems could carry minerals such as pyrite and uraninite as detrital grains because they were not rapidly destroyed by oxidative weathering.

After oxygen increased, those reduced minerals became unstable at Earth's surface.

Red beds containing oxidised iron become more common.

Trace metals respond.

Weathering changes.

Sulfur photochemistry changes.

The ocean begins developing new redox structure.

The most powerful atmospheric clue comes from sulfur isotopes.

It tells us that the atmosphere crossed a chemical threshold.

But even that record has complications.

Old sulfur carrying an ancient atmospheric signature can be eroded from older rocks and redeposited after oxygen rises.

Regional sedimentary basins can preserve different histories.

Atmospheric oxygen itself may have oscillated around critical thresholds.

So the GOE is best understood as a **state transition** rather than a birthday.

Before it, oxygen could be produced locally but could not persist globally.

After it, the atmosphere never returned permanently to the Archean state.

That makes the GOE one of the largest environmental transitions in Earth's history.

It changes which minerals survive weathering.

It changes which gases persist.

It changes nutrient cycles.

It changes the energy available to biology.

It changes ultraviolet radiation as ozone begins to form.

It changes the chemistry of oceans and continents.

But to understand how we know that this happened, we need to read one of the strangest isotope signals in geology.

Sulfur can carry a fingerprint made high in an oxygen-poor atmosphere.

When that fingerprint disappears, the sky has changed.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Crockford et al. (2026), *Communications Earth & Environment*: GOE = interval when O₂ first accumulated in the atmosphere; detailed trajectories and absolute O₂ levels remain strongly debated.
- Current usage spans a transition beginning ~2.5–2.4 Ga, with different proxies defining different thresholds and persistence.
- Oxygenic photosynthesis predates the GOE; local oxygen oases predate persistent atmospheric oxygenation.
- Persistent atmospheric oxygen does not imply immediate oxygenation of the whole ocean.

**Decision:** the GOE is defined as a protracted atmospheric/surface redox state transition, not the origin of oxygen production or a single instant.
