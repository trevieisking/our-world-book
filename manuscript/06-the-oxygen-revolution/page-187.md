# CHAPTER SIX — THE OXYGEN REVOLUTION
## Page 187 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### BANDED IRON FORMATIONS ARE NOT ONE SIMPLE STORY

Few rocks look more like a planetary signal.

Dark iron-rich layers.

Silica-rich bands.

Repeated again and again.

**Banded iron formations**, or BIFs, preserve enormous quantities of iron deposited from ancient seawater.

They are often explained with a simple sentence:

cyanobacteria released oxygen,

oxygen rusted dissolved iron,

iron fell to the seafloor.

That mechanism can operate.

It is not the whole history.

BIFs are much older than the Great Oxidation Event.

Iron formations occur deep in the Archean.

Their abundance becomes especially large between roughly **2.7 and 2.4 billion years ago**, before and around the GOE.

Major iron formation deposition later declines, although important younger episodes occur.

If BIFs simply required an oxygenated atmosphere, their timing would be backwards.

The starting ingredient is clear.

Anoxic seawater can carry much more dissolved Fe²⁺ than modern oxygenated surface water.

Hydrothermal systems and water–rock reactions supplied iron to the ocean.

Something then oxidised Fe²⁺ toward Fe³⁺, causing poorly soluble iron minerals to precipitate.

Several processes can perform that oxidation.

Molecular oxygen can.

Anoxygenic photoferrotrophic microbes can use light and Fe²⁺ as an electron donor without producing oxygen.

Ultraviolet-driven photochemistry may contribute in some settings.

Mixing and mineral reactions matter.

The dominant mechanism probably varied through time and between basins.

Later metamorphism also transformed the original precipitates into minerals such as hematite and magnetite, complicating reconstruction of the first iron phases.

This means BIFs record **iron oxidation**.

They do not automatically identify the oxidant.

New geochemistry can sometimes narrow the possibilities.

A 2025 study analysed nitrogen isotopes across Neoarchean and earliest Paleoproterozoic BIFs in Western Australia.

The record suggests seawater oxygenation expanded into BIF depositional environments from about 2.63 to 2.60 billion years ago.

Then oxygen availability declined around 2.48 billion years ago before rising again through younger iron formations approaching the GOE.

The result reinforces a theme we have already seen:

oxygenation was not monotonic.

The ocean could become more oxidised locally and then less oxidised again.

BIF deposition sits inside that oscillating redox world.

The decline of major BIF deposition after the GOE also has more than one possible cause.

As oxygenated surface waters expanded, dissolved Fe²⁺ could be oxidised closer to its sources or before it accumulated through large water masses.

Changes in hydrothermal iron supply, ocean circulation, silica chemistry and sulfur availability also matter.

Where sulfide becomes abundant, iron can be trapped as pyrite rather than oxide.

So the end of one style of iron formation cannot be read as an oxygen gauge with a single calibration.

BIFs are still extraordinary archives.

Their iron isotopes.

Nitrogen isotopes.

Trace metals.

Rare-earth elements.

Layering.

Mineralogy.

All preserve parts of ocean chemistry.

But their lesson is not:

“oxygen appeared and the ocean rusted once.”

It is:

**the balance among iron supply, biological activity, oxidation mechanisms and ocean circulation changed repeatedly as Earth approached and crossed the oxygen threshold.**

That makes BIFs records of transition rather than a single event.

And as oxygen begins attacking sulfide on land, another dissolved ion expands in seawater.

Sulfate.

Its rise gives sulfur-metabolising microbes a much larger oceanic reservoir to work with.

## Research / fact-check notes
- BIFs predate the GOE and peak in abundance broadly ~2.7–2.4 Ga; substantial younger iron-formation episodes also occur.
- Fe²⁺ oxidation can involve O₂, anoxygenic photoferrotrophy, abiotic photochemistry and other processes; no universal BIF mechanism is assumed.
- 2025 *Nature Geoscience* nitrogen-isotope study records an oxygenation oscillation in Hamersley Basin BIF depositional environments between ~2.63 and 2.45 Ga.
- Decline in major BIF deposition after the GOE reflects changing Fe redox/supply, sulfur chemistry and circulation, not oxygen alone.

**Decision:** BIFs are treated as multi-process ocean-redox archives rather than simple direct products of atmospheric oxygen.
