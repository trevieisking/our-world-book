# CHAPTER SIX — THE OXYGEN REVOLUTION
## Page 180 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### THE OXYGEN BUDGET

Photosynthesis makes oxygen.

That is not enough.

If every molecule of O₂ reacts with something reduced, atmospheric oxygen stays near zero forever.

The Great Oxidation Event therefore requires a budget.

**Sources of oxidising power must exceed sinks.**

At the biological level, oxygenic photosynthesis can be written schematically as:

carbon dioxide + water + light -> organic matter + oxygen.

But if the organic matter is later completely respired or oxidised back to carbon dioxide, the oxygen is consumed again.

No permanent atmospheric gain remains.

For photosynthesis to leave net O₂ behind over geological time, some reduced product must escape re-oxidation.

The classic route is **organic-carbon burial**.

A fraction of biomass becomes sediment.

If that organic carbon is buried and isolated from oxygen, the oxidising equivalent released by photosynthesis can remain in the surface environment.

Burial of reduced sulfur such as pyrite can also contribute to the long-term redox budget.

On the other side are sinks.

Volcanoes release reduced gases.

Hydrothermal systems supply reduced compounds.

Fresh crust contains Fe²⁺ and other oxygen-consuming minerals.

The ocean contains reduced iron and sulfur.

Methane and hydrogen consume oxidants through atmospheric chemistry.

Weathering continually exposes new material.

The atmosphere gains oxygen only when the integrated source exceeds the integrated sink.

This means the GOE can happen in more than one way.

Biological production can increase.

Organic burial can increase.

A major sink can decline.

Continental weathering and burial can change.

Volcanic gases can become less reducing.

Hydrogen can escape to space, leaving the planet more oxidised.

Nutrient availability can change primary productivity.

Tectonics can alter multiple fluxes at once.

No single mechanism has yet won the argument.

A 2026 review of the GOE makes this uncertainty explicit.

The broad source–sink framework is well established.

The relative contributions of the individual terms remain poorly constrained.

This is partly because many of the controlling fluxes leave indirect geological evidence.

We cannot measure a 2.4-billion-year-old global volcanic hydrogen flux.

We infer it from rocks, mantle evolution and models.

We cannot count every photosynthetic cell.

We estimate productivity from nutrient cycles and carbon burial.

We cannot directly sample atmospheric methane.

We reconstruct constraints from photochemistry and isotope records.

So a good explanation of the GOE should not be:

“one thing happened and oxygen rose.”

It should be:

**the redox budget crossed a threshold.**

This also helps explain why atmospheric oxygen can respond nonlinearly.

Imagine production and consumption are almost balanced.

A modest increase in burial or modest decrease in reduced-gas flux can suddenly leave a surplus of O₂.

Once oxygen accumulates, it changes atmospheric chemistry.

Ozone appears.

Methane lifetimes change.

Weathering changes.

Nutrient delivery changes.

Those feedbacks can stabilise or destabilise the new state.

The GOE is therefore both a biological and geological transition.

Microbes supply the oxygen.

The Earth system determines whether it survives.

The next pages examine the largest terms in that budget without pretending the accounting is complete.

We begin with the simplest idea in the whole problem.

If photosynthesis releases oxygen when it makes organic carbon, then **burying the carbon leaves oxygen behind**.

That logic was recognised long before anyone knew the age of the Great Oxidation Event.

It remains central today.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Long-term O₂ accumulation reflects imbalance between biological/geochemical oxidant sources and reducing sinks.
- Burial of photosynthetically produced organic carbon leaves oxidising power behind; reduced sulfur burial can also affect the redox budget.
- Crockford et al. (2026) emphasise broad agreement on source–sink imbalance but major uncertainty over the dominant drivers and O₂ trajectory.
- Candidate controls include productivity/nutrients, organic burial, volcanic/metamorphic reductant flux, weathering, hydrogen escape and tectonics.

**Decision:** no single GOE trigger is selected; the source–sink threshold is established, while individual contributions remain open.
