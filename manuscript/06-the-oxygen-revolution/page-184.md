# CHAPTER SIX — THE OXYGEN REVOLUTION
## Page 184 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### WHEN OXYGEN BECOMES GEOLOGY

A new atmosphere changes the rocks touching it.

Before the Great Oxidation Event, river sediments could preserve detrital grains of minerals that are unstable in oxygen-rich surface conditions.

Pyrite.

Uraninite.

Other reduced phases.

Carry them through oxygenated water today and they weather.

Their disappearance from younger sedimentary records was one of the earliest clues that Earth's atmosphere had changed.

Iron records the change spectacularly.

Ferrous iron is soluble under anoxic conditions.

Oxidise it and ferric iron minerals form.

Weathered continental surfaces become increasingly capable of producing oxidised iron-rich sediments.

Red beds — sedimentary rocks coloured by ferric iron oxides — become more characteristic after atmospheric oxygen rises, although their timing and interpretation must be tied to local depositional conditions.

Sulfur weathering changes too.

Pyrite exposed on land oxidises.

Sulfate delivery to the ocean increases.

That creates more substrate for microbial sulfate reduction.

The sulfur cycle becomes larger and more internally recycled.

Trace metals that were previously immobile under reducing conditions become easier to weather and transport.

Molybdenum.

Uranium.

Rhenium.

Vanadium.

Their concentrations and isotope ratios begin recording a world with new oxidative pathways.

This is why oxygenation is preserved by so many independent proxies.

Oxygen itself is a gas and vanishes from ancient rocks.

Its reactions remain.

The ocean responds unevenly.

Shallow waters in contact with the atmosphere can gain oxygen first.

Productive microbial mats generate additional local O₂.

Deeper waters can remain ferruginous.

Where sulfate increases and organic matter is abundant, microbial sulfate reduction can produce sulfide.

Some basins become **euxinic** — anoxic and sulfidic.

Others remain iron-rich.

The post-GOE ocean is therefore not one uniformly blue oxygenated body.

It becomes chemically stratified.

Oxygen above.

Iron-rich or sulfide-rich waters below in many places.

Redox fronts move with circulation, productivity and basin geometry.

This mixed ocean will persist through much of the Proterozoic.

Biology responds just as unevenly.

Strict anaerobes retreat from oxygenated niches but continue thriving where oxygen remains absent.

Some organisms evolve better oxygen-defence systems.

Others use O₂ as a terminal electron acceptor.

Aerobic respiration can extract much more usable energy from many organic substrates than common anaerobic pathways.

That energetic advantage will become important later for the evolution of complex cells.

But the GOE does not instantly produce eukaryotes, animals or an oxygenated deep ocean.

It changes the **possibility space**.

More energy becomes available in oxygenated niches.

New enzymes evolve.

New minerals become accessible.

New nutrient cycles appear.

Toxicity and opportunity spread together.

This first eight-page block has taken us to the threshold itself.

We know the GOE is not the invention of photosynthesis.

We know S-MIF records the loss of an anoxic atmospheric regime.

We know the transition was protracted and spatially uneven.

We know oxygen accumulation reflects a source–sink imbalance rather than one magic trigger.

We know climate, nutrients, tectonics and redox cycling all feed back on that imbalance.

Now we can follow what oxygen actually did to the planet.

The next part of the chapter moves through the evidence it left behind:

weathered continents,

iron formations,

sulfate-rich oceans,

new mineral species,

changing microbial metabolisms,

and a biosphere forced to adapt to its own waste.

The oxygen revolution has begun.

It is nowhere near finished.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Loss of detrital pyrite/uraninite and appearance/expansion of oxidised continental-weathering products are classic evidence for rising atmospheric O₂.
- Sulfide oxidation increases sulfate delivery and changes sulfur cycling.
- GOE ocean oxygenation was heterogeneous; shallow oxic waters coexisted with widespread ferruginous/euxinic deeper waters.
- Aerobic respiration offers a large energetic advantage but complex life does not appear as an immediate consequence of the GOE.

**Decision:** introduce geological/ecological consequences without implying uniformly oxygenated oceans or instant complex-life emergence.

**Transition:** pages 185–192 will track oxidative weathering, red beds, iron formations, sulfate expansion, ocean redox stratification and the changing mineral inventory.
