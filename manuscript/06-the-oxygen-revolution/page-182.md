# CHAPTER SIX — THE OXYGEN REVOLUTION
## Page 182 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### GEOLOGY CHANGES THE SINKS

Imagine photosynthetic oxygen production stays roughly constant.

The atmosphere can still oxygenate if the sinks weaken.

That makes the solid Earth part of the story.

Volcanoes do not release only carbon dioxide.

They also release hydrogen, carbon monoxide, sulfur species and other gases whose oxidation consumes O₂.

The balance depends on pressure, temperature, magma chemistry and the redox state of the mantle and crust.

Hydrothermal systems deliver reduced iron, sulfur and hydrogen to the ocean.

Fresh volcanic rock contains minerals that can consume oxygen during weathering.

Metamorphism returns reduced carbon and sulfur from buried sediments.

These fluxes form a geological appetite for oxidants.

If that appetite declines, the same biological oxygen production leaves a larger atmospheric surplus.

One influential family of models therefore links the GOE to a long-term decline in the flux of reduced volcanic or metamorphic gases.

The exact cause remains debated.

The mantle may have evolved.

Continental crust may have grown and changed the style of volcanism.

Tectonic reorganisation may have altered burial and degassing.

Subduction carries oxidised and reduced material into Earth's interior and returns gases through arcs.

The oxidation state of volcanic gases need not change in a simple monotonic way.

This is why “the mantle oxidised” is too crude as a complete explanation.

Continents matter in another way.

More emergent crust creates more weathering.

Weathering can consume oxygen when reduced minerals are exposed.

But it also delivers phosphorus and other nutrients to the ocean.

Those nutrients can stimulate photosynthesis and organic burial.

Weathering can therefore both consume O₂ and help the biosphere make more of it.

The sign of the feedback depends on which flux dominates and on the redox state of the material being weathered.

Tectonic uplift can expose fresh rock.

Erosion can accelerate nutrient delivery.

New basins can preserve organic matter.

Changes in sedimentation can alter pyrite burial.

Large igneous provinces can change volcanic gas fluxes and nutrient supply simultaneously.

This complexity is not an excuse to avoid explanation.

It tells us why the GOE is a systems problem.

No single geological variable has to jump suddenly at exactly the same time as oxygen.

Several slowly changing terms can push a balanced system toward instability.

Near the threshold, a small additional change can become decisive.

The newest reviews emphasise exactly this point.

There is broad agreement that oxygenic photosynthesis supplied the oxidant.

There is broad agreement that a source–sink imbalance allowed accumulation.

But multiple combinations of productivity, burial, degassing, weathering, mantle evolution, nutrient supply and hydrogen escape can satisfy those conditions.

The rock record has not yet reduced them to one unique history.

That leaves us with a useful scientific target.

Each model should predict something different.

A nutrient-driven rise predicts changes in phosphorus and productivity proxies.

A reductant-flux decline predicts a different oxygen trajectory.

A weathering-driven change should appear in continental and marine geochemistry.

Tectonic models should align with basin formation, volcanism and crustal evolution.

The hypotheses can therefore be compared rather than merely named.

This is the correct way to treat a complicated cause.

Not:

“we do not know, so anything goes.”

But:

“several mechanisms remain compatible with the data, and each has consequences that can be tested.”

The GOE may ultimately turn out to have no single trigger.

A planet can cross a threshold because several slow changes happen to align.

And once oxygen begins accumulating, it changes the system that controls it.

The next feedback involves one of the most important greenhouse gases of the Archean.

Methane.

Oxygen attacks it.

That links atmospheric chemistry to climate.

At almost the same time Earth was oxidising, it was also entering some of the most severe glaciations in its history.

The coincidence is difficult to ignore.

The causality is much harder to prove.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Reduced volcanic/metamorphic gases, hydrothermal reductants and oxidative weathering are major O₂ sinks in GOE models.
- Declining reductant flux, tectonic evolution, continental growth/weathering, nutrient delivery and hydrogen escape are all active explanatory families.
- Crockford et al. (2026) emphasise that the dominant causal balance remains unresolved and atmospheric O₂ trajectories remain highly uncertain.

**Decision:** solid-Earth evolution is treated as a changing set of source/sink boundary conditions, not a single proven mantle-oxidation trigger.
