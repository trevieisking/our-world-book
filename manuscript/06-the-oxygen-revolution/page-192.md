# CHAPTER SIX — THE OXYGEN REVOLUTION
## Page 192 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### A NEW SURFACE OVER AN OLD DEEP OCEAN

By the middle of the Great Oxidation transition, Earth has become chemically divided.

The atmosphere contains persistent oxygen.

Continental weathering increasingly produces oxidised minerals.

Rivers carry sulfate and redox-sensitive metals toward the sea.

Shallow ocean waters can sustain O₂ over broad regions.

Yet descend through the water column and the old Archean chemistry survives.

Ferruginous deep water.

Sulfidic basins.

Methanogenic sediments.

Anaerobic microbial ecosystems.

The new Earth lies above the old one.

This vertical separation explains several features that can otherwise look contradictory.

Why do oxidised red beds coexist in time with anoxic marine shales?

Different environments.

Why can sulfate delivery increase while deep water remains iron-rich?

Sulfate is delivered from land, but reduction and iron chemistry remove it before it dominates the whole ocean.

Why can aerobic metabolism expand while anaerobic pathways remain ecologically important?

Oxygen is patchy in the ocean even when it is persistent in the atmosphere.

Why do oxygen-sensitive trace metals show complicated records rather than one irreversible jump?

Their behaviour depends on local water-column chemistry and burial.

The GOE creates **heterogeneity**.

Not instant uniformity.

This is important for the history of life.

An anaerobe does not experience “the atmosphere.”

It experiences the chemistry surrounding its cell.

A methanogen living deep in sediment can survive on an oxygenated planet if oxygen is consumed before reaching it.

A sulfate reducer can flourish precisely because oxidative weathering supplies more sulfate to an anoxic habitat.

An iron reducer can persist beneath an oxygenated surface layer.

Oxygenic photosynthesizers keep producing O₂ at the top.

The same planetary transition expands aerobic niches and preserves anaerobic ones.

Evolution now explores a larger chemical landscape.

New redox gradients appear.

New electron acceptors become available.

New defensive systems become valuable.

New metals become limiting.

New symbioses become possible.

The deep ocean will not become broadly oxygenated for a very long time.

Earth's oxygen history is therefore not one event followed by a modern state.

It is a staircase.

The GOE is the first major step.

The 2026 stagewise-oxygenation synthesis makes this explicit: atmospheric oxygenation generally precedes oceanic oxygenation, and the oceans remain spatially and temporally heterogeneous through enormous spans of time.

This prevents us from using “oxygenated Earth” as shorthand for “modern Earth.”

After the GOE, atmospheric O₂ is persistent.

That alone is revolutionary.

But the deep ocean can still be chemically alien to us.

The mineral record changes.

The atmosphere changes.

The ecological opportunities change.

The planet has crossed a one-way boundary without reaching its final condition.

The next eight pages follow what that did to organisms.

Oxygen damages anaerobic molecular machinery.

It also allows far more energetic respiration.

It creates ozone shielding.

It changes the economics of metabolism.

It may alter evolutionary rates and ecological complexity.

And across the later stages of the GOE, oxygen itself appears to rise and fall dramatically before settling into a lower Proterozoic state.

The revolution has two stories.

How Earth became oxidised.

And how life learned to live with the oxidant it created.

We have followed the first.

Now we follow the second.
