# CHAPTER SIX — THE OXYGEN REVOLUTION
## Page 186 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### WHEN CONTINENTS TURN RED

Red sedimentary rock is one of the most visible products of an oxidising surface.

The colour usually comes from ferric iron oxides, especially hematite.

Iron in the Fe³⁺ state coats grains.

A sandstone that began pale can become rust-red.

Red beds therefore seem like an obvious oxygen marker.

The complication is timing.

Groundwater can oxidise iron long after a sediment was deposited.

Burial fluids can move hematite.

Heating can alter iron minerals.

A modern red colour does not automatically record a red ancient river.

To use a red bed as evidence for atmospheric oxygen, researchers need to show that ferric iron formed during or soon after deposition.

A study published in 2024 did exactly that for Huronian rocks in Canada.

The researchers revisited the transition from younger terrestrial strata containing detrital pyrite and uraninite to the oldest convincingly reddened fluvial sandstones.

Inside quartz grains, they found early hematite-rich coatings trapped beneath later quartz overgrowths.

The geometry matters.

The iron coating formed before the quartz cement sealed it inside the grain boundary.

That demonstrates oxidation during early meteoric diagenesis rather than much later alteration.

Geochronological constraints place these terrestrial red beds at roughly **2.31 billion years ago**.

That age closely overlaps the disappearance of strong atmospheric S-MIF signals in correlative Paleoproterozoic successions.

Two very different archives therefore point toward the same broad atmospheric transition.

Sulfur photochemistry changes in the sky.

Iron weathering changes on land.

This is what convergence looks like.

The red beds also show why atmospheric oxygen matters even at concentrations far below modern levels.

Continental weathering does not need today's 21 percent O₂ to change.

A small persistent atmospheric reservoir can continually expose minerals to oxidation through rainwater and groundwater.

Unlike a transient oxygen oasis, the oxidant is available across broad terrestrial landscapes.

The continents begin changing chemically.

Iron becomes immobilised as ferric oxides.

Sulfur becomes more oxidised and mobile as sulfate.

Uranium can be mobilised in oxidised form.

Manganese oxidation becomes increasingly possible where oxygen concentrations are high enough.

The sedimentary record becomes richer in redox contrasts.

Yet the colour map is not globally uniform.

A waterlogged sediment can remain reducing beneath an oxygenated atmosphere.

Deep groundwater can remain anoxic.

Organic-rich mud consumes oxygen rapidly.

Local depositional conditions still control mineral preservation.

The GOE does not paint every rock red.

It makes red terrestrial weathering **possible at scale**.

And once ferric iron becomes widespread in continental sediment, the contrast with the ancient ocean becomes striking.

On land, iron increasingly rusts.

In much of the ocean, dissolved Fe²⁺ can still persist below oxygenated surface waters.

That vertical separation is part of the next stage of Earth's redox evolution.

To understand it, we need to revisit the most famous iron-rich rocks in deep time.

Banded iron formations.

They are often introduced as evidence that oxygen suddenly rusted the ancient oceans.

The real story is more complicated — and more interesting.

## Research / fact-check notes
- 2024 study of Huronian terrestrial red beds identifies primary/early diagenetic hematite coatings and places widespread terrestrial Fe oxidation at ~2.31 Ga.
- Red colour alone is not a reliable atmospheric proxy because hematite can form during later diagenesis/alteration.
- The red-bed transition broadly converges with sulfur-isotope evidence for atmospheric oxygenation.

**Decision:** use demonstrably early hematite formation, not colour alone, as the oxygenation evidence.
