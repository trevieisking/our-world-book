# CHAPTER SIX — THE OXYGEN REVOLUTION
## Page 185 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### MINERALS THAT STOP SURVIVING RIVERS

Some of the clearest evidence for atmospheric change is not a new mineral appearing.

It is an old mineral disappearing from places where it used to survive.

Before persistent atmospheric oxygen, river systems could carry grains of **pyrite**, FeS₂.

They could also carry reduced uranium minerals such as **uraninite**, UO₂.

These minerals are chemically unstable under strongly oxidising surface conditions.

Expose pyrite to oxygenated water and it oxidises.

Sulfur becomes sulfate.

Iron becomes ferric iron minerals.

Expose uraninite and uranium is oxidised to more soluble forms that can be mobilised in water.

So why do ancient river and conglomerate deposits contain detrital grains of pyrite and uraninite?

Because the atmosphere and surface waters through which those grains travelled contained very little oxygen.

The grains survived erosion.

They survived river transport.

They were deposited as physical particles.

Across the Great Oxidation transition, that changes.

Detrital pyrite and uraninite become much rarer or disappear from appropriate terrestrial sedimentary settings.

The change was recognised decades before modern isotope geochemistry made the GOE famous.

It is powerful because a river integrates conditions over a landscape.

A pyrite grain eroded from bedrock must pass through weathering, transport and deposition without being destroyed.

That tests more than a tiny local oxygen oasis.

It tests the oxidative character of the exposed continental environment.

Still, the proxy needs care.

A mineral can survive briefly even in oxygenated water if transport is rapid.

Grains can be protected inside larger rock fragments.

Sedimentary recycling can move old material.

Local acidity, temperature and groundwater chemistry matter.

And the absence of pyrite in one deposit could reflect source rock rather than atmosphere.

This is why geologists do not date oxygenation from one missing grain.

They compare many sedimentary successions and multiple proxies.

The broad pattern agrees with sulfur isotopes.

Reduced detrital minerals characteristic of an anoxic surface become difficult to preserve as atmospheric oxygen rises.

At the same time, oxidative weathering creates new dissolved products.

Sulfate from pyrite.

Soluble uranium species.

Ferric iron.

Redox-sensitive trace metals.

Rivers begin carrying a chemically different load to the ocean.

The continents have become reactors in the oxygen cycle.

This creates a feedback.

Oxidative weathering consumes oxygen.

Every reduced mineral exposed at the surface represents a sink.

But the products of weathering can also feed biological and sedimentary processes.

Sulfate enters the ocean and supports sulfate-reducing microbes.

Phosphorus released from rock can stimulate productivity.

Uranium and molybdenum become more mobile and begin serving as geochemical tracers of ocean redox.

A rise in oxygen therefore produces both evidence of itself and new sinks that resist further rise.

The transition is buffered by the planet it transforms.

By around 2.31 billion years ago, another terrestrial signal becomes much easier to recognise.

Sediments begin carrying ferric iron coatings formed during early weathering and deposition.

The rocks turn red.

But even here, colour alone is not enough.

A red rock can become red long after burial.

To use red beds as atmospheric evidence, geologists have to show when the iron oxidised.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Detrital pyrite and uraninite in Archean/earliest Paleoproterozoic terrestrial sediments are classic evidence for very low atmospheric O₂; their disappearance accompanies oxidative surface conditions.
- Proxy use requires source, transport, recycling and diagenetic context.
- Oxidative weathering mobilises sulfate, uranium and other redox-sensitive elements and creates both O₂ sinks and new ocean inputs.

**Decision:** disappearance of reduced detrital minerals is used as a landscape-scale atmospheric proxy, not as an exact single-grain oxygen threshold.
