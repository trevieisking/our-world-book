# CHAPTER SIX — THE OXYGEN REVOLUTION
## Page 193 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### OXYGEN IS A POISON

We breathe oxygen.

That makes it easy to forget how dangerous it is.

Molecular oxygen, O₂, is relatively stable.

Partially reduce it and more reactive molecules appear.

Superoxide.

Hydrogen peroxide.

Hydroxyl radicals.

These **reactive oxygen species**, or ROS, can attack DNA.

They damage proteins.

They oxidise lipids.

They disrupt metal-containing enzymes.

A cell evolved in an anoxic world is not automatically equipped to survive them.

Before persistent atmospheric oxygen, microbes still encountered small amounts of ROS.

Ultraviolet chemistry can generate oxidants.

Mineral surfaces can produce reactive species.

Oxygen oases expose nearby organisms locally.

So some antioxidant chemistry may predate the GOE.

But as oxygen becomes persistent across shallow water and land, exposure changes from occasional to environmental.

A new problem becomes unavoidable.

Cells need defence.

Modern organisms use several strategies.

**Superoxide dismutase** converts superoxide into hydrogen peroxide and oxygen.

Catalases and peroxidases remove hydrogen peroxide.

Small antioxidant molecules absorb oxidative damage.

DNA repair systems correct lesions.

Metal-binding proteins keep iron and copper from catalysing destructive radical chemistry in the wrong place.

Membranes and enzymes evolve structures that tolerate oxidative conditions.

The evolutionary histories of these systems are complicated.

Genes move horizontally between microbes.

Related enzyme families can perform more than one function.

Some antioxidant proteins may originally have evolved for chemistry unrelated to atmospheric oxygen.

So we cannot point to one enzyme and declare the date life “learned to survive O₂.”

What the GOE changes is selection pressure.

An organism able to detoxify oxygen can occupy environments that become hostile to strict anaerobes.

An organism unable to do so must retreat.

Into sediment.

Into deeper water.

Into microbial mats below the oxic layer.

Into hydrothermal pores.

Into animal guts billions of years later.

Anaerobic life does not vanish.

It loses some territory.

This can create ecological turnover without leaving a conventional mass-extinction fossil record.

Most microorganisms do not leave body fossils.

A lineage can disappear from an oxygenated habitat while closely related anaerobic lineages survive elsewhere.

The geological record therefore registers the crisis mainly through chemistry rather than skeletons.

New oxidised minerals.

Changing sulfur and iron cycles.

Different organic-matter preservation.

New nutrient regimes.

The toxicity of oxygen may also have accelerated biochemical innovation.

Repair systems become valuable.

Metal homeostasis becomes important.

Enzymes that safely control oxygen chemistry are favoured.

And once cells can prevent uncontrolled oxidation, they can begin doing something more ambitious.

They can **use oxygen deliberately**.

Oxygen is dangerous because it is such a powerful electron acceptor.

That same property makes it extraordinarily useful for respiration.

The poison can become fuel.

Or more precisely:

it can become the endpoint that lets cells extract much more energy from their fuel.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Reactive oxygen species include superoxide, hydrogen peroxide and hydroxyl radicals and can damage nucleic acids, proteins, lipids and metal centres.
- Antioxidant systems include superoxide dismutases, catalases/peroxidases, small-molecule antioxidants, repair and metal-homeostasis systems.
- Specific evolutionary dates for antioxidant machinery are difficult to establish because of deep divergence, multifunctionality and horizontal gene transfer.

**Decision:** oxygen toxicity is treated as a genuine ecological/evolutionary pressure, while no single antioxidant enzyme is used as a clock for the GOE.
