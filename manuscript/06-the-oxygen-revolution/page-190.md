# CHAPTER SIX — THE OXYGEN REVOLUTION
## Page 190 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### OXYGEN REARRANGES THE PERIODIC TABLE

Life needs more than carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus and sulfur.

Enzymes depend on metals.

Iron carries electrons.

Nickel helps some ancient anaerobic metabolisms.

Molybdenum sits in the most familiar nitrogenase.

Copper becomes central to many oxygen-dependent enzymes.

Manganese sits at the heart of water-splitting photosynthesis.

Zinc stabilises proteins.

Cobalt sits inside vitamin B₁₂.

The availability of these elements depends strongly on redox chemistry.

Change the oxygen level and the biological periodic table changes with it.

Consider iron.

Under anoxic conditions, Fe²⁺ can remain dissolved.

Oxidation removes it rapidly as ferric minerals.

An organism living after the GOE may therefore find **less dissolved iron** in oxygenated surface water even though iron is abundant in the crust.

Now consider molybdenum.

Oxidative weathering can mobilise molybdenum from continental rocks and deliver it to the ocean.

That sounds beneficial for organisms using molybdenum enzymes.

But in sulfidic water, molybdenum is efficiently converted into forms that are scavenged and buried.

An ocean with widespread euxinia can therefore remove molybdenum again.

Oxygen increases one supply pathway while sulfide opens one removal pathway.

Phosphorus behaves differently.

Iron oxides can adsorb phosphate and bury it.

Under some reducing conditions, phosphorus can be released from sediments.

Under ferruginous conditions, it can also be trapped efficiently.

The result is a nutrient cycle with strong redox feedbacks.

This is why nutrient limitation appears repeatedly in oxygenation models.

The organisms producing oxygen depend on nutrients whose availability is partly controlled by oxygen.

A new atmosphere can therefore throttle its own biological source.

The 2025 and 2026 phosphorus studies of the GOE make this feedback especially concrete.

Marine phosphate availability varied across glacial, redox and carbon-cycle transitions.

Periods of greater phosphorus availability are associated with models of higher productivity, greater burial of reduced products and stronger oxygenation.

But the feedback can reverse.

Enhanced mineral burial can remove phosphorus and weaken productivity.

Sulfate availability changes organic-matter remineralisation.

Redox stratification changes where nutrients are recycled.

No simple “more oxygen means more nutrients” rule works.

Trace metals create similar ecological filters.

Anaerobic metabolisms evolved in an iron- and nickel-rich world.

As surface waters oxidise, some of those elements become less available.

At the same time, copper and other redox-sensitive elements become more accessible in oxidised forms.

This may help explain why the metal content of enzymes differs among ancient anaerobic pathways and later oxygen-utilising pathways.

But biochemical patterns cannot be read directly as a timestamp.

Modern organisms have evolved, exchanged genes and adapted to changing environments.

What the geology establishes is the changing chemical opportunity.

Oxygen reorganises solubility.

Sulfide reorganises it again.

Mineral precipitation locks elements away.

Weathering releases others.

Biological innovation occurs inside those moving constraints.

The GOE therefore changes evolution even for organisms that never use oxygen directly.

A methanogen can be affected because nickel availability changes.

A nitrogen fixer can be affected because molybdenum and iron inventories change.

A phototroph can be affected because phosphorus delivery changes.

The atmosphere has become part of every cell's nutrient economy.

That is one of the quietest revolutions in Earth history.

Oxygen does not merely add a gas.

It redistributes the elements from which metabolism is built.

And once oxidation expands across continents and shallow oceans, it also creates minerals that could not previously exist in abundance.

The planet's mineral diversity begins to grow.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Fe, Mo, P, Ni, Cu and other nutrient/trace-metal availabilities are strongly redox-dependent.
- Oxidative weathering can increase Mo and sulfate delivery, while euxinia efficiently removes Mo.
- Ferric minerals can scavenge phosphate; Precambrian P recycling differs from modern oxygenated oceans.
- 2025–2026 GOE studies support strong coupling among phosphorus availability, productivity, redox and atmospheric O₂.

**Decision:** trace-metal/nutrient effects are presented as redox-controlled ecological constraints, not simple monotonic enrichments or depletions.
