# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 122 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### A SEQUENCE CAN BECOME A TOOL

RNA makes the origin problem interesting because information and function can occupy the same molecule.

Consider a sequence written as a line of bases.

A, U, G and C.

On paper it looks like a code.

In water, it is also a physical object.

Parts of the chain attract complementary parts of the same chain.

The molecule folds.

A different sequence can fold differently.

That creates a bridge:

**sequence -> shape -> chemical behaviour.**

This bridge allows selection to act on chemistry.

Suppose one RNA sequence folds into a shape that binds a useful molecule.

Another folds into a pocket that accelerates a reaction.

Another helps join two RNA fragments.

If those functions increase the survival or copying of the sequences that encode them, function becomes heritable.

That is exactly the kind of connection an early evolutionary system needs.

Natural ribozymes perform several kinds of chemistry.

Some RNA molecules cut themselves.

Some splice RNA strands.

Ribosomal RNA helps catalyse peptide-bond formation.

Laboratory selection has expanded the catalogue much further.

Researchers have evolved RNAs that ligate molecules, phosphorylate substrates and polymerise RNA.

These experiments reveal what the chemistry **can** do.

They do not tell us that every laboratory ribozyme once existed on early Earth.

That distinction is essential.

A modern experiment can begin with an enormous library of random RNA sequences.

Researchers then retain the molecules that show a tiny amount of desired activity, copy them with modern enzymes, introduce variation and repeat the selection.

After many rounds, a powerful ribozyme can emerge.

This process is called **in vitro evolution** or directed evolution.

It uses Darwinian logic to explore RNA sequence space.

But the experimenter controls the environment and often supplies steps that a prebiotic system would have needed to perform for itself.

So laboratory-evolved ribozymes are demonstrations of chemical capacity, not fossils.

Still, they answer a profound objection.

RNA does not need proteins in order to possess catalytic function.

Folded RNA can be an enzyme-like molecule in its own right.

The challenge is probability and pathway.

How long does an RNA need to be before a useful catalytic fold becomes accessible?

How often do useful functions occur among random sequences?

Could shorter fragments cooperate?

Could fragments assemble into larger catalysts?

Could primitive replicators improve gradually rather than waiting for one perfect RNA polymerase to appear?

Recent experiments are beginning to shrink the apparent gap.

Self-reproducing ribozymes assembled from shorter RNA fragments have been demonstrated.

RNA polymerase ribozymes have become progressively more capable.

In 2026, researchers reported a general RNA polymerase activity in an RNA only 45 nucleotides long — far smaller than the earlier polymerase ribozymes that had seemed dauntingly complex.

We will return to that molecule shortly.

For now, the important lesson is simpler.

The RNA world does not require one sequence to appear fully formed with every property of a cell.

Evolution can only begin with what can be copied.

Once copying, variation and selectable function become coupled, chemistry acquires a search process.

A landscape that was once explored only by random reactions can begin to be explored by inheritance.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Natural and laboratory ribozymes establish a broad catalytic repertoire for RNA.
- In vitro evolution uses modern experimental selection/amplification and must not be conflated with a prebiotic pathway.
- Nghe (2025), *FEBS Letters*, discusses stepwise transitions from autocatalytic/self-reproducing RNA systems toward template-based replication.
- Gianni et al. (2026), *Science*, QT45: a 45-nt RNA polymerase ribozyme discovered from random-sequence pools.

**Decision:** ribozyme experiments establish functional possibility and evolvability of RNA sequence space; they are not treated as direct reconstructions of early Earth.
