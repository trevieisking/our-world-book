# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 134 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### DIVISION WITHOUT A DIVISION MACHINE

Modern cell division is a masterpiece of control.

Chromosomes are copied.

Molecular machinery positions them.

Protein structures constrict membranes.

Daughter cells receive organised collections of genes and cellular components.

The first compartments could not begin there.

Fortunately, membranes obey geometry before they obey biology.

In 2009, Ting Zhu and Jack Szostak demonstrated a remarkably simple growth-and-division pathway in fatty-acid model protocells.

Large vesicles were fed additional fatty-acid micelles.

Membrane surface area increased faster than internal volume.

A sphere could no longer accommodate the excess membrane efficiently.

The vesicle elongated into thin, thread-like shapes.

Those threads were fragile.

Expose them to modest shear forces — the kind of physical stress produced by fluid motion — and they broke into smaller daughter vesicles.

The internal contents did not simply spill out.

Encapsulated RNA was redistributed among the daughter compartments.

The process could repeat through multiple cycles.

No protein constriction ring was required.

No cytoskeleton.

No motor.

No chromosome-segregation machinery.

The environment supplied the mechanical force.

This does not mean waves literally divided the first cells in exactly this way.

It demonstrates something more useful:

**growth can create the physical instability that makes division easier.**

Reproduction does not have to begin with a separate division program.

The same process that expands a membrane can push the compartment toward a shape that is easier to split.

Environmental cycles can supply other missing functions.

Freeze–thaw experiments published in 2023 showed that periodic freezing and melting can transiently disrupt lipid vesicles, allow exchange of RNA between them and support propagation of self-replicating catalytic RNAs across successive vesicle populations.

The experiments used laboratory-prepared giant phospholipid vesicles and ribozymes.

The freezing conditions were also more extreme and controlled than most plausible natural cycles.

So they are not a literal reconstruction of a Hadean pond.

But they reveal another important principle.

Early protocells did not need to internalise every function immediately.

Temperature cycles can mix contents.

Shear can divide elongated membranes.

Osmotic pressure can drive growth.

Mineral surfaces can accelerate assembly.

The environment can temporarily act as machinery.

There is, however, a cost.

Primitive division is messy.

RNA molecules are distributed statistically.

A daughter can inherit many copies.

Another can inherit few.

A rare catalyst can be lost completely.

Two molecules that need each other may end up in different compartments.

Very small protocells face especially severe sampling problems.

This creates selection for better inheritance.

A compartment that consistently retains useful molecules has an advantage over one that loses them.

Mechanisms that link genome replication to membrane growth become valuable.

Processes that increase copy number before division reduce the chance that a useful sequence disappears.

Later, active segregation systems can evolve.

What modern cells do with elaborate molecular machinery may therefore have begun as a physical problem gradually brought under genetic control.

That transition is important.

A membrane can divide by accident.

A lineage needs division that preserves what made the parent successful.

The beginning of cellular heredity lies somewhere between those two states.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Zhu & Szostak (2009), *JACS*: fatty-acid vesicle growth produces thread-like morphologies that divide under modest shear while retaining and distributing encapsulated RNA; repeated growth–division cycles were demonstrated.
- Salibi et al. (2023), *Nature Communications*: freeze–thaw cycling enabled RNA exchange, ribozyme assembly and persistence of a self-replicating RNA across vesicle populations under serial dilution.
- The 2023 system used experimentally prepared phospholipid GUVs, directed ribozymes and laboratory freeze–thaw conditions; it is treated as a physical model, not a direct historical replay.

**Decision:** primitive membrane division can be driven by physics, but reliable inheritance and coordinated genome–membrane reproduction remain separate unsolved steps.
