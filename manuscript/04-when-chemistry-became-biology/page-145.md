# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 145 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### THERE MAY HAVE BEEN NO SINGLE ORIGIN FLASK

Origin-of-life experiments are usually performed in containers.

A flask.

A tube.

A reaction chamber.

A freezer.

A pressure vessel.

This is necessary for control.

It can also distort the question.

Early Earth was not one container.

It was a planet covered in connected environments that differed in temperature, acidity, salinity, mineralogy, light, water depth and energy supply.

Some origin chemistry works best when water is scarce.

Other reactions require water.

Some need ultraviolet light.

Others are destroyed by it.

Some proceed in alkaline solution.

Others require acidic conditions.

Some benefit from freezing.

Others need heat.

Some membranes tolerate low salt but fail in concentrated seawater.

Some carbon-reduction reactions benefit from metal-rich hydrothermal settings.

These requirements can look contradictory if we insist that every step happened in one place under one unchanging set of conditions.

They become less contradictory if chemistry moves.

Rain falls.

Pools fill.

Pools evaporate.

Groundwater circulates.

Hydrothermal fluids rise.

Rivers carry dissolved compounds.

Waves wash material across mineral surfaces.

Aerosols move through the atmosphere.

Ice freezes and melts.

Impacts redistribute rock and organics.

Particles settle.

Molecules adsorb to minerals and later desorb.

The early Earth continuously transported matter between chemical environments.

This does **not** prove that life's origin required a tour of multiple locations.

A multi-environment pathway can easily become storytelling.

If reaction A works in a hot spring, reaction B works in sea ice and reaction C works in a hydrothermal vent, we cannot simply connect them with arrows and declare the problem solved.

The products must survive transport.

The environments must coexist in the relevant period.

Concentrations must remain useful.

Transport times must be shorter than degradation times.

Later chemistry must tolerate impurities carried from earlier chemistry.

And there must be a realistic physical mechanism linking the stages.

But the opposite assumption is equally unjustified.

There is no scientific rule requiring abiogenesis to occur in one microscopic birthplace.

Reviews of prebiotic chemistry have repeatedly noted that successful laboratory pathways draw on conditions that can be difficult to reconcile in one static setting.

That observation has encouraged models involving environmental cycling, flow chemistry and transport between chemically distinct zones.

A shoreline already contains several environments within metres.

A wet sediment surface can dry.

Rain can rehydrate it.

Sunlight can drive photochemistry.

Minerals can concentrate solutes.

Pore water beneath the surface can protect products from radiation.

A volcanic hydrothermal field can connect hot fluid, cooler pools, steam, fresh water, rock surfaces and atmospheric chemistry.

Even a vent contains gradients rather than one condition.

The useful unit may therefore not be a site.

It may be a **sequence of conditions**.

That changes how we evaluate origin hypotheses.

Instead of asking:

“Which place contains every ingredient for life?”

we can ask:

**“What process does this environment perform, and can its products physically reach the next process?”**

That is a harder question.

It is also more realistic.

Over the next few pages, we will treat candidate environments as natural chemical machines.

Not birthplaces competing for a trophy.

Reactors performing different jobs.

The first is one of the simplest mechanisms on a rocky planet with exposed water:

let the water disappear.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Kitadai & Maruyama (2018), review of prebiotic building-block synthesis, emphasises that experimentally productive chemistries can require diverse and sometimes mutually incompatible conditions and discusses transport among dynamic environments.
- 2026 early-Earth redox/prebiotic reviews likewise treat transport of organics and multiple local environments as relevant to integrated origin scenarios.
- No multi-environment historical route has been demonstrated.

**Decision:** multiple environments are presented as a physically plausible systems framework, not as permission to combine unrelated experiments without transport, survival and compatibility constraints.
