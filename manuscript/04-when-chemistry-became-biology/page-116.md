# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 116 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### HOW DO YOU RECOGNISE LIFE AFTER BILLIONS OF YEARS?

If the first life was microscopic and chemically simple, it left no bones.

No shells.

No tracks large enough to see.

Its evidence had to survive inside rocks for more than three billion years.

That makes the search for earliest life unusually difficult.

A candidate biosignature must pass two separate tests.

The first is **biogenicity**:

was the feature actually produced by life?

The second is **syngeneity**:

is the feature genuinely as old as the rock or sediment in which it is found?

Both matter.

A carbon-rich structure inside an ancient rock could be younger material carried in later by fluids.

A microscopic tube could resemble a cell while actually being produced by mineral growth.

A carbon-isotope ratio can look biological even though some non-biological reactions also fractionate carbon.

A layered rock can resemble a microbial stromatolite after deformation.

The older the rock, the more severe these problems become.

Heat can turn organic matter into graphite.

Pressure can stretch and fold original structures.

Fluids can dissolve minerals and deposit new ones.

Recrystallisation can erase textures.

Later fractures can introduce contamination.

A three-billion-year-old rock is not a sealed evidence bag.

This is why the strongest claims do not rest on one attractive shape or one unusual number.

Confidence rises when independent lines of evidence converge.

A candidate microbial structure is stronger if its geometry fits the sedimentary environment, if its carbon is demonstrably ancient, if its chemistry matches biological processing, if it occurs in a population rather than as one isolated curiosity, and if plausible abiotic mechanisms fail to reproduce the whole pattern.

Context matters as much as appearance.

Researchers therefore combine sedimentology, microscopy, spectroscopy, elemental mapping, isotope geochemistry and radiometric dating.

They ask where the carbon sits relative to minerals, veins and deformation fabrics.

They ask whether the host rock really formed at Earth's surface.

They ask whether the putative fossil predates metamorphism.

They compare candidate structures with abiotic **biomorphs** — mineral structures that can imitate life.

This cautious method can feel frustrating.

A spectacular claim may be announced and then weakened years later.

But that is not science failing.

It is the evidence being forced to survive better tests.

The history of early-life research contains several examples where an apparently biological structure was reinterpreted after improved three-dimensional imaging, geological mapping or contamination controls.

It also contains cases where new methods strengthened old evidence.

That is exactly how a reliable ancient record has to be built.

For this book, we will therefore use a confidence hierarchy.

Some sites provide multiple, mutually reinforcing biosignatures and are widely treated as convincing evidence of ancient life.

Older sites may contain important evidence but require larger uncertainty labels because alteration has removed more of the original context.

The question is not:

**What is the oldest thing anyone has called a fossil?**

It is:

**What is the oldest evidence that still looks biological after the strongest available abiotic alternatives have been tested?**

That is the standard we now take into the rocks.

**Decision:** morphology, chemistry or isotopes alone are not treated as sufficient. Earliest-life confidence depends on geological context plus multiple independent biosignatures and explicit testing of abiotic alternatives.
