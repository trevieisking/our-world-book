### THE STRAND-SEPARATION PROBLEM

RNA base pairing is both the solution and the trap.

A template strand guides the formation of its complement because matching bases bind.

The completed strands therefore fit each other extremely well.

They form a duplex.

For heredity, that is useful.

For repeated replication, it is a problem.

A copied strand cannot easily serve as a new template while it remains locked to its partner.

Heat can pull RNA duplexes apart.

But when the solution cools, complementary long strands can find each other and re-form the same duplex before fresh building blocks have time to copy either strand.

This is called **product inhibition**.

Modern cells solve comparable problems with enzymes.

They unwind nucleic acids, control temperatures chemically rather than literally heating the cell, protect single strands and coordinate replication.

Primitive RNA had no such machinery.

Any RNA-world scenario therefore needs a physical route by which templates become accessible again.

Several possibilities have been explored.

Temperature cycles can melt and re-form duplexes.

Changing acidity can weaken some base pairs.

Wet–dry cycles alter concentration and ionic conditions.

Thermal gradients can move molecules through different microenvironments.

Freezing can be surprisingly useful.

Most dissolved material is excluded from growing ice crystals and becomes concentrated in tiny liquid channels between them.

Those concentrated brines can promote reactions that would be slow in a dilute solution.

In 2025, James Attwater and colleagues reported an important experimental advance.

They used an artificial RNA polymerase ribozyme that copies templates using activated RNA **trinucleotides** — three-base substrates rather than individual nucleotides.

The triplets did two jobs.

They served as building blocks.

And by binding to newly separated RNA strands, they slowed those strands from simply snapping back together.

Combined with cycles of pH and freezing and thawing, this allowed both complementary strands of test RNAs to be repeatedly copied.

Some sequences underwent exponential amplification.

The system could also replicate a fragment of the polymerase ribozyme itself.

Random-sequence pools changed over repeated cycles as RNAs that were better at being replicated became enriched.

That is striking because it couples a physical environmental cycle to the persistence and selection of RNA sequences.

But we have to say precisely what was demonstrated.

The catalyst was an RNA polymerase ribozyme previously evolved in the laboratory.

Activated triplet substrates were supplied.

Defined cycling conditions were imposed.

Some experiments used primers, although the study also demonstrated primer-free synthesis in later open-ended cycling experiments.

The full polymerase did not autonomously arise and reproduce itself as an independent organism.

This was not life forming in a freezer.

It was an experimentally constructed model showing that a problem once regarded as a severe barrier — repeated strand separation and re-copying — has physically plausible solutions.

That changes the weight of the RNA-world argument.

A difficulty is not the same thing as an impossibility.

But solving a difficulty in one engineered system is not the same thing as reconstructing history.

The result gives us a mechanism to take seriously:

**environmental cycles can become part of a replication system.**

The surroundings do not merely supply ingredients.

They can perform functions that later biology internalised as molecular machinery.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Attwater et al. (2025), *Nature Chemistry*: activated trinucleotide substrates inhibit rapid strand reannealing and, with coupled pH/freeze–thaw cycles, enable repeated and exponential RNA replication by an artificial polymerase ribozyme.
- The work includes replication of both strands of defined RNAs, a fragment of the polymerase ribozyme, and open-ended evolution/amplification from sequence pools.
- The polymerase ribozyme and activated substrates were experimentally supplied; this is not a demonstration of spontaneous abiogenesis or a fully autonomous self-replicating organism.

**Decision:** the 2025 work is treated as a major solution to a physical replication barrier, while laboratory scaffolding and pre-supplied activated substrates remain explicit.
