# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 124 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### BUILDING A CHAIN IS NOT COPYING A CHAIN

Suppose an early environment contains nucleotides.

They still have to become polymers.

RNA is held together by phosphodiester bonds linking one nucleotide to the next.

In modern cells, enzymes make those bonds with exquisite control.

Before enzymes, chemistry had to do the work.

Activated nucleotides can join into RNA-like chains without biology under some conditions.

Mineral surfaces can concentrate reactants.

Drying can bring molecules together that would be too dilute in open water.

Repeated wet–dry cycles can drive condensation and then redistribute products.

Freezing can push solutes into concentrated liquid pockets between ice crystals.

Different environments therefore provide physical mechanisms for concentration as well as chemistry.

But random polymerisation is only the first problem.

An evolving genetic system needs **copying**.

RNA offers a natural template.

The bases prefer particular partners:

A with U,

G with C.

If an RNA strand is exposed to activated nucleotides, complementary bases can align along it.

Chemical bonds can then join neighbouring building blocks to produce a complementary strand.

Laboratory experiments have demonstrated versions of this process without protein enzymes.

That is called **non-enzymatic template-directed copying**.

It is one of the most important bridges between prebiotic chemistry and heredity.

It is also imperfect.

Different template sequences copy at different speeds.

Some nucleotide additions are much easier than others.

Incorrect bases can be incorporated.

RNA secondary structure can hide a template region.

Metal ions that accelerate copying can also accelerate RNA degradation.

Activated nucleotides themselves must be supplied with chemical free energy.

And an experiment that starts with a carefully prepared template has already assumed the existence of something that the earliest system still needs to explain.

Researchers have therefore attacked the copying problem piece by piece.

New activation chemistries can regenerate reactive nucleotide intermediates under conditions compatible with copying.

Short activated oligonucleotides can sometimes serve as substrates instead of single nucleotides.

Mixtures and environmental cycles can change which reactions dominate.

Each improvement removes one obstacle.

None should be silently combined with every other successful experiment as though all were already demonstrated in one natural setting.

The distinction matters because origins research often advances through **proofs of principle**.

Experiment A may show that a nucleotide can form.

Experiment B may show that activated nucleotides copy a template.

Experiment C may show that a membrane tolerates a certain mixture.

If the conditions of A destroy the products required by B, or B destroys the membrane required by C, the complete pathway still does not work.

A credible origin scenario eventually has to join the modules.

For RNA, template-directed chemistry gives us something fundamental:

a molecular mechanism by which sequence information can be transferred from one strand to another without a protein enzyme.

That is the beginning of heredity.

But it immediately creates the next problem.

At the end of copying, the original strand and its complement cling together in a stable double helix.

To copy again, they have to separate.

And once separated, they must remain apart long enough to act as templates.

Early replication therefore faced a paradox.

**The same base pairing that makes accurate copying possible can stop repeated copying from continuing.**

## Research / fact-check notes
- Non-enzymatic template-directed RNA copying is experimentally established using chemically activated nucleotide substrates.
- Zhang et al. (2020), *JACS*: potentially prebiotic bridge-forming activation can produce the reactive intermediate used in non-enzymatic RNA copying.
- Zhou et al. (2020), *Angewandte Chemie*: template-directed ligation can copy mixed-sequence RNA, while stable duplex formation creates product inhibition.
- Environmental concentration/cycling mechanisms are candidate solutions, not a uniquely identified origin setting.

**Decision:** polymer formation is separated from sequence copying; one successful reaction is not treated as a complete replication cycle.
