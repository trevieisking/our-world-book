# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 126 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### COPYING MUST BE WRONG — BUT NOT TOO WRONG

Perfect copying would freeze evolution.

Terrible copying would erase heredity.

Life exists between those extremes.

Every copying system has an error rate.

If a sequence is short, it can tolerate a relatively high chance of mistakes at each position and still produce some faithful descendants.

As sequences become longer, errors accumulate.

Eventually the probability of preserving all of the information required for function becomes too small.

This creates an **error threshold**.

The exact threshold depends on the population and on how much of the sequence must be conserved, but the principle is unavoidable:

more heritable information requires more accurate replication.

This is one reason RNA replication is not merely a question of speed.

A polymerase that makes lots of RNA badly may destroy the very functions evolution is trying to preserve.

In 2024, Nikolaos Papastavrou, David Horning and Gerald Joyce demonstrated the point experimentally with an RNA polymerase ribozyme.

Earlier versions of the polymerase copied with insufficient fidelity to preserve a small functional RNA called the hammerhead ribozyme over repeated evolution.

A more accurate polymerase could copy the hammerhead and its complementary strand with enough fidelity for functional variants to persist.

During repeated rounds of replication and selection, new hammerhead variants appeared.

Some increased in fitness under the experimental conditions.

The lower-fidelity system, by contrast, rapidly lost the functional information.

This is Darwinian logic implemented with RNA:

heritable sequence,

variation,

selection,

changing population composition.

It shows that an RNA catalyst can propagate enough information for another catalytic RNA to evolve.

But again, the boundary around the experiment matters.

The polymerase itself had previously been produced through laboratory-directed evolution.

The experimental evolutionary cycle used modern procedures to recover, amplify and regenerate selected molecules.

Reverse transcription and PCR appeared in the workflow.

A researcher decided which catalytic activity counted as success.

The polymerase was not yet autonomously copying a complete evolving system containing its own replication machinery.

The authors themselves identified further improvement in fidelity as necessary before RNA enzymes as complex as the polymerase could undergo fully autonomous Darwinian evolution.

So the experiment should neither be dismissed nor exaggerated.

It does not recreate first life.

It demonstrates a mechanism first life would have needed:

an RNA-based hereditary system can cross from mere copying into genuine selectable evolution when its accuracy becomes high enough.

That creates a feedback loop.

Better copying preserves larger functional RNAs.

Larger functional RNAs can perform more chemistry.

Some of that chemistry can improve copying.

Improved copying allows yet more information to survive.

Evolution can begin ratcheting upward.

This is a possible route from a simple replicator toward a network.

But it raises a formidable chicken-and-egg problem.

The RNA polymerases capable of good copying have historically been large, complex RNA molecules.

How could an early RNA system accurately copy a polymerase before it already possessed a polymerase capable of accurate copying?

Until recently, that gap looked uncomfortably wide.

Then researchers found something much smaller.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Papastavrou, Horning & Joyce (2024), *PNAS*: a higher-fidelity RNA polymerase ribozyme propagated a functional hammerhead ribozyme through reciprocal copying and selection, while a lower-fidelity lineage lost function.
- The experimental workflow involved directed evolution and modern amplification/selection steps; it was not autonomous prebiotic evolution.
- The study explicitly links copying fidelity to maintenance of heritable functional information and notes that greater fidelity is required for autonomous evolution of RNAs as complex as the polymerase.

**Decision:** error-threshold logic is used to explain why replication quality constrains the amount of early heritable information. The 2024 system is proof of RNA-catalysed Darwinian evolution, not recreated abiogenesis.
