# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 121 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### WHY RNA?

Modern cells divide their most important molecular jobs.

DNA stores long-term genetic information.

Proteins perform most of the catalysis that keeps the cell running.

RNA often carries messages, regulates processes and helps connect the two.

That division is extraordinarily effective.

It also creates an origin problem.

DNA is copied by protein enzymes.

Protein enzymes are encoded by nucleic-acid sequences.

And proteins are built by ribosomes using RNA instructions.

If we project that entire system backward, every part seems to require another part that does not yet exist.

RNA offers a possible way out of the circle.

An RNA molecule is a chain of nucleotides whose **sequence can carry information**.

But RNA is not merely a passive message.

A single RNA chain can fold back on itself.

Bases pair.

Loops form.

Stems, pockets and complex three-dimensional surfaces emerge.

The exact folded shape depends partly on the sequence.

And shape can create chemical function.

Some RNAs are catalysts.

They are called **ribozymes**.

This was not always known.

In the early 1980s, experiments on very different biological systems showed that RNA could catalyse reactions without a protein enzyme doing the chemical work.

That discovery transformed origin-of-life research.

One type of molecule could, in principle, do two jobs that modern biology largely separates:

store heritable sequence information,

and perform chemistry.

Modern life still contains a striking reminder of RNA's catalytic power.

The ribosome — the molecular machine that makes proteins — is built from both RNA and proteins.

Yet the heart of the large ribosomal subunit, where peptide bonds are formed, is dominated by ribosomal RNA.

Structural and biochemical work established that the peptidyl-transferase centre is fundamentally an RNA catalyst.

That does not prove that the first life consisted only of RNA.

A surviving molecular feature cannot by itself reconstruct an entire vanished world.

But it gives the RNA-world framework something unusually valuable:

a possible relic inside every living cell.

The hypothesis, in its simplest form, proposes that before DNA became the main genetic archive and before proteins became the dominant catalysts, RNA or an RNA-like system played both roles.

That would reduce the number of things that had to appear together at the start.

It would not reduce them to zero.

RNA still has to come from somewhere.

Nucleotides have to form.

They have to join into chains.

Useful sequences have to arise.

Those chains have to be copied.

Copies have to separate from templates.

The copying has to be accurate enough to preserve useful information.

Energy and raw materials have to be supplied.

And eventually the RNA system has to become linked to compartments, peptides, metabolism and the genetic code.

So RNA does not solve the origin of life.

It changes the problem into a set of experiments.

Can RNA store information?

Yes.

Can RNA catalyse reactions?

Yes.

Can RNA help copy RNA?

Increasingly, yes.

Could a chemically plausible environment make the ingredients and sustain an evolving RNA system without a scientist continually intervening?

That is where the frontier begins.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Catalytic RNA was established experimentally in the early 1980s; ribozymes demonstrate that RNA can perform chemical catalysis.
- Structural and biochemical work establishes the ribosomal peptidyl-transferase centre as fundamentally RNA-catalysed.
- Higgs & Lehman (2015), *Nature Reviews Genetics*, RNA-world logic and molecular cooperation.
- Fine & Pearlman (2023), *RNA*, modern RNA-focused origin-of-life synthesis.
- The survival of catalytic RNA in modern biology supports an ancient central role for RNA but does not establish a pure, historically exclusive RNA-only beginning.

**Decision:** RNA-world evidence is framed as a strong functional and evolutionary argument, not direct geological proof of a specific RNA-only era.
