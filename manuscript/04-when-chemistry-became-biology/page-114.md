# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 114 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### LIFE NEEDS A MEMORY

Replication sounds like the obvious starting point.

Something makes more of itself.

But copying alone is not enough.

A crystal can grow because incoming atoms or molecules fit an existing lattice.

A flame can spread when heat ignites new fuel.

Neither process produces the kind of inherited record that allows biological evolution to accumulate information.

For Darwinian evolution, three things have to exist together.

There must be **multiplication**.

There must be **heredity**, so that descendants preserve features of their predecessors.

And there must be **variation**, so that descendants are not always identical.

Variation by itself is only noise.

The crucial extra step is selection.

If some inherited variants persist or reproduce more effectively than others under particular conditions, those variants become more common.

Repeated over generations, selection can preserve useful changes and build complexity that no single chemical reaction had to produce all at once.

This is one of the deepest differences between ordinary chemistry and biology.

A chemical reaction happens according to the conditions present.

A biological population also carries information about what worked before.

Its molecules embody a history.

Modern life stores much of that history in DNA.

But DNA is almost certainly too sophisticated to have been the first hereditary system.

Copying DNA in modern cells requires an elaborate network of proteins, enzymes and molecular machinery.

Those proteins are themselves produced through information encoded in nucleic acids.

That creates a circular problem if we simply project the modern system backward.

Which came first — the information carrier or the machinery that copies and interprets it?

Origin-of-life research tries to break that circle by looking for simpler systems in which fewer functions were separated.

RNA is important partly because some RNA molecules can store sequence information while others can catalyse chemical reactions.

Other models explore networks of mutually supporting molecules, peptides, surfaces or compartments.

The details remain unresolved.

But any successful model has to confront the same information problem.

A primitive replicator must copy accurately enough for useful information not to disappear immediately.

Yet copying cannot be perfectly rigid.

Without heritable variation, evolution stops.

Too many errors destroy information.

Too few errors prevent exploration.

This balance between fidelity and variation places a physical constraint on early evolving systems.

And replication must eventually do more than preserve one lucky molecular pattern.

A lineage capable only of repeating the same narrow trick under one carefully controlled condition is fragile.

Life as we know it became powerful because evolution could discover new functions when environments changed.

That is why some researchers distinguish simple molecular evolution from **open-ended** or broadly innovative evolution.

No sharp experimental line tells us exactly when the first chemical population became biological.

But heredity gives us a principle we can test:

**life is not merely chemistry that repeats. It is chemistry that can remember, vary and accumulate the consequences of selection.**

Once that becomes possible, the past begins to shape the future.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Joyce / NASA Astrobiology discussions of Darwinian evolution, molecular memory, heredity and variation.
- Adamski et al. (2020), *Nature Reviews Chemistry*, self-replication, error constraints and the transition toward evolvable replicator systems.
- Seelig & Chen (2025), *Nature Chemistry*, complex biochemical systems as products assembled from earlier functions rather than appearing fully formed.
- Modern DNA replication is not treated as the required architecture of first life.

**Decision:** replication is distinguished from Darwinian heredity. A successful origin model must explain how information can persist, vary and become subject to cumulative selection.
