# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 144 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### RNA FIRST OR METABOLISM FIRST?

Origin-of-life theories are often presented like competing teams.

RNA first.

Metabolism first.

Membranes first.

Vents.

Ponds.

Ice.

Mineral surfaces.

Choose one.

The chemistry does not have to respect those categories.

An RNA molecule cannot replicate indefinitely without activated building blocks and free energy.

A metabolic network cannot undergo open-ended Darwinian evolution unless useful changes can somehow be inherited.

A membrane that encloses nothing useful is only a vesicle.

A catalyst that benefits every molecule equally can be exploited by parasites.

Each subsystem solves a problem while creating another dependency.

That is why the experimental story in this chapter has increasingly become a story of **coupling**.

RNA joins information to catalysis.

A protocell joins local chemistry to an individual compartment.

Osmotic and catalytic effects join internal contents to membrane growth.

Minerals join geochemical energy to organic reactions.

Hydrogen joins rock–water chemistry to carbon reduction.

Environmental cycles can join strand separation, concentration or division to physical changes outside the protocell.

The first evolving system may therefore have been assembled from interactions between components that were individually imperfect.

There is no requirement that one subsystem reached modern sophistication before the others appeared.

This is the appeal of hybrid models.

In one family of scenarios, geochemical carbon fixation produces small organic compounds.

Those compounds contribute to membranes and catalytic networks.

Simple compartments concentrate them.

RNA or RNA-like polymers begin copying within those environments.

Catalytic RNAs and short peptides redirect chemical flux.

Membrane growth makes some molecular combinations reproduce more successfully than others.

Selection gradually transfers functions from uncontrolled geology to molecules that are retained and inherited inside protocells.

This is plausible as a **framework**.

It is not a recovered chronology.

A 2023 review by Stuart Harrison and colleagues explicitly treated life's origin as a continuum from geochemistry to genes rather than a single leap.

Their model emphasises far-from-equilibrium environments, CO₂ and H₂ chemistry, protocell growth, protometabolic networks and the later emergence of genetic information.

Other researchers place informational polymers earlier.

Surface and wet–dry models emphasise polymerisation and concentration rather than hydrothermal gradients.

RNA–peptide models ask whether nucleic acids and small peptides coevolved before either became dominant.

The disagreements are real.

They concern environments, sequence, catalysts, energy coupling and which bottlenecks are hardest.

But the strongest modern experiments also reveal common ground.

No viable origin model can permanently avoid:

matter,

energy,

catalysis,

concentration,

a boundary or equivalent localisation,

heritable information,

variation,

and selection.

The order can differ.

The mechanism can differ.

Some functions can initially be supplied by geology and internalised later.

Some may emerge together.

Some may have been reinvented.

And more than one environment may have contributed.

That last possibility matters.

Laboratory origin-of-life chemistry often asks one vessel to do everything.

Early Earth had oceans, shorelines, volcanic fields, hydrothermal pores, impact systems, ice, rain, evaporation, minerals and transport between them.

A compound formed in one environment could move to another.

A cycle of wetting and drying could alternate with periods of hydration.

A mineral-bound polymer could later become encapsulated.

There may never have been one “origin flask.”

So before this chapter ends, we have one final question to answer.

Not which molecule won.

Not which theory has the best name.

**Where could the required pieces actually have met?**

The last pages of this chapter will compare the major candidate environments without crowning a birthplace that the evidence cannot identify.

Then we will put the pieces together and state exactly how far science has — and has not — crossed the gap from chemistry to biology.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Harrison et al. (2023), *Annual Review of Ecology, Evolution, and Systematics*: presents origin as a continuum linking geochemical energy, carbon fixation, protocells, metabolic heredity and genetic information.
- RNA-world, metabolism-first, protocell, hydrothermal and surface-cycling models solve overlapping rather than completely independent problems.
- Current evidence does not establish one unique sequence or one unique birthplace for abiogenesis.

**Decision:** the book rejects a forced RNA-first-versus-metabolism-first binary. Hybrid coupling is treated as a research framework, not a proven historical narrative.

**Transition:** pages 145–152 will compare candidate environments, examine whether origin chemistry could be distributed across multiple settings, and close Chapter 4 with the strongest current evidence boundary.
