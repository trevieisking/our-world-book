# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 132 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### RNA INSIDE A FRAGILE BUBBLE

Putting RNA inside a membrane sounds like joining two solved problems.

For years, the chemistry refused to cooperate.

RNA copying often benefits from magnesium ions.

Magnesium helps organise negatively charged phosphate groups and can accelerate the chemistry of nucleotide addition.

But high concentrations of free magnesium can damage fatty-acid membranes.

The ion screens the repulsion between negatively charged fatty-acid headgroups.

Membranes can aggregate.

Fatty acids can precipitate.

The compartment falls apart.

Magnesium creates another problem too.

It can accelerate cleavage of RNA.

So an ingredient useful for copying can simultaneously destroy both the genome and its container.

This is exactly the kind of systems problem that appears repeatedly in origin-of-life research.

A reaction cannot be judged only by asking whether it works in isolation.

It has to be compatible with everything surrounding it.

In 2013, Katarzyna Adamala and Jack Szostak found a chemical workaround.

They added **citrate**.

Citrate binds, or chelates, magnesium ions.

Enough catalytically useful magnesium remained available for non-enzymatic RNA copying, while the concentration of destructive free magnesium was reduced.

Fatty-acid vesicles stayed intact.

Single-stranded RNA was also protected against magnesium-catalysed degradation.

Template-directed RNA synthesis could proceed inside the model protocells.

This did not prove that citrate played this role on early Earth.

The importance of the experiment is the principle.

Chemical incompatibilities can sometimes be relieved by additional small molecules in the environment.

The system may have more solutions than a simplified two-component experiment suggests.

Membranes also have to acquire their genetic cargo.

Some encapsulation can happen by chance when vesicles assemble around material already present in solution.

Mineral surfaces provide another route.

In the 2003 montmorillonite experiments, RNA adsorbed to clay became trapped when fatty-acid membranes formed around the mineral particles.

A surface that helps concentrate or polymerise nucleic acids could therefore, under some conditions, help deliver those molecules into a compartment.

This creates an attractive handoff:

chemistry on a surface,

followed by enclosure in a mobile vesicle.

It remains a hypothesis about early Earth.

But it can be tested physically.

More recent work shows that different membrane chemistries can also tolerate catalytic RNA.

The cysteine-derived diacyl-lipid vesicles reported in 2025 supported functional ribozyme activity.

Other protocell models use mixtures of fatty acids, alcohols or glycerol esters to improve robustness.

There may never have been one universal primitive membrane recipe.

The most important lesson is that the membrane and its contents cannot be treated as independent inventions.

A compartment changes pH.

It changes local concentration.

It changes ion availability.

Its surface can bind molecules.

Internal molecules can change osmotic pressure and membrane growth.

A peptide or catalyst inside can alter which lipids the membrane acquires.

Once these feedbacks appear, the “container” becomes part of the chemistry it contains.

That is the beginning of something more interesting than encapsulation.

The inside can begin changing the outside.

And when internal chemistry changes how fast its own compartment grows, heredity gains something it desperately needs:

**a route from molecular function to reproductive success.**

## Research / fact-check notes
- Adamala & Szostak (2013), *Science*: citrate protects fatty-acid membranes from Mg²⁺ disruption while allowing non-enzymatic RNA copying and reducing RNA degradation.
- Hanczyc, Fujikawa & Szostak (2003), *Science*: RNA adsorbed to montmorillonite can become encapsulated during mineral-assisted vesicle assembly.
- Cho et al. (2025), *Nature Chemistry*: cysteine-derived diacyl-lipid protocells support functional ribozyme activity.
- The historical role of citrate, clay or any particular membrane composition is not established.

**Decision:** Mg²⁺–membrane incompatibility is presented as a real systems-level constraint with demonstrated chemical workarounds, not as evidence that one specific solution occurred historically.
