# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 117 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### A SAFE PLACE TO SAY: LIFE WAS HERE

To find the origin of life, we first need a date by which life definitely existed.

The geological record cannot usually tell us the moment life began.

It can give us anchors.

One of the strongest early anchors comes from the roughly **3.48-billion-year-old Dresser Formation** in the Pilbara Craton of Western Australia.

These rocks are valuable not because they contain one dramatic object labelled “the first fossil,” but because several kinds of evidence occur together in a well-studied geological setting.

The Dresser Formation preserves stromatolites — layered structures built as microbial communities interact with sediment and mineral precipitation.

It also preserves microbial mat textures and other sedimentary structures consistent with biological activity.

Carbon and sulfur isotope patterns provide additional evidence of ancient biological processing.

Hydrothermal and hot-spring deposits preserve further textures interpreted as microbial.

The rocks have been mapped in detail, allowing the structures to be evaluated in their original sedimentary and volcanic context rather than as isolated shapes.

This convergence is why the Dresser record is commonly described as among the **earliest convincing evidence for life on Earth**.

That wording matters.

A stromatolite-like shape alone would be weaker.

An isotope measurement alone would be weaker.

Organic carbon alone would be weaker.

Together, in rocks whose setting and age are independently constrained, the argument becomes much harder to reproduce with one abiotic explanation.

The picture is also ecologically interesting.

By 3.48 billion years ago, life was not necessarily confined to one narrow chemical niche.

The Dresser environment included shallow-water and hydrothermal settings, and evidence has been interpreted as showing microbial mats associated with volcanic and hydrothermal landscapes.

Nearby ancient terrains add to the impression that life was already ecologically established.

In South Africa's Barberton Greenstone Belt, about 3.42-billion-year-old hydrothermal-vein rocks contain putative filamentous microfossils interpreted as subsurface microorganisms associated with methane cycling.

Those filaments remain an interpretation rather than a time machine into the first cell.

But they fit a broader pattern:

by the middle Palaeoarchaean, microorganisms occupied more than one environment.

This immediately changes the origin question.

If life was already widespread, metabolically active and building communities by about 3.5 billion years ago, then its origin must be earlier.

How much earlier?

The Dresser rocks cannot tell us.

They preserve a biosphere that already had history.

They do not preserve the moment chemistry first crossed into biology.

That is a distinction we will keep returning to.

**A fossil gives a latest possible date for an origin that must already have happened.**

It is evidence of existence.

It is not a birth certificate.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Djokic et al. (2017), *Nature Communications*: the ca. 3.48 Ga Dresser Formation preserves stromatolites and other microbial biosignatures in a volcanic-hydrothermal setting.
- Recent review of the earliest biosphere (2025, *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*): oldest known stromatolites and phototrophic mat evidence occur in the 3.48 Ga Dresser Formation.
- Cavalazzi et al. (2021), *Science Advances*: ~3.42 Ga putative filamentous microfossils in a Barberton subseafloor hydrothermal-vein system.
- No claim is made that Dresser preserves the first life or the site where life originated.

**Decision:** ~3.48 Ga is used as a conservative anchor for convincing early-life evidence, not as the date of abiogenesis.
