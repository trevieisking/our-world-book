# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 129 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### LIFE NEEDS AN INSIDE

An RNA molecule can carry information.

A ribozyme can perform chemistry.

A population of replicators can evolve.

But if every useful product diffuses into the same surrounding water, a new problem appears.

Imagine one RNA that helps make a molecule that improves replication.

The useful molecule spreads away.

Nearby RNAs that did nothing to produce it can use it too.

The catalyst pays the chemical cost.

The freeloaders receive the benefit.

In evolutionary language, useful chemistry has become a **public good**.

Without some way of keeping benefit close to the sequence that produced it, selection can favour parasites rather than cooperation.

Compartments change the rules.

Put a small number of molecules inside a boundary and the products of their reactions remain locally associated with them.

If one compartment contains a replicator that improves its own internal chemistry, that compartment can grow, persist or reproduce more successfully than neighbouring compartments.

Now selection can act at a new level.

Not just on molecules.

On **packages of molecules**.

This is one of the deepest consequences of cellular organisation.

A boundary creates an individual.

The boundary does not have to be a modern cell membrane.

Mineral pores can confine chemistry.

Droplets formed by liquid–liquid phase separation can concentrate molecules.

Ice can create microscopic liquid channels.

Rock surfaces can keep reacting species close together.

And amphiphilic molecules — compounds with a water-attracting region and a water-repelling region — can spontaneously assemble into closed membrane vesicles.

Different origin scenarios give different roles to these compartments.

There is no evidence that the first biologically important compartment had to be one particular type.

But a membrane has an exceptional advantage.

It can move.

It can grow.

It can divide.

And it can carry its contents with it.

That turns local chemistry into something capable of lineage.

Compartmentalisation also helps control parasites.

A parasitic RNA that copies quickly while contributing nothing useful can overrun an unstructured solution because it has access to every catalyst around it.

Divide the population among many small compartments and the parasite's access becomes local.

Some compartments may be ruined.

Others may contain more cooperative combinations.

If successful compartments reproduce more often, selection between compartments can preserve molecular cooperation that would collapse in bulk solution.

This does not magically solve the origin of individuality.

Contents can still be lost during division.

Useful molecules may end up in different daughter compartments.

A compartment that grows fastest chemically may not contain the best replicator.

Membranes can block nutrients as well as parasites.

And a perfectly sealed container is useless if nothing can enter or leave.

The boundary therefore has to perform a balancing act.

Keep large, valuable molecules in.

Allow small nutrients and waste products to move.

Grow when new boundary material arrives.

Divide without destroying everything inside.

And somehow connect what happens **inside** to the reproductive success of the whole compartment.

Modern cells accomplish all of this with proteins, pumps, channels, cytoskeletons, genomes and metabolic networks.

The first compartments had none of those.

They needed physics to do most of the work.

That makes the first membrane question surprisingly simple:

**Can ordinary molecules assemble themselves into a boundary before life exists?**

Yes.

Under the right conditions, they can.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Chen, Salehi-Ashtiani & Szostak (2005), *JACS*: compartmentalisation localises RNA catalysis and provides a route toward genotype–phenotype coupling.
- Salibi et al. (2023), *Nature Communications*: compartmentalisation can restrict replicators and environmental cycling can propagate catalytic RNAs between vesicle populations.
- Protocell/RNA reviews through 2024 emphasise suppression of parasitic replicators and localisation of cooperative chemistry as major evolutionary consequences of compartments.
- Membrane vesicles are one important class of prebiotic compartment; mineral pores, coacervates, ice channels and surface localisation remain alternative or complementary possibilities.

**Decision:** compartmentalisation is presented as a functional requirement for stable molecular cooperation and selection, not as proof that lipid vesicles were necessarily the first compartment used by life.
