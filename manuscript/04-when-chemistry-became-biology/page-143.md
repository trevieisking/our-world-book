# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 143 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### A CYCLE IS HARDER THAN A REACTION

Draw a circle on paper.

A becomes B.

B becomes C.

C becomes A.

It looks self-sustaining.

Chemistry is less forgiving.

For a true metabolic cycle to continue, every required transformation has to occur fast enough.

The intermediate produced by one reaction must survive long enough to enter the next.

Competing reactions must not drain too much material away.

Catalysts must work in compatible conditions.

The cycle must regenerate the compound with which it began.

And if the network is meant to grow rather than merely turn in place, it has to make more useful material than is lost.

This is why demonstrating one reaction — or even several adjacent reactions — is not equivalent to demonstrating a cycle.

Leslie Orgel made this criticism forcefully in 2008.

He argued that proposals for complex pre-genetic metabolic cycles often underestimated side reactions, chemical specificity and the difficulty of keeping every step operating without enzymes.

A failure at one necessary reaction can collapse the entire loop.

A highly favourable side reaction can drain an intermediate before it completes the circuit.

Modern enzymes solve these problems with extraordinary selectivity.

They accelerate desired reactions by enormous factors.

They position substrates precisely.

They control local environments.

Cells regulate enzyme abundance and pathway flux.

They separate incompatible chemistry into compartments.

They continuously regenerate cofactors.

A prebiotic cycle cannot assume those solutions before it has explained them.

The objection remains important.

It prevents a familiar form of wishful chemistry:

Reaction 1 has been demonstrated in one paper.

Reaction 2 works in another solvent.

Reaction 3 works at another temperature.

Reaction 4 requires a different catalyst.

Therefore the full cycle existed on early Earth.

That conclusion does not follow.

The right response, however, is not to declare protometabolism impossible.

Experiments since Orgel's critique have improved the case in exactly the places that matter.

Several reverse-TCA-like reactions have been run consecutively under common conditions.

Iron-driven networks produce many central metabolites simultaneously.

Hydrogen and metal catalysts can drive linked carbon and amino-acid chemistry in one pot.

Researchers are testing environmental cycles, minerals and small-molecule cofactors that might provide selectivity without proteins.

A 2024 review of metabolism-first chemistry framed the central challenge as finding conditions in which geochemical reaction networks become organised by energy flow and then acquire catalysts that redirect and expand that flow.

That is a much stricter target than “make a molecule found in metabolism.”

As of 2026, the target has not been reached completely.

No laboratory system has demonstrated a fully autonomous, self-sustaining, enzyme-free version of a core biological metabolic cycle that begins from simple plausible geochemical feedstocks, continually regenerates its intermediates, couples its energy supply to growth and then becomes heritably evolvable.

That sentence leaves room for progress.

It also states what remains to be done.

Partial networks matter because evolution does not require a modern pathway to appear first.

A messy reaction network can contain useful channels.

A mineral may favour one branch.

A small organic catalyst may improve another.

A compartment can keep products local.

An inherited polymer can eventually encode or template better catalysts.

Selection can turn broad geochemistry into narrower biochemistry.

This gives metabolism-first models a possible evolutionary route.

But the moment heredity begins improving catalysts, the argument is no longer purely “metabolism first.”

Information has entered.

And once an informational polymer requires continuous activated chemistry to replicate, an “RNA first” system is no longer purely about information either.

The two stories begin to converge.

Perhaps the most productive question is therefore not:

**Which came first?**

It is:

**What is the smallest coupled system in which energy flow, catalysis, compartments and heredity can improve one another?**

That is the system the experiments have not yet fully built.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Orgel (2008), *PLoS Biology*: classic critique of prebiotic metabolic-cycle proposals based on specificity, side reactions and completion of all required steps.
- Fahrenbach & Tran (2020), *Nature Chemistry*: later experiments substantially strengthened non-enzymatic protometabolism but did not remove the integration problem.
- Zimmermann et al. (2024), *Accounts of Chemical Research*: metabolism-first models require geochemical energy flow, network organisation and progressive catalytic control/coenzyme emergence.
- No complete self-sustaining, heritably evolvable, enzyme-free prebiotic metabolic cycle is claimed as demonstrated.

**Decision:** partial cycles and connected networks are evidence for chemical plausibility, not permission to infer missing steps.
