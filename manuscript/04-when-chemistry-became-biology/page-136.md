# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 136 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### A PROTOCELL IS NOT YET A LIVING CELL

We can now build remarkable pieces of a primitive cell in the laboratory.

Simple amphiphiles can self-assemble into compartments.

Mineral surfaces can accelerate vesicle formation and help encapsulate RNA.

Small nutrients can cross some primitive membranes while larger polymers remain trapped.

RNA copying can occur inside model vesicles.

Chemical additives can reconcile some conflicts between magnesium-dependent RNA chemistry and fragile fatty-acid membranes.

Vesicles can grow by acquiring new amphiphiles.

Internal molecules can make some vesicles outcompete others.

Growth can produce shapes that divide under modest physical forces.

Environmental cycles can redistribute and propagate catalytic RNAs.

And peptide sequence can change the growth rate of a fatty-acid compartment.

Recent experiments have pushed the boundary even farther.

In 2026, Marian Ebeling and colleagues demonstrated an organocatalytic reaction cascade in which small precursor molecules produced lipid-like compounds that spontaneously assembled into protocell-like structures.

The experiment removes the need to begin with a bottle of finished fatty acids.

Also in 2026, Satyam Khanal and colleagues showed that lipid bilayers can be generated **de novo** without a pre-existing membrane template.

Their system built lipid tails from acetate and cysteine using soluble enzymes, generated diacyl lipids that assembled into vesicles, and produced glycolipid membranes capable of maintaining proton gradients.

That addresses a real conceptual puzzle:

if membranes are normally inherited from existing membranes, how could a first membrane appear?

But this second system used evolved enzymes and pore-forming peptides.

It is a synthetic-biological demonstration of possibility, not a prebiotic pathway from bare geochemistry.

Another 2026 synthetic-cell study integrated DNA self-replication, transcription–translation and membrane-lipid biosynthesis inside phospholipid vesicles.

Its synthetic genome encoded six proteins.

That is an impressive integration of functions.

It is also built from recombinant molecular machinery ultimately borrowed from existing biology.

It tells us how cellular modules can cooperate.

It does not recreate the route by which those modules first arose.

This distinction is now more important than ever because laboratory systems are becoming increasingly life-like.

A synthetic cell can be sophisticated without being prebiotic.

A prebiotically plausible vesicle can be chemically simple without being alive.

And a system can reproduce one part of itself without reproducing the complete organisation that makes it a lineage.

As of 2026, no experiment has started with a realistic set of simple geochemical feedstocks and produced a self-maintaining protocell that autonomously:

generates its own boundary material,

copies its own heritable information,

uses energy to maintain its internal chemistry,

grows,

divides,

passes the coupled system to descendants,

and continues open-ended Darwinian evolution without researchers repeatedly supplying evolved biological machinery or directing key steps.

That missing integration is the heart of the origin-of-life problem.

But the gap is no longer empty.

It is crowded with demonstrated mechanisms.

The first cell did not need to invent every modern function at once.

Physics can make boundaries.

Chemistry can copy information.

Environmental cycles can separate strands and divide compartments.

Simple catalysts can change membrane fitness.

Selection can couple those pieces.

One major requirement remains unavoidable.

Every sustained system must pay for its chemistry.

Copying costs free energy.

Building membranes costs free energy.

Maintaining gradients costs free energy.

Repairing damage costs free energy.

A membrane can keep chemistry together.

It cannot power chemistry by itself.

So the next question is not about information or boundaries.

It is about **metabolism**.

Before there were enzymes, ATP synthases or respiratory chains, what flows of energy could keep an early chemical system away from equilibrium?

That takes us back to rock, water, metals and gradients.

Back to the planet.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Ebeling et al. (2026), *Nature Communications*: organocatalytic formation of lipid-like products from small precursors followed by spontaneous protocell assembly.
- Khanal et al. (2026), *Nature Communications*: de novo bilayer generation without pre-existing membrane templates; system uses soluble enzymes and pore-forming peptides and is not presented here as prebiotic chemistry.
- Restrepo Sierra et al. (2026), *Nature Communications*: synthetic phospholipid vesicles integrate transcription–translation, self-replication of a DNA program and membrane synthesis using a six-protein synthetic genome and recombinant cell-free machinery.
- Abil et al. (2024), *Nature Communications*, explicitly noted that a functionally integrated autonomous synthetic cell remained out of reach; the 2026 integration study advances that programme but does not create a prebiotic autonomous cell from simple geochemical feedstocks.

**Decision:** modern synthetic-cell achievements are separated from origin-of-life reconstruction. No complete autonomous transition from simple plausible prebiotic chemistry to an open-ended evolving cellular system is claimed.
