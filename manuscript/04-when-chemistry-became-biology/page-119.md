# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 119 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### THE OLDER THE CLAIM, THE HARDER THE TEST

If 3.7 billion years is not the limit, how far back can we go?

There are claims that reach deeper into the Hadean.

None should be treated casually.

In northern Quebec, the Nuvvuagittuq Supracrustal Belt contains some of the oldest rocks known on Earth.

A 2017 study described microscopic haematite tubes and filaments in iron-rich rocks from the belt and interpreted them as fossil microorganisms associated with ancient hydrothermal vents.

The authors argued that the structures were at least 3.77 billion years old and, depending on the age assigned to the host sequence, possibly far older.

That possibility attracted enormous attention.

It also exposed two independent problems.

**How old is the specific material containing the structures?**

And:

**are the structures biological?**

The surrounding Nuvvuagittuq terrain contains extraordinarily ancient rocks, and newer geochronology has strengthened the case that some parts of the belt are more than four billion years old.

But the age of a terrain does not automatically date every mineral, vein or later-altered unit inside it.

A biosignature has to be shown to be syngenetic with the material whose age is being quoted.

A 2022 study challenged the age of the particular jaspilite-carbonate iron formation associated with purported microfossils, reporting mineral ages around 2.5 billion years and arguing for substantial later hydrothermal processing.

That does not make the entire Nuvvuagittuq belt young.

It means the relationship between the candidate fossils, their host material and the oldest crust is not simple.

The second problem is biogenicity.

Hydrothermal systems can create filamentous and tubular mineral forms without life.

Iron minerals can grow into shapes that resemble biological structures.

Carbon isotope signals can also be modified or introduced after the original rock formed.

Proponents of the biological interpretation point to the combination of morphology, mineralogy and carbon chemistry.

Critics ask whether known abiotic and metamorphic processes can reproduce enough of those features.

The debate remains scientifically useful precisely because it forces the standards higher.

The lesson is not that very ancient life is impossible.

The lesson is that **oldest** is one of the hardest words in palaeobiology.

The farther back we travel, the fewer rocks survive.

The rocks that do survive are more likely to have been heated, squeezed, fractured and chemically altered.

At some point the record becomes so incomplete that the absence of a secure fossil tells us little about whether life existed.

This creates an asymmetry.

A convincing ancient biosignature can establish that life existed by a certain time.

Failure to find one cannot establish that life did not yet exist.

The origin may therefore be older than our oldest surviving evidence by an unknown amount.

That missing interval is where geology begins handing the problem back to chemistry.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Dodd et al. (2017), *Nature*: putative microbial filaments and tubes from Nuvvuagittuq hydrothermal precipitates were interpreted as >3.77 Ga and potentially much older.
- Nuvvuagittuq age remains a complex geochronological problem; 2025 work strengthened evidence that some belt rocks are at least ~4.16 Ga.
- Lan et al. (2022), *Precambrian Research*: mineral dating of the jaspilite-carbonate BIF hosting purported microfossils yielded ~2.5 Ga ages and challenged a simple Eoarchean age assignment for that unit.
- Ancient hydrothermal mineral systems can produce abiotic biomorphs; morphology must be paired with syngeneity, chemistry and geological context.

**Decision:** Nuvvuagittuq is presented as an important but unresolved older-life claim. The great age of parts of the belt is not used automatically to date the candidate biosignatures.
