# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 113 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### WHEN DOES CHEMISTRY BECOME LIFE?

At the end of the last chapter, the young Earth had water, rock, gases, minerals, energy gradients and organic molecules.

That gives chemistry a vast playground.

It does not tell us where biology begins.

The difficulty starts with a question that sounds simpler than it is:

**What counts as life?**

There is no single scientific definition accepted in every field.

Modern organisms share familiar features. They use energy. They maintain internal chemistry. They reproduce. They carry heritable information. They respond to their surroundings. Populations evolve.

But any checklist immediately creates awkward edge cases.

A sterile animal is alive even though it cannot reproduce.

A dormant seed may show almost no obvious metabolism.

Viruses carry genetic information and evolve, yet depend on cells for the machinery that copies them.

Fire consumes fuel, grows and spreads, but no biologist considers a flame an organism.

Crystals can produce ordered copies of their structure, but they do not build a heritable system that accumulates adaptive innovations generation after generation.

For origin-of-life research, a useful working definition therefore focuses less on appearance and more on process.

A frequently used astrobiology definition describes life as a **self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution**.

It is a working definition, not a law of nature.

Its strength is that it points toward the transition we are trying to explain.

A living system must somehow use matter and energy to continue operating.

It must preserve enough information for descendants to resemble their predecessors.

Copies must sometimes differ.

Some of those differences must affect persistence or reproduction.

And the information required for the system to keep evolving must be carried by the evolving system itself rather than continually supplied by an experimenter.

That last point matters.

A chemist can make molecules copy, move products between flasks, purify useful compounds and add fresh reagents at exactly the right time.

Nature had no laboratory technician.

The first evolving system had to function within the physical environment that produced it.

This does not mean the first life resembled a bacterium.

It almost certainly did not possess the full molecular machinery of any modern cell.

DNA genomes, protein enzymes, ribosomes, ion pumps and sophisticated membranes are products of an immense evolutionary history.

Using a modern cell as the minimum requirement would make the origin problem impossible by definition.

The real target is more basic:

**When did chemistry acquire heritable memory, self-maintenance and the capacity to evolve without an external designer directing each step?**

That boundary may not have been crossed in a single instant.

There may have been intermediate systems that were more life-like than ordinary chemistry but less autonomous than anything alive today.

The origin of life may therefore be less like a switch being thrown and more like a threshold being crossed.

Our task is to identify what that threshold required — and what evidence could ever show that it happened.

## Research / fact-check notes
- NASA Astrobiology, working definition of life: a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution.
- Joyce interview/history of the NASA working definition: definition is useful but not universally binding.
- Adamski et al. (2020), *Nature Reviews Chemistry*, integration of replication, metabolism and compartmentalisation.
- Graham (2026), *Annual Review of Earth and Planetary Sciences*, agnostic biosignatures and the difficulty of defining life only by familiar Earth traits.

**Decision:** no universal definition of life is presented as settled fact. The chapter uses Darwinian evolvability, self-maintenance and internally carried information as an operational boundary for investigating the chemistry-to-biology transition.
