# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 133 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### A PROTOCELL CAN COMPETE

A membrane that never grows has no future.

For a compartment to reproduce, it must acquire more boundary material.

Fatty-acid vesicles can do this without enzymes.

When additional fatty acids are supplied as micelles or soluble molecules, those amphiphiles can move into existing membranes.

The vesicle gains surface area.

Its boundary grows.

At first glance, every vesicle in the same solution should have equal access to the new material.

Experiments show that they do not.

In 2004, Irene Chen, Richard Roberts and Jack Szostak placed different amounts of RNA inside fatty-acid vesicles.

The encapsulated RNA increased the concentration of trapped solute.

That created an osmotic effect.

Vesicles with more internal RNA were under greater pressure to increase their volume.

One way to relieve that imbalance was to acquire fatty acids from neighbouring membranes.

As a result, RNA-rich vesicles could grow at the expense of more relaxed vesicles.

Nothing in the RNA had to encode a membrane protein.

The RNA did not manufacture lipids.

A simple physical consequence of having more internal material was enough to connect **genome content** to **membrane growth**.

That is a primitive form of competition.

The result is important because it shows how genotype and compartment can begin interacting before modern genetics exists.

But osmotic pressure is not the only possible connection.

In 2013, Katarzyna Adamala and Jack Szostak built a different model.

Their fatty-acid vesicles contained a small peptide catalyst.

The catalyst helped make a second, more hydrophobic dipeptide.

That product associated with the membrane.

Vesicles containing the productive chemistry gained a greater affinity for fatty acids and grew preferentially when competing with other vesicles.

Now internal **catalytic function** affected external reproductive success.

This is much closer to the logic evolution needs.

A molecule inside performs useful chemistry.

That chemistry alters the membrane.

The altered membrane acquires more resources.

The entire compartment gains a fitness advantage.

The connection still was not genetic in the modern sense.

The catalytic peptide had been supplied by the experimenters rather than encoded and inherited through a primitive genome.

But the physical bridge was demonstrated.

A reaction inside a simple compartment can change the growth rate of the compartment itself.

This matters because natural selection needs differences that affect persistence or reproduction.

If every vesicle grows at the same rate regardless of its contents, the membrane contributes little to molecular evolution.

If internal chemistry changes growth, then different contents create different protocell fates.

Selection can begin comparing **whole systems**.

There is also a more subtle consequence.

A compartment can turn concentration into advantage.

Useful catalysts are kept nearby.

Reaction products accumulate.

Molecules that would be too dilute in open water can reach higher local concentrations.

Internal osmotic pressure can feed back onto membrane behaviour.

The boundary is no longer a passive bag.

It becomes part of an interacting physical system.

But growth creates a geometric problem.

A bigger membrane around the same volume becomes floppy.

Add enough surface area and the vesicle changes shape.

Eventually a successful compartment has to solve the same problem every cell faces:

**how does one become two?**

For the first protocells, the answer may not have required a division machine.

It may only have required growth, movement and a little force.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Chen, Roberts & Szostak (2004), *Science*: encapsulated nucleic acids create osmotic pressure that can drive preferential fatty-acid uptake and competitive vesicle growth.
- Adamala & Szostak (2013), *Nature Chemistry*: an encapsulated catalyst produced a membrane-associated dipeptide that increased fatty-acid affinity and protocell growth during competition.
- These systems demonstrate physical coupling between internal contents/function and membrane fitness; neither is a complete genetically encoded evolving protocell.

**Decision:** competition between model protocells is experimentally established. The manuscript distinguishes physical/catalytic fitness coupling from a complete hereditary genotype–phenotype system.
