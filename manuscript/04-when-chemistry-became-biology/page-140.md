# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 140 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### MINERALS CAN CATALYSE CHEMISTRY

Energy is useless if reactions cannot reach it.

Hydrogen and carbon dioxide can coexist for a long time without rapidly becoming the molecules a cell needs.

The overall reaction may be energetically favourable while a large **activation barrier** keeps it slow.

A catalyst lowers that barrier.

Modern organisms solve this with enzymes and metal-containing cofactors.

Before proteins existed, the planet supplied catalytic surfaces of its own.

Iron.

Nickel.

Cobalt.

Sulfur minerals.

Clays.

Oxides.

Metallic grains.

These materials can bind molecules, transfer electrons and stabilise reaction intermediates.

They do not behave like precise modern enzymes.

A mineral surface offers many imperfect sites rather than one genetically tuned active site.

Products can stick too strongly.

Side reactions can occur.

Catalyst composition varies from place to place.

But mineral chemistry establishes an important principle:

**catalysis does not require life.**

Iron and sulfur are particularly interesting because they remain deeply embedded in modern biochemistry.

Many enzymes contain iron–sulfur clusters.

These small assemblies of iron and sulfur shuttle electrons and help catalyse reactions in respiration, carbon fixation and biosynthesis.

Their geometric and chemical resemblance to some iron-sulfide minerals has encouraged proposals that biological cofactors inherited functions from earlier mineral catalysts.

That possibility is intriguing.

Similarity is not ancestry.

Modern iron–sulfur clusters are assembled and controlled by proteins.

Natural minerals form under different constraints.

We cannot look at a modern enzyme, notice an iron–sulfur centre and conclude that its direct ancestor was a hydrothermal mineral grain.

What experiments can test is whether geochemical materials perform relevant chemistry.

They do.

Native iron, nickel and cobalt can reduce carbon dioxide.

Iron sulfides can generate hydrogen under suitable hydrothermal conditions.

Nickel can catalyse hydrogen-driven reductive chemistry.

Iron ions can promote networks involving central metabolic intermediates.

Meteorite minerals can perform some of the same reactions in laboratory systems.

The 2025 iron-sulfide chemical-garden experiment gives a particularly clear example of the distinction between **fuel** and **life**.

Mackinawite and greigite formed under simulated early-Archaean hydrothermal conditions.

Their formation released abiotic hydrogen.

A living methanogen then used that hydrogen.

The minerals did not become the organism.

The geochemistry fed it.

That is exactly the kind of interface an origin scenario must eventually explain in reverse.

Before enzymes existed, mineral and solution chemistry may have supplied catalytic functions externally.

As evolving molecules improved, organic catalysts could have begun replacing, modifying or recruiting those functions.

A loosely controlled surface reaction could become associated with a compartment.

A small organic cofactor could carry metal chemistry away from the rock.

A peptide could bind the cofactor more selectively.

Eventually a protein enzyme could make the reaction fast, specific and regulatable.

This is a plausible evolutionary direction.

The historical details are unknown.

The important evidence comes from what minerals can demonstrably do.

They can generate disequilibrium.

They can accelerate reactions.

They can produce compounds used in biology.

They can create structured microenvironments.

What they have not yet been shown to do is organise those reactions into a complete, self-sustaining, heritable metabolism.

For that, we need to look at the actual carbon products.

Can geochemical catalysts make molecules that sit inside the metabolic map of life?

Yes.

And that is where the metabolism-first case becomes much more experimentally concrete.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Helmbrecht et al. (2025), *Nature Ecology & Evolution*: iron-sulfide formation produces abiotic H₂ capable of feeding a modern H₂-dependent archaeal metabolism.
- Varma et al. (2018), *Nature Ecology & Evolution*: native Fe, Ni and Co promote CO₂ reduction to acetate and pyruvate.
- Muchowska et al. (2017, 2019): transition metals and Fe²⁺ promote multiple non-enzymatic reactions overlapping core carbon metabolism.
- Iron–sulfur clusters are ancient and widespread biological cofactors, but mineral/cofactor structural or chemical similarity does not by itself establish direct evolutionary ancestry.

**Decision:** mineral catalysis is established as chemically relevant to origin scenarios. Direct descent from specific mineral catalysts to specific modern enzymes remains a hypothesis.
