# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 115 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### A COPY IS NOT ENOUGH

Imagine a molecule that can make another molecule like itself.

That would be remarkable.

It would still face an immediate problem.

Where does the energy come from?

Living systems are not objects sitting peacefully at equilibrium.

They are maintained by continuous flows of matter and energy.

Cells take in energy-rich chemicals or light.

They drive reactions that would not otherwise proceed fast enough or in the required direction.

They build molecules.

They repair damage.

They maintain gradients.

They export waste and heat.

When those flows stop permanently, organised living chemistry eventually collapses toward equilibrium.

The first life did not need modern metabolism.

But some form of **energy coupling** was unavoidable.

Chemical work requires a source of free energy and a mechanism that channels part of that energy into useful reactions.

Geochemical environments provide many possibilities.

Hydrothermal systems generate redox and proton gradients.

Sunlight supplies photons.

Wet–dry cycles can repeatedly concentrate reactants and change reaction conditions.

Mineral surfaces can adsorb molecules and alter reaction pathways.

Natural chemical gradients can place electron donors beside electron acceptors.

Yet energy alone does not organise a living system.

Reaction rates matter too.

Many biologically useful reactions are painfully slow without catalysts.

Modern cells solve this mainly with protein enzymes and catalytic RNA.

Primitive systems had to begin with simpler catalysts: metal ions, mineral surfaces, short molecules, ribozymes, or combinations that have not survived unchanged into modern life.

Then comes another problem.

Useful products diffuse away.

A replicator that helps make a catalyst gains little if every neighbouring molecule receives the benefit equally.

Chemistry needs some way to create local neighbourhoods.

Modern cells use lipid membranes.

The earliest compartments may have been simpler.

Fatty-acid vesicles can self-assemble under suitable conditions.

Rock pores can confine reactions.

Droplets and other phase-separated structures can concentrate molecules without a modern membrane.

Mineral surfaces can hold reactants close together.

Different origin scenarios use different forms of confinement.

No single one has been established as the historical answer.

What matters is the function.

A compartment can keep interacting molecules near one another.

It can maintain gradients.

It can link the success of a replicator to the chemical system that supports it.

And if compartments grow, divide and pass useful chemistry to descendants, selection can begin acting on whole molecular collectives rather than isolated molecules.

This is why the origin problem cannot be reduced to finding one magical molecule.

Replication matters.

Energy matters.

Catalysis matters.

Compartmentalisation matters.

But the decisive step is **integration**.

The pieces must begin helping one another persist.

The system has to remain far from equilibrium long enough to reproduce its organisation.

And the information controlling that organisation must become inheritable.

Modern life makes this look effortless because billions of years of evolution sit between us and the first successful systems.

At the beginning, every dependency had to be simpler.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Adamski et al. (2020), *Nature Reviews Chemistry*: de novo life requires integration of replication, metabolism/energy use and compartmentalisation in an out-of-equilibrium system.
- Singh et al. (2024), *Nature Reviews Chemistry*: living-matter-like systems combine non-equilibrium assembly, metabolic processes, replication and confinement.
- Origin-of-life compartment research includes fatty-acid vesicles, rock pores and phase-separated droplets; none is treated here as the uniquely established historical route.
- Hydrothermal, photochemical and wet–dry energy sources are presented as candidate environments, not verdicts.

**Decision:** no modern-cell component is made a prerequisite for first life. The functional requirements are energy coupling, catalysis, local confinement and integration with heredity.
