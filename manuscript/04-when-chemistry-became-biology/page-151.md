# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 151 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### WHAT HAVE WE ACTUALLY BUILT?

After forty pages, it is time to take inventory.

Not of theories.

Of experiments.

Scientists have produced amino acids and many other organic molecules without biology.

They have identified extraterrestrial amino acids, nucleobases and related organics in primitive Solar-System material.

They have demonstrated plausible routes toward activated ribonucleotide building blocks.

They have produced common precursor chemistry feeding molecules relevant to RNA, peptides and membranes.

RNA can store sequence information.

RNA can catalyse reactions.

Laboratory-evolved ribozymes can copy RNA templates.

Higher-fidelity RNA polymerase ribozymes can propagate functional RNAs through Darwinian selection.

Freeze–thaw and pH cycling can help solve the strand-separation problem.

In 2026, the 45-nucleotide QT45 ribozyme demonstrated the two component template-copying reactions required for replication of its own sequence and its complement under controlled conditions.

Simple amphiphiles spontaneously form vesicles.

Primitive membranes can admit some small building blocks while retaining larger polymers.

RNA copying can occur inside model protocells.

Fatty-acid vesicles can grow.

They can compete for membrane material.

Internal chemistry can change their growth rate.

Growth can produce shapes that divide under modest physical forces.

Peptide sequence can alter vesicle fitness.

New 2025–2026 chemistry can form protocell-like membranes from simpler precursors rather than requiring researchers to begin with finished membrane lipids.

Metals can reduce CO₂ into acetate and pyruvate.

Non-enzymatic networks can produce large subsets of central metabolic intermediates.

Hydrogen can drive metabolism-like amino-acid chemistry.

Iron-sulfide geochemistry can generate enough H₂ to fuel an existing hydrogen-dependent microorganism.

Modern synthetic-cell systems can now combine several astonishingly complex modules.

DNA programs can replicate.

Transcription and translation can operate inside vesicles.

Lipid biosynthesis can enlarge membranes.

All of these statements are true.

Now the equally important statement:

**No experiment has yet joined all of those achievements into the complete natural transition we are trying to explain.**

The strongest RNA systems still receive chemically activated substrates, controlled cycling or laboratory-selected catalysts.

The strongest protocell systems still begin with prepared components or rely on supplied catalysts.

The strongest protometabolic systems still lack a heritable mechanism that improves the whole reaction network autonomously.

Synthetic cells use proteins, ribosomes and other molecular machinery inherited from billions of years of biological evolution.

They demonstrate integration.

They do not erase the origin problem.

This is why two apparently contradictory claims can both be scientifically accurate.

“Scientists have reproduced many processes required for life's origin.”

True.

“Scientists have not recreated life's complete origin from plausible simple prebiotic inputs.”

Also true.

The distance between them has become much smaller.

It has not disappeared.

And the shrinking matters.

Every successful experiment converts one kind of mystery into a mechanism.

Membranes no longer require a cell in order to exist.

Catalysis does not require proteins.

Information storage does not require DNA.

Carbon fixation does not require enzymes in every step.

Replication does not require a modern polymerase.

Division does not require a cytoskeleton.

A natural environment can perform functions later internalised by life.

The remaining problem is not one impossible miracle-shaped gap.

It is an **integration problem**.

Can chemistry create a coupled system in which each useful subsystem helps the others persist — and in which inherited variation lets that whole system improve without a researcher steering it?

That is the experiment that has not yet been completed.

When it is, it will not tell us with certainty that Earth followed exactly the same route.

But it would show that the boundary can be crossed.

For now, we have reached that boundary from both sides.

Chemistry shows us what might approach it.

Geology shows us that life somehow crossed it.
