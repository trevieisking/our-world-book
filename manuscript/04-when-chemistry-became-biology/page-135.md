# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 135 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### WHEN A SEQUENCE CHANGES A CELL'S FITNESS

Natural selection needs a connection.

Information must change function.

Function must change reproductive success.

Modern life makes the chain obvious.

A DNA sequence changes a protein.

The protein changes metabolism, structure or behaviour.

That change affects survival or reproduction.

The altered DNA is inherited.

Early protocells had to discover a simpler version of the same logic.

In April 2026, Akiko Baba and colleagues reported a useful experimental step toward that bridge.

They studied fatty-acid vesicles in the presence of short peptides whose amino-acid sequences were deliberately varied.

Different peptide sequences changed vesicle growth by different amounts.

Some sequences promoted growth much more strongly than others.

Changing one amino acid could alter the effect.

Importantly, the effect of a substitution depended on the rest of the peptide sequence.

Geneticists call this **epistasis** when the effect of one change depends on other positions.

The experiment therefore produced something resembling a primitive **fitness landscape**:

sequence differences mapped onto differences in compartment growth.

The mechanism was physical.

The peptides altered the chemical potential of fatty-acid molecules.

Some sequences made incorporation of fatty acids into the vesicle membrane more favourable.

Those vesicles grew faster.

This is significant because no modern metabolic pathway was required.

No membrane protein was required.

No lipid-synthesis enzyme was required.

Molecular sequence itself could influence the reproductive component of a protocell.

But the experiment did **not** contain a primitive genetic code.

The peptides were prepared and supplied by the researchers.

They were not translated from an RNA genome inside the vesicles.

The vesicles were fed fatty acids and peptides.

Growth was measured; a complete growth–division–inheritance cycle driven by internally encoded peptides was not demonstrated.

That boundary keeps the result useful.

It shows what evolution could exploit **if** a primitive informational system became capable of producing sequence-dependent peptides.

The moment an inherited sequence influences membrane growth, mutations are no longer only changes in molecular copying.

They can become changes in compartment fitness.

This points toward one possible route by which an RNA world and a peptide world could become entangled.

RNA sequence can already produce catalytic RNA structure.

Short peptides can alter membranes and catalysis.

If RNA eventually helped specify peptide sequence — even crudely — the functional possibilities would expand dramatically.

Peptides offer side-chain chemistry that RNA cannot easily match.

Membranes provide a body in which the benefits can stay local.

Selection can then favour information systems that build molecules improving their own compartment.

The modern genotype–phenotype relationship did not need to appear in one leap.

There could have been intermediate stages:

RNA sequence affects RNA function.

RNA function changes local chemistry.

Local chemistry changes membrane growth.

Short peptides join the network.

Some peptide sequences improve membrane or catalytic behaviour.

Eventually sequence information becomes systematically translated into peptide sequence.

At that point, heredity gains a new language.

This remains a scenario, not a recovered historical sequence.

But the individual links are becoming experimentally testable.

And that changes the origin-of-life problem again.

The question is no longer whether molecular sequence **can** affect a protocell boundary.

It can.

The harder question is how a primitive system connected that advantage to reliable inheritance.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Baba et al. (2026), *Communications Chemistry*: defined peptide sequences produced different fatty-acid-vesicle growth rates; the system showed sequence-dependent fitness effects and epistasis driven by changes in fatty-acid chemical potential.
- Adamala & Szostak (2013), *Nature Chemistry*: encapsulated catalytic chemistry can also alter membrane growth and protocell competition.
- No translation or genetically encoded peptide synthesis occurred in the 2026 Baba system.

**Decision:** 2026 peptide–vesicle coupling is treated as direct evidence that primitive polymer sequence can map onto compartment growth, while the missing hereditary encoding step is kept explicit.
