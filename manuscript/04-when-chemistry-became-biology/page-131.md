# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 131 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### A GOOD MEMBRANE MUST LEAK

A closed membrane solves one problem by creating another.

If nothing can cross it, the chemistry inside eventually stops.

Raw materials are consumed.

Waste accumulates.

Energy sources run down.

The compartment becomes a sealed chemical tomb.

Modern cells avoid this with extraordinary transport machinery.

Protein channels allow selected ions through.

Pumps spend energy to move molecules against gradients.

Receptors and transporters distinguish nutrients from unwanted compounds.

Primitive compartments had none of that.

Their membranes had to provide useful transport through their own physical properties.

This is one reason fatty-acid membranes are interesting.

Compared with the phospholipid membranes of modern cells, many fatty-acid membranes are more dynamic and more permeable.

Individual fatty-acid molecules exchange with the surrounding solution.

Small polar molecules can cross under conditions where larger polymers remain trapped.

The membrane is not simply a wall.

It acts as a crude molecular filter.

In 2008, Sheref Mansy and colleagues demonstrated this experimentally in a model protocell.

Their membranes were built from fatty acids and related simple amphiphiles.

Small charged molecules, including activated nucleotides, could cross the membrane from the outside.

Longer oligonucleotides remained inside.

The incoming activated building blocks could then participate in template-directed copying of a genetic polymer inside the vesicle.

That is an important combination.

A primitive compartment does not necessarily need a protein transporter before it can feed an internal genetic system.

The membrane itself can discriminate partly by size, charge and molecular structure.

Later experiments extended the result.

Citrate-chelated magnesium ions can increase the permeability of fatty-acid membranes to short RNA oligomers.

Elevated temperature can increase passage of short oligomers while longer RNA templates remain confined.

Mixed-sequence RNA templates have therefore been copied chemically inside model vesicles containing all four canonical RNA bases.

Again, this is not yet a cell.

The experimenter prepares the membrane.

The experimenter supplies activated nucleotides or oligomers.

The experimenter controls the solution.

But an old objection has been weakened.

A membrane around an early genetic system does not automatically isolate that system from its food.

Permeability also creates risks.

If the membrane is too leaky, useful products escape.

If it is too impermeable, nutrients cannot enter.

If changing salt, pH or temperature makes the membrane fall apart, heredity is lost.

Different molecules cross at different rates.

And a membrane composition that is ideal for RNA copying may be poor for growth or environmental stability.

This means membrane evolution could itself become selectable.

A slightly different mixture of amphiphiles can change permeability.

A bound peptide can change membrane behaviour.

Adding more complex lipids can strengthen a boundary but may also make passive nutrient uptake harder.

The transition toward modern membranes was therefore probably not a simple story of “better membranes become less leaky.”

Modern cells can afford tight membranes because they possess transport proteins and energy-driven pumps.

Primitive cells had to trade control for accessibility.

Before channels evolved, **leakiness was a feature**.

It allowed chemistry outside a compartment to feed heredity inside it.

And it gives us another example of a recurring theme in life's origin:

a weakness in a modern engineering sense can be an advantage before machinery exists.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Mansy et al. (2008), *Nature*: fatty-acid-based membranes permit entry of activated nucleotides while retaining longer genetic polymers, enabling internal template copying.
- O'Flaherty et al. (2018), *JACS*: citrate-chelated Mg²⁺ and temperature can enhance permeability to short RNA oligomers and support mixed-sequence copying inside fatty-acid vesicles.
- Schrum, Zhu & Szostak (2010), *Cold Spring Harbor Perspectives in Biology*: dynamic fatty-acid membranes provide primitive growth and nutrient-uptake properties absent from tightly sealed modern phospholipid membranes.

**Decision:** primitive membrane permeability is presented as a functional trade-off, not as modern selective transport. Passive uptake works for some model systems and molecules; no universal nutrient-permeability solution is claimed.
