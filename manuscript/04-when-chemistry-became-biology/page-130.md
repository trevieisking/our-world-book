# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 130 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### MEMBRANES BEFORE CELLS

A membrane looks complicated when we examine a modern cell.

Its basic physical principle is not.

An **amphiphile** is a molecule with two different preferences.

One part interacts favourably with water.

Another avoids it.

Place enough suitable amphiphiles in water and they can organise themselves.

Water-loving regions face outward.

Water-avoiding regions gather together.

Sheets can form.

Sheets can close.

A closed bilayer sheet becomes a vesicle: a microscopic water-filled compartment surrounded by a membrane.

No gene has to instruct the molecules where to go.

No enzyme has to place them one by one.

The arrangement emerges from molecular interactions and the tendency of the system to lower its free energy.

Modern cell membranes are dominated by sophisticated phospholipids.

The first membranes need not have been.

Single-chain amphiphiles such as fatty acids are chemically simpler and have therefore been studied extensively as protocell candidates.

Fatty acids and related compounds have several plausible abiotic sources.

They occur in carbonaceous meteorites.

They can form in catalytic reactions involving metals.

They can be produced in laboratory simulations of energetic and hydrothermal chemistry.

A 2023 review concluded that membrane-forming fatty acids could plausibly have been available on early Earth, while stressing that their actual local abundance is poorly constrained.

That last point matters.

Having fatty acids somewhere on a planet is not the same as having enough of them in one puddle, pore or shoreline to build membranes.

Vesicle formation depends on concentration.

It depends on chain length.

It depends strongly on pH.

Salts and divalent ions can destabilise some simple membranes.

Mixtures can behave differently from pure compounds.

This is one reason a primordial membrane should not be imagined as a perfect sphere made from one laboratory-grade fatty acid.

Prebiotic chemistry was messy.

Messiness may sometimes help.

Experiments with mixtures of fatty acids, fatty alcohols and related amphiphiles have shown that mixed membranes can remain stable across broader ranges of pH, salt and temperature than some pure systems.

Mineral surfaces can also change the process.

In 2003, montmorillonite clay was shown to accelerate the conversion of fatty-acid micelles into vesicles dramatically.

Some of the clay particles became enclosed by the membranes.

RNA bound to the clay could become encapsulated with them.

This connected three previously separate ingredients:

a mineral surface,

an informational polymer,

and a self-assembling boundary.

Recent experiments have widened the possibilities further.

In work published in 2025, cysteine reacted in water with short-chain thioesters to produce diacyl lipids that spontaneously formed protocell-like vesicles.

Silica enhanced the process, and the resulting compartments were compatible with functional ribozymes.

In 2026, another team demonstrated an organocatalytic reaction cascade beginning with small molecules that generated lipid-like products up to about twenty carbons long.

Those products spontaneously assembled into protocell-like structures without researchers first supplying finished membrane amphiphiles.

Neither experiment reproduces the first membrane on Earth.

Their importance is more general.

The membrane problem is not limited to one route in which a large supply of perfect, pre-made fatty acids must somehow appear.

Chemistry can make amphiphiles.

Surfaces can organise them.

Mixtures can stabilise them.

And once suitable molecules become concentrated, **a boundary can assemble itself**.

A vesicle is still not alive.

But one of life's defining pieces does not require life to build it.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Cohen et al. (2023), *ACS Earth and Space Chemistry*: review of plausible early-Earth fatty-acid sources and the unresolved concentration problem.
- Hanczyc, Fujikawa & Szostak (2003), *Science*: montmorillonite accelerates fatty-acid vesicle formation and can promote encapsulation of clay-associated RNA.
- Jordan et al. (2019), *Nature Ecology & Evolution*: mixed single-chain amphiphiles can improve vesicle formation/stability over broader environmental conditions.
- Cho et al. (2025), *Nature Chemistry* (published online 2024): cysteine plus short-chain thioesters generates diacyl-lipid protocell-like vesicles compatible with ribozyme activity.
- Ebeling et al. (2026), *Nature Communications*: organocatalytic formation of lipid-like molecules from small precursors followed by spontaneous protocell assembly.

**Decision:** spontaneous membrane assembly is established physical chemistry. The composition, source, concentration mechanism and location of the first biologically relevant membranes remain unresolved.
