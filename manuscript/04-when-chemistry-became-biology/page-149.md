# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 149 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### EVERY ENVIRONMENT PAYS A PRICE

We can now put the major candidate environments beside one another.

Not to choose a winner.

To see the trade-offs.

**Deep hydrothermal systems** provide continuous water–rock chemistry.

They can generate hydrogen.

They create redox gradients.

Minerals provide catalytic surfaces.

Flow can continually supply reactants and remove products.

But deep ocean settings receive almost no ultraviolet light.

They do not naturally provide the same drying cycles available on land.

High salt and metal concentrations can interfere with simple membranes and some RNA chemistry.

And the specific claim that natural proton gradients powered prebiotic carbon fixation remains unresolved.

**Freshwater volcanic pools and hot springs** provide a different machine.

They can concentrate solutes by evaporation.

Wet–dry cycling can favour condensation chemistry.

Rehydration can reorganise amphiphiles into vesicles.

Sunlight and volcanic chemistry provide energy.

Mineral surfaces are abundant.

But exposed pools also expose fragile molecules to ultraviolet radiation.

Water chemistry can change rapidly.

Repeated heating and drying can destroy as well as build.

And the amount of exposed Hadean land and freshwater habitat is still uncertain.

**UV-lit surface environments** make photochemistry possible.

The 2015 cyanosulfidic network shows how light can help generate precursors relevant to RNA, amino acids and lipids from related chemistry.

But useful ultraviolet wavelengths can also destroy products.

Successful surface chemistry therefore needs timing, shielding, depth, mineral protection or transport.

**Ice and freezing environments** concentrate dissolved material into eutectic liquid channels.

Cold can protect RNA from hydrolysis.

Freeze–thaw cycles can separate, concentrate and redistribute molecular systems.

Recent experiments show that such cycling can support remarkably sophisticated RNA replication chemistry.

But freezing slows many reactions.

Not every precursor survives high local salt concentrations.

And ice does not supply every carbon, energy or membrane-building step.

**Mineral surfaces** solve another set of problems.

They adsorb molecules.

They concentrate them.

They orient reactants.

They catalyse reactions.

Some accelerate membrane formation.

But molecules that bind too strongly can become trapped.

Minerals can also catalyse destructive reactions.

Their composition varies dramatically.

And a molecule attached to a rock has to become mobile if it is eventually to participate in a freely reproducing cell.

No environment receives everything for free.

That is not a flaw in origin-of-life science.

It is a constraint.

A useful theory has to explain not only what an environment provides but what it makes difficult.

This is why the phrase **“the place where life began”** may be premature.

The origin could have been local.

One environment might eventually prove capable of performing nearly every essential transition.

But current evidence does not establish that.

It is also possible that the decisive system formed where environments met.

A wet mineral surface beside a hydrothermal pool.

A shoreline receiving volcanic and atmospheric products.

An icy region periodically warmed.

A porous rock connected to open water.

The boundary between environments can itself create gradients.

What matters is not the label on the location.

It is the sequence of physical operations:

concentrate,

activate,

polymerise,

protect,

copy,

separate,

encapsulate,

feed,

grow,

divide,

inherit.

Different environments perform different subsets of those operations well.

The origin problem is the problem of joining them.

And joining them requires movement.
