# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 150 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### THE PLANET CAN BE THE APPARATUS

A laboratory chemist changes conditions deliberately.

Heat this.

Cool that.

Change the pH.

Add a catalyst.

Remove water.

Rehydrate.

Illuminate.

Shield from light.

Transfer the product into a new vessel.

Origins researchers rightly worry about this.

If a pathway only works because a scientist performs a carefully timed sequence of interventions, then the experiment has not yet explained how the early Earth performed those interventions.

But a planet changes conditions too.

Not intelligently.

Physically.

Water flows downhill.

Rain dilutes.

Evaporation concentrates.

Groundwater moves through rock.

Hot fluid rises.

Cold water sinks.

Wind carries aerosols.

Particles settle.

Minerals adsorb compounds.

Ice excludes solutes.

Thawing releases them.

Waves, tides and streams move material between zones.

Sunrise and sunset change radiation.

Eruptions and impacts abruptly change local chemistry.

The scientific question is whether these ordinary processes can replace the technician.

That is the strongest form of a multi-environment origin model.

Not:

“Reaction A works somewhere and reaction B works somewhere else.”

But:

**“A natural process physically carries the output of A into conditions where B can happen.”**

Each proposed handoff must survive four tests.

First, **transport**.

Can the material actually move between the settings?

Second, **survival**.

Does it persist long enough to arrive?

Third, **compatibility**.

Do impurities and side products from the first environment poison the next reaction?

Fourth, **concentration**.

After transport and dilution, is enough material left for the next step to matter?

These tests are demanding.

They should be.

A multi-stage pathway that ignores them is only a story drawn with arrows.

There are already experimentally suggestive handoffs.

RNA adsorbed to montmorillonite clay can become encapsulated when fatty-acid vesicles form around the mineral.

A polymer can therefore move conceptually from a surface-concentrated state into a membrane compartment without a scientist placing the molecule through a membrane.

Dry lipid films containing polymers can rehydrate into vesicles that encapsulate part of the material.

Freezing can concentrate an RNA system and thawing can return it to a more dilute environment.

Hydrothermal flow can move reduced compounds from rock–water reactions into cooler mixing zones where different chemistry becomes possible.

These examples demonstrate physical mechanisms.

They do not yet combine into one historical pathway from simple feedstocks to life.

A 2018 review of life's building blocks reached an important systems conclusion: many successful prebiotic reactions require diverse environmental conditions, suggesting that transport through dynamic connected settings deserves serious consideration.

A 2026 review of early-Earth redox chemistry similarly emphasises multiple local environments and movement of organic material rather than one universal reaction zone.

This does not mean a complicated planetary itinerary is necessary.

The simplest successful route may still be local.

A single hydrothermal field can contain hot and cool regions.

A pool contains a wet centre, drying margins and mineral sediments.

Ice contains solid crystals beside concentrated liquid channels.

Environmental diversity exists across centimetres as well as continents.

The useful idea is therefore **chemical geography**.

The origin of life is not only about which reactions are possible.

It is about where those reactions can coexist in space and time.

At some point, however, the geography has to stop doing the work.

A true cell carries its essential chemistry with it.

It can leave the mineral pore.

It can survive when the pool refills.

It can reproduce after the temperature changes.

Its catalysts, information and boundary are linked closely enough that selection acts on the whole system.

The transition from planet-powered chemistry to self-contained biology may be one of the most important transitions in the entire story.

And we still do not know exactly how it happened.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Kitadai & Maruyama (2018), review of building-block origins: diverse reaction requirements motivate connected/dynamic environmental scenarios.
- 2026 *Communications Chemistry* review of early-Earth redox chemistry discusses transport of organics and multiple local environments.
- Hanczyc et al. (2003): mineral-associated RNA can be captured during fatty-acid vesicle formation.
- Wet–dry, freeze–thaw and hydrothermal-flow examples demonstrate mechanisms of environmental handoff, not a complete historical sequence.

**Decision:** “the planet as apparatus” is an evidence-constrained framework. Every proposed transfer remains subject to transport, survival, compatibility and concentration tests.
