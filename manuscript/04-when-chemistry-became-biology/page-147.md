# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 147 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### LIGHT CAN BUILD — AND DESTROY

The early Earth had no modern ozone layer.

Ultraviolet radiation reached surface environments much more strongly than it does today.

That sounds hostile to life.

Before life, it could also power chemistry.

A photon carries energy.

Absorb the right wavelength and a molecule can enter an excited state.

Bonds can break.

Electrons can move.

Reactions that are slow or inaccessible in darkness can proceed.

One influential demonstration came in 2015.

Bhavesh Patel, John Sutherland and colleagues began with hydrogen cyanide-derived chemistry and related simple feedstocks.

Using hydrogen sulfide as a reductant, copper photoredox chemistry and ultraviolet light, they produced chemical routes leading toward **precursors of ribonucleotides, amino acids and lipids**.

The result was important for more than the individual molecules.

RNA, proteins and membranes are often treated as separate origin problems.

Here, precursor chemistry relevant to all three emerged from a related chemical network.

That weakened the assumption that one entire subsystem had to arise before the others could even begin.

The energy source was light.

That places at least part of this chemistry near a surface or within water shallow enough for the required ultraviolet wavelengths to penetrate.

It also creates a problem.

UV radiation is not benevolent.

The same photons that drive useful reactions can break RNA.

They can damage nucleobases.

They can degrade organic molecules and membranes.

Photochemistry therefore depends on **dose, timing and context**.

A compound can require light for its synthesis and shielding for its survival.

Clouds alter the spectrum reaching the ground.

Water absorbs some wavelengths.

Minerals can protect adsorbed molecules.

Dissolved iron and sulfur species can change photochemistry.

A pool can be illuminated at the surface while material settles into darker sediment.

Day and night automatically provide a cycle.

This creates another example in which apparently conflicting requirements can become sequential rather than simultaneous.

Make a precursor in light.

Move or shield it.

Use it in a later dark reaction.

That sounds plausible.

It is not yet a demonstrated planetary sequence.

The laboratory chemistry also used selected reagents and controlled concentrations.

Hydrogen cyanide and reduced sulfur compounds have plausible prebiotic sources, but their local availability on early Earth would depend strongly on atmospheric, volcanic and impact chemistry.

Copper chemistry adds another geochemical constraint.

And successful photochemical networks produce side products as well as useful ones.

The planet does not purify the flask between steps.

Still, ultraviolet-driven chemistry gives surface environments one capability deep ocean settings largely lack:

a continuous high-energy input capable of rearranging simple molecules without enzymes.

This means sunlight before photosynthesis could have mattered in two opposite ways.

It was a destructive radiation hazard.

And a chemical power supply.

Life later evolved pigments, repair systems and ozone-producing ecosystems that changed that relationship.

At the origin, there was no repair machinery.

Prebiotic chemistry had to exploit light without being erased by it.

A surface pool solves this through time, depth, minerals and transport.

Another environment solves it almost completely by shutting the light out.

Freeze the water.

Inside ice, chemistry enters a world that appears still from the outside.

At the molecular scale, it is anything but.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Patel et al. (2015), *Nature Chemistry*: UV-driven cyanosulfidic chemistry produces precursors relevant to ribonucleotides, amino acids and lipids from related feedstocks.
- The experiment demonstrates common chemical accessibility, not simultaneous production of a living system.
- Early surface UV was stronger before an ozone shield; UV is both a synthetic energy source and a degradation hazard.

**Decision:** UV photochemistry is treated as a powerful surface-energy mechanism with genuine compatibility and survival constraints, not as evidence that life necessarily originated at the surface.
