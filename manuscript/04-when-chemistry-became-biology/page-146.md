# CHAPTER FOUR — WHEN CHEMISTRY BECAME BIOLOGY
## Page 146 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### WHEN WATER LEAVES

Water is essential for life.

It is also a chemical obstacle.

Joining small molecules into larger polymers often releases water.

In a large dilute ocean, reactants can remain far apart.

Hydrolysis can break newly formed bonds.

Concentrations can be too low for useful reactions to compete with diffusion.

Drying changes all of that.

As water evaporates, dissolved molecules become concentrated.

Compounds that rarely meet in dilute solution collide more often.

Mineral and lipid surfaces become crowded with reactants.

Low-water conditions can favour condensation reactions that join monomers into larger molecules.

Then water returns.

Products disperse.

Membranes re-form.

Some polymers become encapsulated.

The system begins another cycle.

This is the logic behind **wet–dry cycling** models.

The important feature is not one dramatic drought.

It is repetition.

A pond edge can wet and dry.

A hot spring can fluctuate.

A geyser can splash a mineral surface that repeatedly heats and dries.

Rain can refill an evaporated depression.

Tides can expose and submerge shorelines, although seawater salts create additional membrane and chemistry problems.

Laboratory experiments have shown that hydration–dehydration cycles can promote formation of polymers and produce lipid-encapsulated polymer mixtures when the system is rehydrated.

Bruce Damer and David Deamer developed these observations into the **hot-spring hypothesis**, in which fluctuating freshwater volcanic pools act as repeated chemical reactors.

In that model, amphiphiles organise into layered films during drying.

Monomers become concentrated between lipid layers.

Polymer-forming reactions occur.

Rehydration releases vesicles containing mixtures of polymers.

Repeated cycling subjects enormous numbers of different compartments and molecular combinations to physical and chemical selection.

It is an attractive systems model because one environmental cycle performs several jobs:

concentration,

polymerisation,

encapsulation,

mixing,

and repeated departure from equilibrium.

But the model is not established history.

It requires appropriate exposed land and volcanic hydrothermal fields.

Chapter Three already showed why the amount and distribution of Hadean land remain uncertain.

Freshwater chemistry must be supplied and renewed.

Heat that drives drying can also destroy fragile products.

Salts and metal ions can help some reactions while disrupting membranes.

Repeated dehydration can damage RNA as well as help condensation.

And a surface environment receives ultraviolet radiation far more strongly than a deep ocean setting.

The hot-spring hypothesis therefore solves some problems by accepting others.

That is exactly how a useful origin hypothesis should behave.

It makes predictions that experiments can attack.

Can realistic mixtures polymerise?

Can membranes survive repeated cycles?

Do useful molecules become enriched rather than random tar?

Can catalytic sequences persist?

Can products move between dry films and hydrated protocells without being destroyed?

These questions are still being tested.

What wet–dry experiments have already established is simpler:

**water activity is a chemical variable.**

Early Earth did not have only “water” and “no water.”

It had continuously changing concentrations.

A reaction impossible in a dilute ocean can become favourable on a drying surface.

A polymer made in a dry phase can become functional again after rehydration.

Environmental cycling can therefore connect chemistry that no single steady condition permits.

That insight does not tell us where life began.

It tells us why a changing planet may be more chemically powerful than a stable flask.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Damer & Deamer (2020), *Astrobiology*: hot-spring hypothesis based on repeated hydration/dehydration, polymer formation, lipid organisation and protocell cycling.
- Laboratory and field work demonstrates several component processes; the full progression from wet–dry chemistry to autonomous life is not experimentally complete.
- Hadean subaerial land/freshwater availability is uncertain and is not assumed globally abundant.

**Decision:** wet–dry cycling is treated as a demonstrated concentration/polymerisation mechanism and a plausible environmental framework, not a proven birthplace of life.
