### WHEN THE RECORD GOES QUIET

If an animal disappears from the fossils, has it disappeared from the world?

Sometimes. But the evidence must distinguish extinction from the failure to preserve or recover another specimen.

We have already seen how exceptional deposits keep soft anatomy that ordinary rocks usually lose. That difference becomes especially important when following a group through time.

A shell-bearing animal and a delicate animal need not have equal chances of entering the record. A change in sediment or burial chemistry can alter those chances without changing the animals' evolutionary history at that moment.

An important discovery beyond the Cambrian makes the problem concrete.

In 2010, researchers reported diverse soft-bodied assemblages from the early Ordovician Fezouata formations of Morocco. These included forms associated with the remarkable Cambrian communities represented by the Burgess Shale.

Their presence showed that some of those lineages persisted after the Cambrian. An apparent disappearance from familiar deposits had shortened the history available to researchers, rather than necessarily recording the actual extinction of every group involved.

This does not make Fezouata a surviving copy of one Burgess Shale ecosystem. It is a younger assemblage in its own environment, containing both continuing lineages and other elements.

Its discovery extends the investigation. It also raises a new question.

Are these unusually informative deposits equally good at preserving the same things?

A 2020 study compared tissue occurrences in Fezouata, Chengjiang and the Burgess Shale. The Cambrian deposits retained a wider range of tissue types per genus. At Fezouata, soft cellular structures were more often associated with strengthened or mineralised parts.

The authors proposed that those more resistant structures could help protect softer tissue during fossil formation.

Calling all three deposits “exceptionally preserved” therefore does not make their biological inventories directly interchangeable.

The surviving anatomy is part of the evidence for an animal. It is also evidence about the process that selected which details survived.

Further work in 2024 examined variation within the Fezouata record itself. Different ecological categories were not preserved equally: predators and scavengers showed better preservation than some other feeding groups. The earlier of two studied Ordovician intervals also retained better preservation than the later one.

These patterns concern that dataset and region. They are not rules assigning every predator a better fossil record everywhere.

They show that even a famous fossil window has changing clarity.

The consequence for reading a diversity curve is practical. Before interpreting a rise or fall, researchers need to ask which deposits supplied the specimens, which structures could survive there and which organisms could be recognised from the remains.

Collecting and classification add further filters between the ancient community and the published count.

No single adjustment restores a perfect census. Independent records, repeated sampling and explicit preservation models can instead narrow the possible explanations.

This care does not erase the real losses described on the previous page. It helps establish which changes reflect biology and which may partly reflect access to the past.

With that distinction in mind, we can carry the Cambrian story across its formal boundary without imagining that every familiar animal stopped there.
