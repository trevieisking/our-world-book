### ACROSS THE LINE IN THE ROCKS

Where does the Cambrian end?

On a modern geological chart, a line separates it from the Ordovician. In the landscape, the formal reference for that division lies within a succession of marine rocks at Green Point in western Newfoundland.

The June 2026 international chart assigns the boundary an estimated age of **486.85 million years**, with an uncertainty of **1.5 million years**. In ordinary prose, that is about 487 million years ago.

The apparent precision of the printed number needs its uncertainty beside it.

The Cambrian began around 538.8 million years ago on the same chart, so the period spans roughly 52 million years. The communities explored in this chapter were distributed through a considerable interval, rather than gathered into one short-lived world.

There is also a distinction between dating the boundary and defining it.

The physical reference point at Green Point is an agreed level in the rocks, called a **Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point**, or GSSP. It provides a standard against which other successions can be compared.

The published definition identifies a level within Bed 23, associated with the local first appearance of the conodont *Iapetognathus fluctivagus*. Conodont remains supply important fossil markers for correlating ancient marine strata.

A first appearance in a reference section does not, by itself, identify the instant when that species evolved everywhere.

Correlations require evidence from other sections. They can draw on additional fossils and chemical changes, while accounting for gaps, preservation and differences between environments.

Improved dating can revise the numerical age assigned to a boundary. The agreed physical reference is a different part of the system.

Neither part declares that evolution stopped on one side and restarted on the other.

The Fezouata evidence discussed on the previous page already carries some Cambrian-associated lineages into the Ordovician. Continuity and change belong in the same reconstruction.

The following period also saw a substantial expansion of marine diversity, often called the **Great Ordovician Biodiversification Event**.

That long name can suggest one sudden occurrence. Research instead describes numerous diversifications that differed among regions, groups and habitats.

A 2018 review distinguished overlapping phases involving drifting organisms, communities on relatively level seabeds and reef communities. Their major expansions did not all begin and end together.

Different definitions of the event emphasise different portions of that history. A broad usage gathers the successive increases across the Ordovician; a narrower one highlights particular pulses.

We should therefore avoid drawing a second worldwide explosion at one exact moment immediately after the Cambrian boundary.

The chapter leaves an animal world whose relationships were still changing. Some branches disappeared. Others continued and diversified. Later communities inherited combinations of anatomy and behaviour while acquiring new ones.

Period names help us compare those histories. They do not turn evolution into a sequence of completed designs.

Our attention now shifts toward terrestrial habitats, while the marine story continues alongside them.

The next transition is not a claim that land had waited empty until the Ordovician. To understand life ashore, we must first ask what was already there.
