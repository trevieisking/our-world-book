### A WORLD OF RELATIONSHIPS

A fossil assemblage is an inventory.

A food web asks what the inventory was doing.

Who produced organic matter? Who collected suspended particles? Who consumed material on the bottom? Who ate whom?

A **food web** is the network of feeding relationships within an ecosystem. Its arrows describe transfers of matter and energy, rather than a ladder from supposedly inferior organisms to superior ones.

Microbes and algae remain fundamental in the Cambrian.

Animals do not replace that foundation. They add routes through which its products are gathered, moved and consumed.

A deposit feeder takes food from sediment. A suspension feeder captures particles from water. A predator consumes another animal. Decomposition returns material to chemical and microbial cycles.

Different activities connect habitats that might otherwise exchange less material.

How can those connections be reconstructed?

Anatomy can constrain possible diets. Gut contents may identify a particular meal. Damage can record an attack. Burrows reveal interaction with sediment. Comparisons with living organisms can help, provided the comparison is justified rather than imposed by superficial resemblance.

These forms of evidence have different strengths.

A preserved meal supports a feeding interaction directly, but it does not reveal every item the animal could eat. A plausible feeding structure supports a range of possibilities, not necessarily one exact prey species.

In 2008, researchers assembled food-web reconstructions for Chengjiang and the Burgess Shale and explicitly tested uncertainty in the inferred links. Many measured aspects of network organisation resembled those of modern food webs, although some differences remained.

The result supports substantial ecological complexity.

It does not make every arrow equally certain or prove that Cambrian ecosystems were identical to present ones.

The environment also sets limits.

A 2024 review of Cambrian marine evidence emphasised oxygen levels that were often low and variable compared with modern seas. Habitable conditions could coexist with nearby oxygen-poor water and recurring environmental stress.

A separate physiological argument links oxygen supply with feeding ecology: more energetically demanding activities, including active carnivory, can be constrained by oxygen availability. That offers a way for environmental conditions and predator-prey selection to interact.

It does not turn oxygen into an evolutionary instruction.

More oxygen cannot specify a particular shell, appendage or eye. It can change which activities are viable. Inherited variation, development and ecological competition determine what happens within those opportunities.

The feedback proposed across these pages is therefore a synthesis, not one measured sequence operating everywhere.

Burrowing alters habitat.

Hard parts change encounters.

Senses change detection.

Feeding moves matter.

Environmental conditions permit some combinations and restrict others.

As animals diversify, they modify the opportunities and dangers facing other organisms. The sea becomes increasingly structured by relationships as well as by its physical setting.

There is no single moment when the system becomes complete.

The next pages enter particular Cambrian communities and examine their inhabitants more closely. We will follow body structures and feeding equipment, asking how unfamiliar fossils fit into animal history.

The strange shapes are the beginning of the investigation.

Their place in the web is what makes them a world.
