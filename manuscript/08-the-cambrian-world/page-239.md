### SKELETONS THAT BUILD HABITAT

A skeleton can protect a body.

Many skeletons growing together can help construct an environment.

Early Cambrian reefs included **archaeocyaths**, extinct sponge-grade animals with porous, mineralised skeletal walls. Their skeletons contributed to frameworks alongside microbial growth and other reef components.

They were not the coral builders of a modern tropical reef. The comparison concerns what a framework does, rather than an identity of organisms.

As skeletal structures rise from the bottom, they create surfaces, spaces and obstacles to flow. A formerly simpler patch of seabed can acquire a more varied physical setting.

Calling an organism an **ecosystem engineer** means that its activities or body structures modify habitat in ways relevant to other organisms. It does not imply that it plans the result.

How can that role be investigated in a fossil reef?

The rocks preserve more than an isolated species list. Researchers can examine which skeletal parts grew together, how they were arranged and what sediment occupied the intervening spaces.

Three-dimensional reconstruction adds another level of evidence. A study published in 2023 reconstructed branching archaeocyaths from three localities on the ancient continent Laurentia. The shapes supported comparisons with other branching reef organisms and helped constrain interactions with water flow.

Growth architecture can reveal a physical capacity to influence the surroundings.

It does not automatically establish every biological interaction associated with a modern reef. For example, a shape compatible with hosting photosynthetic partners is not the same as preserved evidence of those partners inside the animal.

Feeding presents a related challenge.

The porous walls and central spaces invite questions about how water passed through the skeleton. Was surrounding current sufficient to deliver food, or did the animal actively move water?

A separate 2023 study tested digital skeletal models with computational fluid dynamics, a method for simulating fluid motion. Under the tested currents, little water circulated through the models by passive flow alone.

Comparison with living sponges supported an interpretation involving active suspension feeding.

That is a model-based conclusion. Its strength depends on the skeletal reconstruction and the tested conditions; the experiment does not recover fossil pumping cells or directly observe a living archaeocyath.

The distinction allows a useful hypothesis without disguising how it was obtained.

Reef development also depended on the surrounding sea.

A study of early Cambrian reefs on the Siberian platform found changes in their extent and community organisation associated with episodes of environmental change, including oxygenation. The reefs did not expand smoothly through one uninterrupted improvement in conditions.

This regional record connects habitat-building organisms with the limits imposed by their environment. It does not establish a single oxygen explanation for every reef or every evolutionary event.

Nor was every Cambrian community a reef community. The earlier reef records on this page must remain separate from the Burgess Shale assemblages we have just examined.

Together, the examples show two scales of animal-made opportunity: a surface for one attached neighbour, and a framework that alters a patch of habitat.

The chapter's next animal brings us back to the body itself, and to structures within a lineage that eventually includes us.
