### WHEN SEAS CROSSED THE LAND

The Cambrian sea extended beyond the edges we would draw around today's continents.

Water spread across parts of continental interiors, creating broad areas of shallow marine habitat. The animals we have examined occupied a world whose coastlines and seabeds were changing along with its communities.

A continent is a body of crust, not a guarantee of dry land.

If the land surface subsides, or sea level rises relative to it, seawater can advance inland. Geologists call that advance a **marine transgression**. It describes a changing position of the shoreline through time, rather than a single wave sweeping over a continent.

The surviving evidence can now stand far above the sea.

In the Grand Canyon region, Cambrian rock formations preserve deposits associated with sandy coastal settings, muddy seafloors and shallow carbonate-producing seas. The Tapeats Sandstone, Bright Angel Formation and Muav Formation record different parts of this environmental history.

Their present exposure is the outcome of later geological change. A canyon wall reveals ancient deposits; the Cambrian animals did not inhabit today's canyon.

The rocks also lie above much older formations along surfaces where time is missing from the preserved sequence. Such a break is an **unconformity**: erosion or non-deposition interrupts the record.

A contact between two rocks can therefore represent an interval much longer than the thickness of the contact suggests.

The widespread break called the Great Unconformity is important to this story, but its occurrence does not mean that erosion and marine flooding happened everywhere on one date.

How can the local sequence be dated?

A 2018 study used zircon grains carried into sediments above and below the relevant surfaces in the Grand Canyon region. Zircons can preserve the age at which they crystallised, while fossils supply additional constraints on the sediments that contain them.

The two forms of evidence answer related but different questions.

A grain can be older than the deposit into which erosion carried it. The youngest reliable grains help limit how old deposition could be; they do not automatically give an exact date for the arrival of seawater at every point.

The study revised the regional timing of the Sauk transgression, the large marine advance represented in these rocks.

It made the history more specific rather than turning it into a worldwide instant.

Broader work has proposed connections between continental erosion, flooding and changes in ocean chemistry. Those proposals are useful causal hypotheses, not proof that one geological event uniquely produced the Cambrian radiation.

For organisms, the changing geography matters even without a single master cause.

An advancing sea alters the distribution of shorelines, substrates and water depths. Different sedimentary environments provide different opportunities for attachment, burrowing and feeding. The same broad continental region can contain a mosaic of habitats rather than one uniform shallow-water community.

The fossil record samples pieces of that mosaic.

To understand what could live in each piece, we must also ask what the water supplied. A habitable depth and a suitable bottom are only part of the answer.

Next comes a substance essential to much animal activity, whose distribution was far from uniform: oxygen.
