### A RADIATION INTERRUPTED

Did Cambrian diversity keep growing once animals had acquired shells, limbs and varied diets?

The rocks preserve a less orderly history.

The **Sinsk event**, an early Cambrian extinction interval, sharply reduced many marine groups. It occurred within the Cambrian, long before the period ended. The radiation of animals therefore included losses and reorganisations alongside evolutionary innovation.

Reef-associated archaeocyath sponges were among the groups severely affected. Other inhabitants survived, but survival did not mean that the earlier community simply resumed unchanged.

To understand the difference, researchers ask two questions.

How many kinds of organism were present?

And how many different ways of living did they represent?

A 2025 study of skeletal animals from the Siberian Platform compared these measures across the radiation, extinction and aftermath. **Functional diversity** describes variation in ecological traits, such as feeding, movement and relationships with the bottom.

In the study, species richness fell sharply at the Sinsk event and later began to recover. The range of functional traits followed a different course, continuing to contract while species numbers increased.

The recovering assemblages also shifted toward different trait combinations, including mobile animals and groups with varied feeding modes or broader depth ranges.

More species did not automatically restore the earlier ecological pattern.

These results come from a particular regional dataset and from traits assigned to fossil groups. They cannot measure every activity performed in every Cambrian sea. They nevertheless show why counting names alone can miss a major change in community organisation.

What caused the losses?

Evidence for expanded oxygen-poor waters supplies a substantial part of the explanation. A more specific 2024 hypothesis connected the crisis to tectonic changes along Gondwana, the large southern continental assembly.

Fossils and sedimentary successions in Antarctica and Australia helped correlate changes along that margin. The authors proposed a cascade involving habitat loss, volcanism, greenhouse gases, climate change and incursions of anoxic water.

This is a causal hypothesis assembled from several records. Its timing depends on the precision of dates and correlations. Simultaneous-looking events are a starting point for testing a connection, rather than a guarantee of one.

The oxygen story also needs geographical detail.

A 2026 investigation of a South China succession reported geochemical evidence for persistent oxygenation in a shallow marine setting through the relevant interval. Such a result allows local refuges within a broader crisis; it argues against making every habitat anoxic at once.

Another 2026 discovery widens the picture of the aftermath.

The Huayuan biota, in Hunan, preserves a diverse community with exceptional soft tissues shortly after the Sinsk event. Comparisons with other deposits supported marine community reorganisation and suggested that outer-shelf environments could provide places of survival and subsequent diversification.

A rich post-extinction deposit does not undo the extinction. It reveals how uneven its consequences could be, and adds organisms that a record of shells alone would miss.

The Cambrian world was neither a steady climb nor a single disaster followed by a reset.

To follow its changing diversity, we must next examine the changing quality of the evidence itself.
