### A BRANCH THAT INCLUDES US

The temptation is easy to understand.

Find an early animal related to our own group, and call it the beginning of us.

The fossil record supports a more careful and more interesting account. A lineage can include our ancestry while also containing innumerable branches that did not lead to humans.

We belong to the chordates, a group that includes vertebrates as well as living relatives without vertebral columns. Chordate comparisons involve a suite of structures, including a dorsal nerve cord and a supporting rod called the **notochord**, present during at least part of the life cycle in living chordates.

These structures must be distinguished from one another when interpreting a flattened fossil. An elongated mark is not self-identifying as a nerve, gut or support.

The Burgess Shale animal *Pikaia gracilens* demonstrates the problem.

Its slender body preserves repeated muscle units and other longitudinal features. Those characters have long informed comparisons with chordates, but researchers have disagreed about how particular structures should be identified.

In 2024, a new analysis interpreted one structure as a dorsal nerve cord and another as a gut canal. The resulting anatomical model reversed the animal's previously accepted upper and lower surfaces.

The same fossil changed orientation without becoming a different specimen.

What changed was the tested explanation of its parts.

The revised study supported a position on the chordate stem and investigated how characteristic chordate structures could have been combined in early relatives. Its tree is a scientific hypothesis supported by a particular anatomical interpretation and comparative dataset.

It should guide reconstruction while remaining open to further testing.

It does not demonstrate that *Pikaia* was the direct ancestor of vertebrates, let alone of humans.

An earlier Cambrian record in China supplies different evidence.

*Haikouichthys*, known from the Chengjiang biota, is interpreted as an early vertebrate. Specimens preserve a differentiated head with eyes and body structures relevant to vertebrate comparison.

Some proposed sensory structures are less certain than the eyes. Describing possible nasal or ear-related capsules as established organs would give the reconstruction more confidence than the evidence allows.

Likewise, an early vertebrate need not possess the conspicuous mineralised backbone familiar from many later animals. The history of the group cannot be reduced to the first appearance of a modern-looking skeleton.

*Haikouichthys* and *Pikaia* belong to different deposits and intervals. Their positions should not be drawn as two observed steps in a single direct lineage. A comparative tree can place them in relation to one another without making them parent and descendant.

These fossils matter because they constrain the history of body organisation.

They offer evidence for structures, combinations and relationships. They do not reveal an evolutionary intention to produce a particular future animal.

Across this section, that principle has followed us from claws and mouthparts to attachment surfaces and skeletal frameworks. Unfamiliar bodies become understandable through careful comparison, while their lives remain tied to particular environments.

We now need to widen the view again.

What kinds of seas supported these communities? How did changing conditions open opportunities, impose stress and reorganise the living world through the rest of the Cambrian?
