### WHEN SEEING CHANGES THE CONTEST

A shell matters only after an encounter.

An eye can change whether the encounter happens.

Light-sensitive cells are not the same thing as image-forming vision. Detecting brightness can guide movement without revealing the outline of another animal. More elaborate optical arrangements can provide information about direction, movement and objects.

Those distinctions matter because a fossil eye preserves anatomy, not an ancient visual experience.

In the Emu Bay Shale of South Australia, exceptionally preserved compound eyes reveal arrays of closely packed lenses. A compound eye gathers information through many repeated optical units. Their arrangement gives researchers something measurable: lens size, spacing, number and the geometry of the visual surface.

One 2011 study described eyes with more than three thousand lenses and a region of enlarged lenses interpreted as an optical specialisation. Another described large paired eyes assigned to an *Anomalocaris*-like radiodont, with at least sixteen thousand lenses visible in a single eye.

Radiodonts were extinct relatives along the evolutionary stem leading toward modern arthropods, the group containing insects, crustaceans and spiders.

These eyes are roughly 515 million years old. They demonstrate that sophisticated compound vision existed during the early Cambrian; they do not identify the first eye in evolutionary history.

Later research distinguished different radiodont eye designs within the Emu Bay assemblage. Their differences support varied visual ecologies rather than one uniform way of seeing across the group.

What can such anatomy establish?

It can constrain optical performance and help test an ecological interpretation. It cannot show precisely what colours an animal perceived, how its nervous system processed an image or what one individual was looking at when it died.

A lens count is not a direct count of subjective clarity.

Nevertheless, detecting moving objects at a useful distance could alter the contest between predator and prey. A hunter might locate an exposed animal sooner. A prey animal might detect an approach earlier. Selection could then favour different combinations of concealment, movement, defence and sensory ability.

These are plausible ecological consequences, not a filmed account of Cambrian behaviour.

Diet requires its own evidence.

The familiar picture of *Anomalocaris* crushing trilobites has been tested rather than simply repeated. A 2023 biomechanical study modelled the frontal appendages of *Anomalocaris canadensis*. Their movement and mechanical performance were more consistent with grasping soft prey than crushing heavily mineralised animals.

The inference applies to the tested species and structures. Other radiodonts had different feeding equipment and may have occupied different niches.

This is a good example of the book's method. An impressive body shape suggests a hypothesis. Fossil anatomy, mechanical constraints and comparison with other evidence decide how well the hypothesis survives.

The Cambrian becomes less like a parade of monsters and more like a set of interacting ecological specialists.

But most eyes and feeding structures decay.

Our ability to investigate them depends on a few unusual deposits that preserve what ordinary fossilisation removes.

To understand the animals, we must also understand those deposits.
