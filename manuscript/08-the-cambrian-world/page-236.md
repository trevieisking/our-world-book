### A MEAL INSIDE A WORM

“Worm” describes a shape more readily than a relationship.

Long, soft bodies occur in several animal lineages. To place a fossil worm, we need structures more informative than its outline.

*Ottoia* supplies them.

Burgess Shale specimens preserve a ringed trunk and an anterior region armed with hooks, spines and teeth. Parts of that anterior equipment could be drawn inward and turned outward again. The animal is generally interpreted as a stem-group relative of living priapulid worms.

The rings along its body do not, by themselves, make it an annelid. Repeated surface markings need not represent the same internal organisation in different groups.

Detailed microscopy has also distinguished more than one species of *Ottoia*. Small differences in hard structures matter to identification, even when whole bodies initially look similar.

Those structures provide another route into the fossil record.

A study published in 2015 compared the hooks, spines and teeth of Burgess Shale bodies with small carbonaceous remains from other western Canadian rocks. Diagnostic elements extended the record of ottoiid priapulids beyond the exceptional whole-body deposit.

A fragment becomes more informative when its connection with an animal is tested.

The feeding story has a second, unusually direct source: material preserved inside the gut.

In 2012, Jean Vannier described gut contents of *Ottoia prolifica* that included remains of several kinds of small animals. Some specimens contain the conical skeletal parts of *Haplophrentis*, a hyolith. Others preserve brachiopod or arthropod remains.

This is stronger evidence of ingestion than a mouthpart that merely looks capable of catching something.

It still has limits.

An animal inside a gut has been consumed, but it need not have been killed by the consumer. Carrion can supply the same hard parts as living prey. Associations between *Ottoia* and arthropod carcasses support a scavenging interpretation, although they are preserved arrangements rather than observations of the event.

The combined evidence favours a dietary generalist, able to consume a range of available material.

It does not justify turning every specimen into an identical ambush predator with one preferred victim.

Preservation also filters the menu. Durable shells and plates can survive when soft food leaves little recognisable trace. A sample of gut contents is therefore evidence for included foods, not a complete catalogue of everything eaten.

We can now separate three questions that a vivid reconstruction might otherwise collapse.

What could the anterior structures do?

What material demonstrably entered the gut?

How was that material obtained?

Anatomy constrains the first. Preserved contents answer part of the second. The third may remain a choice among supported behavioural possibilities.

Keeping these questions apart makes the food web more useful. It gives a feeding link its own evidence grade rather than borrowing certainty from an impressive set of teeth.

The animal also reminds us that ecological flexibility is not a recent invention. Different food sources could already converge on one consumer in a Cambrian community.
