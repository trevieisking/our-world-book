# CHAPTER EIGHT — THE CAMBRIAN WORLD
## Page 227 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### ANIMALS REBUILD THE SEAFLOOR

For billions of years, microbial mats stabilised huge areas of shallow sediment.

Sticky microbial films bound grains.

Chemical gradients developed beneath them.

Ediacaran organisms often lived **on** these matgrounds.

Cambrian animals increasingly lived **in** the sediment.

That difference changed the physical planet.

A burrowing animal mixes grains.

It pumps water into sediment.

It carries oxygen downward.

It moves organic matter.

It destroys some microbial laminations.

It creates tubes and cavities.

It changes which organisms can live nearby.

This is **bioturbation**.

The broader transition from mat-dominated to increasingly churned substrates is often called the Cambrian Substrate Revolution.

It was not instantaneous.

A 2025 analysis of the Chapel Island Formation in Newfoundland assembled the largest high-resolution trace-fossil dataset yet used for this transition.

The rocks preserve about twenty million years from the latest Ediacaran through Cambrian Age 2.

The study measured more than fossil names.

Burrow depth.

Width.

Intensity.

Architectural diversity.

Vertical tiering.

Ecological space occupied.

The pattern unfolded in stages.

First:

an Ediacaran **matground ecology**.

Animals interact mostly with stable microbial surfaces.

Then:

a Fortunian **matground–firmground ecology**.

New behaviours penetrate substrates while microbial mats remain important.

Finally:

by the latest Fortunian and Cambrian Age 2, a more thoroughly mixed **mixground ecology** expands.

Burrowing becomes deeper and more diverse.

The researchers found another important pattern.

Behavioural innovation was not uniform across every environment.

Lower offshore settings appear to have acted as an early centre of diversification.

New behaviours later expanded into shallower environments.

Again, evolution and first local fossil appearance are not the same thing.

The ecological consequences extend beyond the burrower.

Microbial mats become less continuous.

Animals that depend on intact mats lose habitat.

Other organisms gain newly oxygenated pore space.

Suspension feeders benefit when sediment chemistry changes.

Deposit feeders access buried organic matter.

Burrows create refuges and attachment surfaces.

Predators follow prey into the substrate.

Sediment itself becomes a vertically partitioned ecosystem.

Bioturbation also changes geochemistry.

When oxygenated seawater is pumped downward, reduced compounds are oxidised.

Phosphorus can be retained or released differently.

Sulfur cycling changes.

Organic carbon that might have been buried can be exposed to respiration.

Animal behaviour begins affecting carbon, oxygen and nutrient fluxes.

A worm moving grains therefore becomes an **ecosystem engineer**.

No intention is required.

Physical activity changes habitat.

The same concept will recur throughout life's history.

Reefs alter water flow.

Roots break rock.

Forests create soils.

Beavers dam rivers.

Humans reshape landscapes.

The Cambrian gives us one of the first global-scale animal examples.

The sea floor is no longer merely a surface on which evolution happens.

Animals modify the substrate and thereby modify selection.

This creates feedback.

A deeper burrow escapes surface predators.

Predators evolve digging or probing behaviours.

Better sediment ventilation opens deeper habitats.

New food sources become available.

The environment created by one generation becomes the ecological starting point for the next.

That feedback helps explain why Cambrian diversification cannot be reduced to a list of new body plans.

The world animals inhabit is changing partly **because animals are inhabiting it**.

A second transformation makes that feedback much easier to fossilise.

Bodies begin carrying mineralised hard parts.

Tiny ones first.

Teeth.

Spines.

Tubes.

Plates.

Cones.

A rain of pieces geologists call the **small shelly fossils**.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Mángano et al. (2025/current publication), *Current Biology*: Chapel Island trace-fossil record supports staged transition from Ediacaran matground to Fortunian matground/firmground and later mixground ecology.
- Metrics include bioturbation intensity, burrow depth/width, tiering and ichnodiversity; lower offshore settings are interpreted as early centres of behavioural diversification.
- Bioturbation affects sediment oxygenation, organic-carbon remineralisation and nutrient/sulfur cycling.

**Decision:** Cambrian Substrate Revolution is presented as a staged ecological-engineering transition, not a synchronous global event.
