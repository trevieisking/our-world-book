### A NET INSTEAD OF A GRASP

What if a formidable-looking feeding appendage gathered a cloud of small animals rather than seizing one large victim?

The answer changes how we imagine a Cambrian food web.

Radiodonts, the group that includes *Anomalocaris*, did not all use the same feeding equipment. Their frontal appendages carried different arrangements of projections, and those differences offer clues to different ways of obtaining food.

One particularly revealing example comes from the early Cambrian Sirius Passet fossils of North Greenland.

The appendages of *Tamisiocaris borealis* have long, regularly arranged spines bearing dense rows of much finer spines. In a 2014 study, researchers interpreted this equipment as a collecting mesh.

They proposed **suspension feeding**: gathering food suspended in water.

A sweep through the water could bring many small particles into contact with the mesh. Its spacing suggested the capture of food roughly half a millimetre across and larger, including small drifting animals.

That size estimate comes from the structure of the appendage. It is not an identified meal preserved inside the animal.

Nor do these appendages give us an equally secure view of every part of its body. Feeding reconstructions must acknowledge what the specimens actually preserve.

The ecological implication is still substantial. A relatively large consumer could obtain nourishment by collecting numerous small food items, connecting different sizes of life within the water column.

Another radiodont points toward a different resource.

*Cambroraster falcatus*, described from the Burgess Shale in 2019, carried stout frontal appendages with rake-like projections. These could form a basket around the mouth. Its broad head shield, upward-facing eyes and compact body also support activity close to the seabed.

Researchers proposed that movements of the appendages disturbed sediment and collected organisms living on or within it.

The interpretation concerns sifting at the bottom, rather than a fine net sweeping through open water. Its equipment could process small inhabitants of the sediment, while allowing some larger food items too.

These animals are examples from different deposits and times. Putting their feeding strategies side by side does not put them together in one witnessed community.

It does reveal why a single label such as “predator” cannot describe the full ecological variety of radiodonts.

There is a further caution hidden in this research.

A 2018 description interpreted delicate structures associated with *Pahvantia* as an exceptionally fine filtering apparatus. In 2021, a re-examination proposed that much of this material belonged to the trunk and gills instead. The revised appendage reconstruction favoured a different feeding interpretation.

Before asking what an anatomical structure could do, researchers must establish which structure they are looking at.

Flattening, overlap and incomplete preservation make that identification difficult. Comparisons with better-preserved relatives can change it.

Such revisions strengthen the investigation by making its assumptions visible. A proposed feeding mechanism can be reconsidered without erasing the fossils that prompted it.

The Cambrian sea contained more possibilities than pursuit and escape. Animals collected, sifted and processed food in ways that connected water, sediment and other organisms.

But feeding an adult was only part of maintaining a population.

The next evidence concerns eggs carried beneath an animal's protective covering.
