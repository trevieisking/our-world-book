### TEETH WITHOUT A FAMILIAR SHELL

A mollusc need not wear a shell.

That is true of living animals, and it matters when investigating their early relatives. Searching only for a snail-like coil or a clam-like pair of valves would exclude important evidence before the comparison began.

The Burgess Shale animal *Odontogriphus* had a flattened, soft body. Renewed study of its specimens revealed rows of teeth in a feeding apparatus compared with a molluscan **radula**.

A radula is a tooth-bearing feeding structure. In living molluscs that possess one, its teeth can gather or process food; the details vary substantially among groups.

The fossil comparison concerns how teeth are arranged and supported, not just whether teeth exist.

In 2006, researchers interpreted *Odontogriphus* as an early molluscan relative. Later work compared its mouthparts with those of *Wiwaxia*, another Burgess Shale animal whose back carried overlapping hard elements and conspicuous spines.

The two animals looked markedly different from above.

Their feeding structures supplied evidence that the dorsal outlines could not.

A 2012 analysis supported a radula-like construction and interpreted both as grazing deposit feeders. That describes collecting food associated with the bottom, potentially including organic material on or in surface deposits. It does not identify one exclusive food species or prove that every meal was obtained in the same way.

*Wiwaxia* has attracted competing comparisons, including with annelid worms. Its dorsal elements, underside and mouthparts have not always seemed to tell the same simple story.

The useful response is to examine how the characters fit together, and whether apparent similarities reflect shared ancestry, similar function or preservation.

One feature can be informative without settling the entire animal's placement.

That distinction becomes especially clear in a recent case from China.

In 2024, *Shishania*, a spine-covered fossil from Cambrian rocks in Yunnan, was described as an early molluscan relative. Structures interpreted as a foot and associated soft anatomy helped support the proposal.

New material published in 2025 challenged it.

The later study offered preservation-based explanations for the supposed molluscan features and compared the animal instead with chancelloriids, an enigmatic group of sponge-like animals.

We therefore cannot use *Shishania* as an uncomplicated illustration of the molluscan ancestor. Its interpretation has changed, and the deeper relationships of chancelloriids remain a research problem.

This example comes from a different deposit and interval from the Burgess Shale animals above. Comparison is legitimate; placing them together in one reconstructed community would be misleading.

Revision does not make the fossils less valuable.

It improves the question asked of them. A supposed foot must be tested as a structure, rather than accepted because it completes an attractive evolutionary story. Additional specimens can reveal whether an apparent boundary belongs to the animal or to what happened during burial.

Across these examples, early molluscan history emerges from combinations of evidence rather than a parade of increasingly modern shells.

And a hard element can tell us something beyond the body that produced it.
