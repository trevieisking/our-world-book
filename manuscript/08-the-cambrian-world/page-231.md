### THE ROCKS THAT KEEP SOFT BODIES

Imagine reconstructing a modern sea using only the parts that resist decay.

Shells would remain prominent. Many worms, delicate feeding structures and internal organs would disappear. The resulting inventory would tell us something true about that sea, but it would give a badly distorted impression of its inhabitants.

The Cambrian record has the same problem.

Its most revealing exceptions are deposits called **Lagerstätten**, a term used for fossil accumulations with exceptional abundance or preservation. Here the important kind preserves unusual anatomical detail, including the remains of soft-bodied animals.

Chengjiang in China and the Burgess Shale in Canada are famous examples.

They are not simultaneous snapshots.

Chengjiang records an early Cambrian community around 518 million years ago. The Burgess Shale records younger, middle Cambrian communities. Neither is a photograph of the entire planet, and each contains multiple beds whose fossils must be placed in their own geological context.

Why did soft structures survive there?

Death normally begins a competition between destruction and preservation. Scavengers, microbes, chemical reactions and physical disturbance can remove a body before sediment captures its form.

Rapid burial helps, but it is not a complete explanation.

Microbes can continue decomposing an animal after it is buried. Oxygen-free sediment is not automatically free of decay: organisms can use other chemical pathways. The chemistry and permeability of the burial environment therefore matter as much as the speed of entombment.

A sedimentological and geochemical study published in 2012 proposed that Burgess Shale-type preservation was promoted by restricted access to oxidants, substances used in decomposition reactions. Fine sediment and early carbonate cement could limit replenishment from the water above, helping organic remains persist as compressed carbon-rich films.

This is a supported preservation model, not a claim that every tissue at every site followed exactly the same pathway.

Local mineralisation and later alteration can also affect what we see.

The environment in which animals lived may differ from the place in which their remains were finally buried. Currents can transport bodies. A bed can combine material from more than one habitat or episode. Before drawing a community, we must ask how that community entered the rock.

Research on an unweathered Chengjiang core illustrates the importance of that question. A 2022 study interpreted the fossil-bearing succession as a delta influenced by river floods and storms, with fluctuations in salinity and sediment supply.

That interpretation challenges the habit of depicting all exceptionally preserved Cambrian communities as tranquil offshore settings.

The scientific reconstruction becomes more particular.

Which deposit?

Which bed?

Which animals are actually recorded together?

Which details survived, and which are inferred?

We can compare separate deposits to look for wider patterns. We cannot merge every famous Cambrian animal into one timeless scene and call it an ecosystem.

Exceptional preservation enlarges the investigation.

It does not remove the need for evidence.

Once soft anatomy becomes visible, however, the fossil record offers something richer than a list of bodies.

It begins to reveal their relationships.
