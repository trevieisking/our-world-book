### THE SMALL SHELLY WORLD

Before trilobites dominate museum displays, the Cambrian fossil record becomes crowded with smaller things.

Millimetre-scale tubes.

Caps.

Cones.

Spines.

Plates.

Hooks.

Tooth-like elements.

They are collectively called **small shelly fossils**, or SSFs.

The name is convenient.

It is not a biological group.

Some are whole tiny animals.

Some are pieces of larger animals.

Some are feeding structures.

Some are armour plates called sclerites.

Some belong to early molluscs, brachiopods, cnidarians or extinct lineages.

Some remain difficult to classify.

What unites many of them is **biomineralisation**.

Different lineages incorporate calcium carbonate, calcium phosphate or silica into durable structures.

Mineralised skeletons had Ediacaran precedents.

*Cloudina* and other terminal Ediacaran organisms already built hard tubes.

The Cambrian difference is scale and repetition.

Mineralised parts evolve across many unrelated lineages.

Skeletonisation becomes an ecological strategy.

Why?

There is no single reason.

A shell protects.

A spine deters.

A hard feeding element cuts or scrapes.

A rigid support allows a body to push against sediment.

Mineral skeletons can support larger or stiffer bodies.

They also provide surfaces for muscles to pull against.

Changing seawater chemistry can alter the energetic cost of precipitation.

Predation can make defence more valuable.

The expansion of burrowing can make abrasion resistance useful.

Several pressures can favour mineralisation independently.

The fossil record exaggerates the transition.

Hard parts preserve far more readily than soft tissues.

A lineage may exist invisibly before one descendant evolves mineralised structures.

Early Cambrian phosphatic preservation also creates a special **taphonomic window**.

Tiny shells and even microscopic anatomical details can be replicated in phosphate.

This is one reason small shelly assemblages are so rich.

But preservation depends on sedimentary environment.

A 2025 study of more than **35,000** Cambrian small shelly fossils from North China showed that preservation quality and abundance differ strongly among microfacies.

The record is not a neutral census.

Rock type filters it.

This is important when diversity appears to rise or fall.

A change can reflect biology.

Preservation.

Or both.

Even with those biases, the Cambrian skeletonisation signal is real.

Different lineages independently experiment with hard materials.

Some construct external shells.

Others assemble armour from many separate sclerites.

Others mineralise feeding apparatuses.

This is evolutionary convergence driven through different developmental programmes.

A hard part also changes the ecology around it.

It can be attacked.

Broken.

Drilled.

Repaired.

A scar preserves an interaction.

Suddenly, predator and prey leave evidence on the **same fossil**.

That makes a new kind of evolutionary history possible.

Instead of inferring predation only because one animal looks dangerous, we can compare defensive structures and damage through a stratigraphic sequence.

In South Australia, one early Cambrian animal preserved exactly that record.

Its armour got thicker.

The predators kept drilling.

The resulting study provides an unusually early population-level record consistent with an evolutionary arms race.

The predator's identity and its own adaptive changes are less directly constrained.

## Research / fact-check notes
- SSFs are a preservational/category term, not one clade; many record the early Cambrian expansion of biomineralisation.
- Mineralised skeletons have Ediacaran antecedents; Cambrian radiation greatly expands their abundance/diversity.
- Hu et al. (2025), *Geoscience Frontiers*: analysis of 35,161 SSF specimens shows strong microfacies/taphonomic controls on preservation and challenges overly simple “phosphatization window” interpretations.
- Mineralisation has multiple possible functions and cannot be attributed uniquely to predation.

**Decision:** treat Cambrian skeletonisation as repeated evolutionary innovation whose apparent fossil abruptness is amplified, not created, by preservational bias.
