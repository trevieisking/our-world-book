### ARMOUR WITH A HISTORY

A shell with a hole in it is a small piece of evidence.

A sequence of shells in which both the damage and the armour change is something more: a possible record of selection at work.

In lower Cambrian rocks of South Australia, researchers examined the mineralised plates of *Lapworthella fasciculata*, an extinct animal known from small skeletal elements called sclerites. The animal's complete appearance remains incompletely resolved. Its armour survives more readily than its body.

A study published in 2025 identified more than two hundred perforated sclerites. Across the sampled rock succession, perforations became more frequent, while the skeletal walls also became thicker. The authors interpreted the relationship as an adaptive response to predation and evidence of an early evolutionary arms race.

The interaction took place around 517 million years ago.

The attacker remains unidentified.

That last sentence prevents the reconstruction from becoming more complete than the evidence. We have the damaged skeletal elements and their changes through the succession. We do not have a securely identified predator whose anatomy can be followed through the same sequence. A reciprocal improvement in its ability to penetrate armour is an inference, rather than a directly measured history of its feeding apparatus.

Why would thicker armour become common?

Suppose a population already contains inherited variation in skeletal thickness. If some variants survive attacks more often and leave more offspring, their contribution to later generations can increase. Selection does not require an animal to understand the danger or decide to improve its shell.

Nor does it require every attack to fail.

A defence can be advantageous while predators continue killing defended prey. What matters is a difference in reproductive success, compared with the alternatives available in that population.

There are costs, too. A skeleton uses materials and energy. An economical defence in one environment may become burdensome in another. Evolutionary escalation therefore need not proceed endlessly toward heavier armour. It can favour hiding, burrowing, faster escape, a different habitat or a different life cycle.

These possibilities explain why “arms race” is a useful model rather than a universal law.

The fossil test must also distinguish predatory damage from breakage after death. Abrasion, dissolution and crushing can alter skeletal remains. The strength of an argument comes from the form and distribution of damage, its relationship to anatomy, and its pattern through independently ordered strata.

A dramatic-looking hole alone is insufficient.

The *Lapworthella* study is valuable because it connects repeated injuries with a changing defensive structure. It supports ecological escalation in one documented population-level record. It does not establish predation as the sole cause of the Cambrian radiation.

Predation also predates this particular succession.

The broader lesson is that another organism can become part of an animal's selective environment. Rock, water and chemistry still matter. Increasingly, so do bodies capable of finding, grasping or piercing other bodies.

Finding them introduces another question.

What could Cambrian animals see?

- AMNH's study account supplies the approximate 517 Ma context and explicitly leaves the predator unidentified.
- Population-level adaptive response is STRONGLY SUPPORTED; reciprocal predator adaptation is an inference.
- General selection examples are explanatory scenarios, not observations of the fossil animals.
- Earlier predation is acknowledged; no universal “first predator” or single-trigger claim is made.
