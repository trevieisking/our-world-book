### A HABITAT THAT COULD CLOSE

A shallow sea could offer space, food and a suitable bottom while still restricting animal life.

The missing condition might be enough oxygen.

Oxygen supports the energy-releasing reactions used by most animals. Its availability depends on both supply and consumption: exchange with the atmosphere, photosynthetic production, circulation and the breakdown of organic matter all matter.

Those processes operate together. Productive surface waters can generate organic material whose later decomposition consumes oxygen elsewhere.

The result need not be a uniformly well-oxygenated sea.

Water near the surface and water close to the bottom can differ. So can neighbouring basins, and the same basin at different times. **Anoxia** means oxygen is absent; low oxygen and complete absence are different conditions.

Neither condition means that every form of life disappears. Microbial processes can continue where oxygen-dependent animals face severe restrictions.

The challenge is reconstructing a distribution that no instrument measured while the Cambrian animals were alive.

Researchers use **proxies**, surviving observations that can be related to an environmental condition. The chemical forms of iron and the behaviour of trace metals can help constrain conditions around ancient sediments. Isotope records can support broader reconstructions, provided the connection between seawater, burial and the preserved rock is tested.

A proxy is evidence interpreted through a mechanism.

It is not a fossil oxygen meter with one unquestionable reading.

In 2019, a study combined uranium-isotope records from Cambrian carbonates in several regions with a model of linked chemical cycles. The results supported a fluctuating atmosphere–ocean system, including substantial expansions of oxygen-poor seafloor.

That model helps investigate broad change. It does not specify the oxygen available to every animal in every fossil bed.

A different 2019 investigation exposed why the scale of sampling matters.

Fine scanning of an Alum Shale succession identified brief oxygenated intervals within a longer record dominated by anoxic conditions. Some thin intervals also contained fossil shells. The association supported an interpretation in which animals occupied the bottom when conditions permitted.

Coarser sampling could have combined those intervals into an apparently contradictory result: animal fossils within a record described as persistently anoxic.

The contradiction can therefore concern resolution, rather than animals somehow living without the conditions they required.

This local example does not solve every fossil–chemistry disagreement. Transport after death, chemical alteration and ecological interpretation still need examination in other deposits.

It does show how a habitat could repeatedly open and close.

A 2024 review of Cambrian marine evidence similarly emphasised oxygen levels that were low and variable, with viable ecosystems vulnerable to environmental stress.

The consequences depended on physiology and behaviour. Different organisms require different supplies, and energetic activities place different demands on them. A change tolerable to one inhabitant may restrict another.

That makes oxygen part of the explanation for ecological opportunity and loss, alongside food, temperature, habitat and evolutionary history.

It supplies no instructions for constructing a claw or a shell.
