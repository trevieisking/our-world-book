### BEFORE THE FORESTS

What would “life ashore” mean before trees, leaves and walking vertebrates?

It could mean something small enough to disappear beneath the unaided eye.

Continental environments had a biological history long before the Cambrian animal communities explored here. The next chapter therefore cannot begin with an empty land surface suddenly receiving all its inhabitants from the sea.

Some direct evidence takes us much further back.

In 2011, researchers described diverse microfossils from the Torridonian rocks of northwest Scotland, roughly a billion years old. Organic walls and structures preserved in phosphatic nodules revealed complex cells, cysts and some multicellular arrangements.

The deposits record non-marine environments. The authors interpreted the fossils as evidence of eukaryotes in freshwater and habitats exposed to air at least at times.

Eukaryotes are organisms with cells organised around a nucleus, the broad group that includes animals, plants, fungi and many microscopic forms.

These fossils are evidence for life within continental landscapes. They are not a billion-year-old forest.

Nor does living in a lake establish the same way of life as growing permanently on a dry surface. Water bodies, wet sediment and intermittently exposed margins offer different conditions.

A 2015 review assembled evidence for terrestrial biotas before land plants. It distinguished fossil cells and biological structures from chemical clues to microbial activity, stressing the sparse record and uncertain identities of many organisms.

That distinction matters when reconstructing ground cover. Evidence for local communities does not establish one continuous carpet over every continent.

By the Cambrian, microscopic remains provide further clues to the transition toward the plant world.

Some deposits contain resistant spore-like bodies commonly discussed as **cryptospores**. Their walls and arrangements can preserve information about how the producing organisms developed and reproduced.

But the producer is often missing.

Recognising a durable reproductive structure is not equivalent to recovering the whole organism, with its growth form, tissues and habitat intact.

A 2021 study described early Ordovician microfossils from Western Australia with features linking older Cambrian forms and younger spores associated with land plants. The authors proposed that the record could track stages in the acquisition of plant developmental traits.

They did not establish every Cambrian spore-like body as the product of a fully formed land plant. The older forms lack some diagnostic features of later plant spores, and their relationships remain debated.

A 2026 review makes the terminology itself explicit: a broad definition of cryptospores includes forms proposed to come from algae, while a narrower definition restricts the term to land-plant spores.

The name alone cannot settle the organism's identity.

This is the threshold at which we leave the Cambrian chapter. We have followed bodies, feeding structures, habitats and losses in the seas. Beyond them lies another investigation, already under way in microscopic communities.

Chapter 9 will follow separate histories of plants, fungi and animals as terrestrial ecosystems developed. It will ask how evidence establishes residence outside water, how organisms reproduced there and how increasingly complex habitats formed.

The question is no longer simply when life appeared on land.

It is which life, in which environment, with which evidence.
