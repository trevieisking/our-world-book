# CHAPTER THREE — THE WATER PLANET

### IRON: ABUNDANT UNTIL OXYGEN CHANGED THE RULES

Modern surface seawater contains very little dissolved iron.

Early seawater could contain much more.

The reason is redox chemistry.

Under anoxic conditions, iron can remain dissolved as **Fe²⁺** far more readily than it can in today's oxygenated surface ocean.

Hydrothermal systems supplied iron from hot rock.

Weathering of iron-bearing crust supplied more.

With little free oxygen available to oxidise Fe²⁺ into poorly soluble Fe³⁺ minerals, reduced iron could accumulate in seawater.

Estimates vary strongly with place and time, but reconstructions of Archean oceans commonly allow dissolved Fe²⁺ concentrations far above modern values.

That iron did not remain dissolved forever.

Change the chemistry and it precipitates.

Oxidation can convert Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺.

Ferric iron forms insoluble oxyhydroxides and oxides.

Silica can interact with those precipitates.

Sulfide can remove iron into minerals such as pyrite.

This is why iron-rich chemical sediments become such powerful archives later in the Archean.

**Banded iron formations**, or BIFs, contain repeated iron-rich and silica-rich layers deposited from ancient seawater.

They do not record one simple mechanism.

Iron oxidation could be driven by oxygen in some settings, by anoxygenic photosynthetic microbes in others, or by abiotic photochemical processes under particular conditions.

The dominant mechanisms changed through time.

For this chapter, the important point comes before biology.

An anoxic ocean can carry a much larger dissolved iron inventory than today's ocean.

That changes water colour, mineral precipitation, nutrient availability, redox chemistry and the kinds of surfaces present in sediments.

Iron is therefore more than an ingredient.

It is a chemical switch.

As Earth's oxidation state changed, the ocean's ability to keep iron dissolved changed with it.
