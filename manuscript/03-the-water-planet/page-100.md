# CHAPTER THREE — THE WATER PLANET
## Page 100 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### THE OCEAN IS NOT ONE PLACE

Stand on a modern beach and say the word **ocean**.

It sounds singular.

Geologically, it is thousands of environments connected by moving water.

Deep basin.

Continental shelf.

Volcanic island.

Tidal flat.

Estuary.

Lagoon.

River mouth.

Hydrothermal field.

Sediment pore.

Open surface water.

Dark abyss.

Each has its own pressure, temperature, chemistry, light, flow and relationship with rock.

Early Earth multiplied that diversity with a closer Moon, faster rotation, hotter crust, higher heat flow, uncertain continental geography and an anoxic atmosphere.

The result would not have been one giant chemical soup mixed to uniformity.

It would have been a network of connected environments.

The Moon forced tides.

But basin geometry determined how large they became.

Rain and rivers moved material toward the sea.

But exposed land may have been scarce and temporary.

The open ocean transported chemicals globally.

But shallow and confined environments could concentrate them.

Hydrothermal circulation supplied heat and reduced compounds.

But the chemical outcome depended on what those fluids mixed with.

Sediments trapped material.

Minerals sorted it.

Evaporation concentrated it.

Water diluted it again.

This is where the history of a planet begins to approach the history of chemistry.

Not because the ocean itself is alive.

Not because any one shoreline or vent has been proved to be life's birthplace.

But because Earth now possesses the ingredients for chemical complexity:

**liquid solvent, energy flow, mineral surfaces, gradients, cycles, transport and time.**

Before asking how life began, however, we still need one more piece.

What exactly was dissolved in these oceans?

Carbon.

Nitrogen.

Phosphorus.

Sulfur.

Iron.

Silica.

Metals.

Organic molecules from atmospheric chemistry, impacts and geological reactions.

Some were abundant.

Some were scarce.

Some were chemically inaccessible.

Some were trapped in minerals.

Some changed oxidation state.

The next part of this chapter follows those ingredients.

Because a water planet can contain an element without making it chemically available.

And availability may matter more than abundance.
