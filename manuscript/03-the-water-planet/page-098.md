# CHAPTER THREE — THE WATER PLANET
## Page 098 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### WHERE TWO CHEMISTRIES MEET

A chemical gradient is a difference across space.

Temperature can form a gradient.

Acidity can form a gradient.

Salt concentration can form a gradient.

Oxidation state can form a gradient.

Hydrothermal environments generate several at once.

Water circulating through hot rock can emerge carrying hydrogen, sulfide, dissolved metals and other reduced compounds.

The surrounding ocean can have a different temperature, composition and redox state.

When the two fluids meet, the system is out of equilibrium.

That matters because chemical reactions can extract energy from differences.

On modern Earth, oxygen is an important oxidant in many vent ecosystems.

Early Earth was different.

The Hadean and early Archean oceans were essentially anoxic on a global scale.

So early hydrothermal gradients should not be illustrated as a modern oxygen-rich vent with the date changed.

Other redox couples mattered.

Hydrogen.

Carbon dioxide.

Reduced iron.

Sulfur compounds.

Mineral surfaces.

Possibly nitrate or locally generated oxidants in limited settings, depending on time and mechanism.

Alkaline hydrothermal models add another kind of gradient.

Water–rock reactions such as serpentinization can produce alkaline, hydrogen-rich fluids.

If surrounding seawater was more acidic because of high atmospheric carbon dioxide, mixing could create steep pH gradients across mineral precipitates and pores.

That is one reason alkaline-vent models have attracted attention in origin-of-life research.

But the exact pH of Hadean seawater is uncertain.

The exact prevalence of alkaline vent systems is uncertain.

And a gradient capable of driving reactions is not equivalent to a demonstrated pathway to life.

The secure point is physical:

**early Earth contained environments where fluids with different temperatures, compositions and redox states met continuously.**

Those boundaries were active.

Minerals precipitated.

Rock altered.

Heat flowed.

Electrons could move through chemical reactions.

The ocean was full of interfaces where equilibrium was being prevented.

Life later became extraordinarily good at exploiting such conditions.

The gradients came first.
