# CHAPTER THREE — THE WATER PLANET
## Page 111 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### ORGANIC MOLECULES ARE NOT ALIVE

By this point, the young Earth could plausibly contain:

amino acids,

simple lipids,

sugars and sugar-related compounds,

nitrogen-bearing organics,

reduced carbon compounds,

and other small molecules.

That is impressive.

It is still nowhere near a cell.

Life requires functions that must work together.

Some form of boundary or compartment must keep useful chemistry from simply dispersing.

Energy must continuously drive reactions away from equilibrium.

Information must be stored or reproduced.

Replication must occur with enough fidelity to preserve useful structure, but enough variation to permit evolution.

Catalysis must accelerate reactions that would otherwise be too slow.

And the whole system has to persist while making more systems like itself.

Modern life solves these problems with a bewildering network of membranes, proteins, RNA, DNA, ion gradients and molecular machines.

The first living systems were almost certainly simpler.

The difficulty is explaining how the pieces became integrated.

Self-replicating molecules exist in laboratory systems.

Fatty-acid vesicles can form compartments.

RNA can both store information and catalyse some reactions.

Mineral surfaces can concentrate molecules.

Metabolic reaction networks can be driven by geochemical energy.

But these achievements usually solve **parts** of the problem.

A 2020 review of synthetic replicator systems identified the core challenge as integrating replication, metabolism and compartmentalisation into an out-of-equilibrium system capable of open-ended Darwinian evolution.

That challenge remains.

Even sophisticated modern synthetic-cell experiments have not recreated a fully autonomous prebiotic cell assembling itself from simple geochemical ingredients without external intervention.

So the sentence:

**"Scientists have made the building blocks of life"**

can be true.

The sentence:

**"Scientists have recreated the origin of life"**

is not.

The distance between those statements is the subject of the next chapter.
