# CHAPTER THREE — THE WATER PLANET
## Page 093 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### THE MOON WAS CLOSER, AND THE DAYS WERE SHORTER

The Moon we see today is not where the Moon began.

Tidal interactions have been transferring angular momentum from Earth's rotation into the Moon's orbit for billions of years.

Earth has slowed.

The Moon has moved outward.

That basic relationship is well established.

The precise early history is not.

The tide-generating effect of the Moon becomes much stronger as distance decreases. To first order, the lunar tidal forcing scales approximately with the inverse cube of the Earth–Moon distance.

Closer Moon.

Stronger astronomical forcing.

Faster-spinning Earth.

Shorter day.

Those statements are safe.

A single exact Hadean tide height is not.

Modern lunar laser ranging measures the Moon receding from Earth today, but we cannot simply run that modern rate backward in a straight line for 4.5 billion years. Ocean-basin geometry, continental configuration, seafloor depth, friction, resonances and Earth's rotation rate all change tidal dissipation.

Geological constraints improve as we move toward younger rocks.

Tidal rhythmites — layered sediments deposited under periodically varying tidal currents — can preserve the number of tidal cycles in a month or year and therefore constrain past Earth–Moon dynamics.

But the oldest proposed Archean day-length estimates are difficult.

A widely discussed estimate from roughly 3.2-billion-year-old rocks in South Africa is considered too ambiguous for many modern compilations.

By younger Precambrian time, the record becomes more useful.

This matters because it stops us from printing a dramatic but unjustified number such as:

**THE HADEAN DAY WAS EXACTLY 12 HOURS LONG.**

Models permit very short early days.

The rocks do not currently give us one universally accepted Hadean stopwatch.

What the physics does tell us is enough:

the early Earth–Moon system was more compact,

Earth rotated faster,

and tides operated under a different astronomical forcing than they do today.

That changed the rhythm of coastlines long before any animal watched them.
