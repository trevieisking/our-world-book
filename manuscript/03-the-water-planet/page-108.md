# CHAPTER THREE — THE WATER PLANET
## Page 108 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### ABUNDANCE IS NOT AVAILABILITY

By now, early Earth has all the famous elements.

Carbon.

Nitrogen.

Phosphorus.

Sulfur.

Iron.

Silicon.

Metals.

Hydrogen.

Oxygen bound inside water and minerals.

But a list of elements tells us very little about what chemistry could actually occur.

Nitrogen can be abundant as N₂ and still be difficult to fix.

Phosphorus can be abundant in rock and scarce in solution.

Iron can be soluble under anoxia and disappear when oxidised.

Sulfur can shift between sulfide, elemental sulfur and sulfate.

Silica can remain dissolved until saturation or nucleation triggers precipitation.

Carbon can be abundant as CO₂ while reduced organic carbon remains scarce.

This is the difference between **inventory** and **availability**.

Chemistry cares about form.

Charge.

Solubility.

Oxidation state.

Concentration.

Temperature.

Pressure.

Catalytic surfaces.

Competing reactions.

The ocean therefore created both opportunities and bottlenecks.

A hydrothermal fluid might supply abundant hydrogen but little phosphate.

A shallow evaporative pool might concentrate phosphate but lose volatile molecules.

An open ocean might supply stable conditions but dilute rare reactants.

A mineral surface might concentrate one molecule and destroy another.

A shoreline might provide wet–dry cycling but expose compounds to ultraviolet radiation.

There is no universally perfect environment.

That fact will become central in the next chapter.

Before life existed, Earth did not need one miraculous substance.

It needed sequences of reactions that could occur under physically realistic conditions, connect to one another, and persist long enough for increasingly complex chemistry to emerge.

This chapter has established the stage.

A persistent ocean.

An atmosphere.

Exposed land.

Hydrothermal circulation.

Tides.

Sediments.

Minerals.

Energy gradients.

Carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, iron and silica in changing chemical forms.

Now we can ask the question the entire journey has been approaching:

**When does chemistry become biology?**

Not yet.

There are four pages left in this chapter.

First we need to examine the organic molecules already available to the young Earth — made here, delivered from space, or produced by impacts — and establish exactly what those molecules prove.

And what they do not.
