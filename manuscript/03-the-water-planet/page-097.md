# CHAPTER THREE — THE WATER PLANET
## Page 097 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### THE OPEN OCEAN HAS A CHEMICAL PROBLEM: IT IS VERY BIG

Oceans are excellent at mixing.

That can be useful.

It can also be inconvenient.

Imagine adding one drop of dye to a glass.

Now add the same drop to a swimming pool.

Now add it to an ocean.

The molecule still exists.

Its concentration does not.

This simple fact matters whenever chemistry depends on reactants meeting often enough to react.

The open ocean can supply enormous volumes of water and dissolved material, but it can also dilute scarce compounds.

Other environments do the opposite.

Evaporation removes water and leaves dissolved substances behind.

Pore spaces between sediment grains restrict volume.

Mineral surfaces can adsorb ions and organic molecules.

Freezing can exclude solutes from growing ice and concentrate them in the remaining liquid.

Tidal pools can repeatedly fill and partially dry.

Shallow ponds can experience strong evaporation.

Hydrothermal pores can maintain continuous fluid flow through confined spaces.

Sediment can trap particles that would otherwise remain dispersed.

None of these concentration mechanisms proves where life began.

That is a later question.

They establish a physical principle:

**different aquatic environments process the same chemical inventory differently.**

An open ocean favours dilution and large-scale transport.

A shoreline can provide cycling.

A pore network can provide confinement.

An evaporative surface can provide concentration.

A mineral surface can provide adsorption and catalysis.

A river mouth can mix chemically distinct waters.

A hydrothermal vent can supply sustained gradients.

This is why "the ocean" should never be treated as one chemical beaker.

It contains thousands of microenvironments.

Some flush material away.

Some trap it.

Some dilute.

Some concentrate.

Some keep temperature stable.

Some oscillate rapidly.

The physical geography of water determines what chemistry gets repeated.

And repeated chemistry is much more important than one lucky reaction.
