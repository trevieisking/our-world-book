### SILICA: AN OCEAN WITH MORE DISSOLVED ROCK

Silicon is one of the most abundant elements in Earth's crust.

In modern oceans, dissolved silica is strongly affected by biology.

Diatoms, radiolarians and other organisms extract silica from seawater to build hard parts.

The early Earth had no such biological sink.

At the same time, high heat flow, vigorous hydrothermal circulation and rapid weathering of volcanic crust supplied dissolved silica.

As a result, Archean seawater is widely thought to have contained much more dissolved silica than modern seawater.

Some reconstructions place concentrations near saturation with amorphous silica or cristobalite, roughly in the millimolar range.

Again, these are model- and proxy-dependent values.

The qualitative contrast is more secure than one exact number.

High silica matters because silica interacts with almost everything else in this chapter.

It can precipitate directly as silica-rich sediment.

It can coat mineral surfaces.

It can influence iron precipitation.

It can help preserve delicate sedimentary textures.

It can alter the porosity and permeability of crust.

And over geological time it becomes **chert** — hard, silica-rich rock that preserves some of Earth's oldest sedimentary and biological evidence.

In iron-rich seas, silica and iron often became linked.

Banded iron formations contain both.

Experimental work suggests that high dissolved silica can affect how ferric iron precipitates, producing mixed iron–silica gels or altering particle growth.

So an early ocean rich in Fe²⁺ and dissolved silica did not behave like modern seawater with the oxygen removed.

It was chemically different in multiple coupled ways.

This is another reason modern seawater is a poor default model for the Archean.

Remove oxygen.

Raise iron.

Raise silica.

Change sulfate.

Change phosphorus chemistry.

Change crust.

Change heat flow.

And suddenly the same H₂O becomes a very different ocean.
