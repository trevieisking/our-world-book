# CHAPTER THREE — THE WATER PLANET
## Page 089 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### THE OCEAN AND THE AIR START NEGOTIATING

Once Earth had persistent liquid water, the atmosphere could no longer evolve on its own.

Carbon dioxide dissolves in water.

Rainwater reacts with rock.

Dissolved ions are carried toward lakes and oceans.

Carbon can become stored in dissolved bicarbonate and, under suitable conditions, carbonate minerals.

Volcanism returns carbon to the atmosphere.

This exchange is the beginning of a long-term planetary feedback.

On modern Earth, the **carbonate–silicate cycle** helps stabilise climate over geological timescales.

Warm, wet conditions generally accelerate silicate weathering.

Faster weathering removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere more rapidly.

Less carbon dioxide weakens greenhouse warming.

Cooling then slows weathering.

The feedback is not a thermostat with a number printed on it.

It is slower, messier and strongly affected by biology, tectonics, exposed land area and ocean chemistry.

On early Earth, those conditions were very different.

There may have been much less exposed continental crust.

Basaltic rocks were more widespread at the surface.

Heat flow was greater.

Seafloor alteration may have removed substantial carbon dioxide without requiring modern continents.

The balance between volcanic carbon input, continental weathering and seafloor weathering therefore changed through time.

This is important for the faint young Sun problem.

A stable liquid ocean requires the climate system to avoid both permanent global freezing and a runaway hot state.

Carbon cycling offers a mechanism for long-term adjustment.

But we should not pretend the early carbon cycle worked exactly as the modern one does.

The magnitude of seafloor weathering is debated.

The timing of modern-style plate tectonics is debated.

The area of exposed land is debated.

And biology later transforms weathering rates dramatically.

The secure conclusion is more modest:

**once atmosphere, water and silicate rock were exchanging material, climate and geology became chemically coupled.**

Earth's temperature was no longer only a matter of sunlight.

The planet had started regulating itself through feedbacks.

Not consciously.

Not perfectly.

But physically.
