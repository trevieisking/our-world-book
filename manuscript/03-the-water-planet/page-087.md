# CHAPTER THREE — THE WATER PLANET
## Page 087 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### THE FIRST OCEANS DID NOT HAVE TODAY'S RECIPE

Modern seawater is chemically familiar because we live beside it.

Sodium.

Chloride.

Magnesium.

Sulfate.

Calcium.

Potassium.

A long list of smaller components.

It is tempting to assume the ancient ocean was simply the same mixture in an older basin.

It was not.

Ocean chemistry changes when the atmosphere changes, the crust changes, continents grow, biology evolves and hydrothermal circulation alters the seafloor.

The early ocean was especially different because free oxygen was extremely scarce.

Without abundant oxygen, dissolved iron could remain in the reduced **Fe²⁺** form much more readily than it can in modern oxygenated surface seawater.

Manganese chemistry was different too.

Sulfate concentrations were probably far lower.

Dissolved silica was probably much higher because there were no silica-secreting organisms removing it on a modern biological scale.

A 2026 review of habitable-ocean chemistry notes that Hadean and Archean seawater was likely strongly influenced by intense water–rock interaction.

Models and ancient chemical sediments suggest seawater may have been relatively enriched in calcium and depleted in magnesium compared with today.

Its sulfur chemistry was also constrained by reducing conditions and abundant iron.

But we need one enormous warning label:

**the exact composition of Hadean seawater is poorly constrained.**

Salinity is a good example.

Some models suggest early oceans may have been as salty as, or saltier than, modern seawater because volcanic inputs were strong and large continental evaporite deposits had not yet become an efficient sink.

Other estimates remain model-dependent.

We do not possess a Hadean bucket of brine.

So this book will not print one precise salinity value as though someone measured it.

The same caution applies to pH.

Early-ocean pH has been estimated through models involving atmospheric carbon dioxide, basalt alteration, carbonate chemistry and hydrothermal exchange.

Different assumptions produce different results.

The scientifically secure statement is broader:

**the first oceans were anoxic, chemically dynamic and strongly coupled to volcanic rock and hydrothermal systems.**

That difference matters enormously for the chemistry that comes later.
