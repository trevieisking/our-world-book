### PHOSPHORUS: THE ELEMENT THAT LIKES TO HIDE

Phosphorus is essential to modern life.

It is part of DNA and RNA.

It is part of cell membranes.

It participates in the energy chemistry of living cells.

That makes its early availability important.

Unfortunately, phosphorus often prefers minerals to seawater.

On Earth, phosphate commonly occurs in minerals such as apatite.

Many phosphate minerals are poorly soluble.

For years, this produced what looked like a serious problem for prebiotic chemistry:

the element existed,

but perhaps not enough of it dissolved.

The modern picture is more interesting.

A 2023 review concluded that early-Earth phosphorus sources were more diverse than a simple apatite-only model.

Phosphorus could occur in phosphate minerals, as a minor component of silicate minerals, and in more reactive reduced phosphorus phases delivered by meteorites and dust.

Weathering could release these forms at different rates.

Then a 2022 experimental and modelling study changed another part of the argument.

Early seawater contained abundant dissolved Fe²⁺ under anoxic conditions.

Older estimates often assumed that iron would strongly remove phosphate from solution.

The experiments instead found that Fe²⁺ complexing can substantially increase the solubility of phosphate minerals in anoxic seawater.

Their models predicted marine phosphate concentrations **orders of magnitude higher** than some earlier estimates.

That does not mean the phosphorus problem is solved globally.

The result depends on pH, ionic strength, mineral assemblage, local iron chemistry and the strength of competing sinks.

Later formation of ferric iron oxides can remove phosphate efficiently.

Local volcanic lakes or evaporative settings can concentrate it far beyond open-ocean values.

The correct conclusion is therefore not:

**"phosphate was scarce"**

or

**"phosphate was abundant."**

It is:

**phosphorus availability was highly environment-dependent, and modern experiments have widened the range of plausible early-Earth conditions.**

Another chemical door.

This one may have been less tightly locked than scientists once thought.
