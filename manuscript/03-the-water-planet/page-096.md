# CHAPTER THREE — THE WATER PLANET
## Page 096 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### THE SHORELINE IS NOT A LINE

On a map, a coast is a boundary.

In nature, it is a moving zone.

Waves run up and retreat.

Tides flood and expose sediment.

Storms rearrange beaches.

Rivers push fresh water into salt water.

Groundwater enters from below.

Ponds appear and evaporate.

Mud cracks when it dries.

Channels migrate.

A shoreline can therefore contain more physical and chemical variation across a few kilometres than the open ocean contains across hundreds.

How early did such environments exist?

We should distinguish the obvious physical prediction from the surviving geological evidence.

Wherever early Earth had exposed land beside an ocean, waves and tides must have produced shore zones.

But Hadean shorelines are not preserved as recognisable beaches.

The direct sedimentary record becomes much clearer later.

By about **3.2 billion years ago**, the Moodies Group of South Africa preserves alluvial, shallow-marine, tidal and estuarine deposits.

These rocks contain sedimentary structures produced by currents, waves and repeated wetting and exposure.

They demonstrate that ancient coastlines could already include environments recognisably similar in physical process to modern tidal flats and estuaries, even though the atmosphere, organisms and chemistry were profoundly different.

The distinction matters.

A 3.2-billion-year-old tidal flat proves that tidal flats existed by 3.2 billion years ago.

It does not prove that the first ocean shoreline looked exactly the same 1.2 billion years earlier.

What it does show is that once land, tides, sediment and water existed together, familiar sedimentary physics emerged.

Sand still moved with current.

Mud still settled when water slowed.

Channels still migrated.

Exposed surfaces still dried.

The shoreline was already a machine for repeatedly changing temperature, salinity, water depth and chemical concentration.

Later, when we ask which environments could help complex chemistry proceed, that repeated cycling will matter.

For now, it is enough to recognise the coast as something more than an edge.

It is an engine of change.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Moodies Group literature, Barberton Greenstone Belt, ~3.2 Ga tidal–estuarine siliciclastic deposits.
- Javaux and related early-sedimentary-environment studies.

**Decision:** Hadean tidal flats are physically plausible but not directly preserved; the ~3.2-Ga Moodies record is used as direct younger evidence of tidal/estuarine sedimentary systems.
