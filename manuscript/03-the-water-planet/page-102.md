### NITROGEN: EVERYWHERE, BUT HARD TO USE

Nitrogen creates a perfect example of the difference between abundance and availability.

Molecular nitrogen — **N₂** — is chemically stubborn.

Its two nitrogen atoms are held together by a strong triple bond.

That makes N₂ an excellent atmospheric reservoir.

It also makes it difficult to convert into reactive forms.

Modern life solves the problem using enzymes that perform **nitrogen fixation**, converting N₂ into ammonia and related compounds that cells can use.

Before biology evolved that machinery, geochemistry had to do the work.

Several abiotic mechanisms can fix nitrogen.

Lightning provides enough energy to break N₂ apart and form nitrogen oxides.

Ultraviolet chemistry can contribute under appropriate atmospheric conditions.

Impacts can drive high-temperature reactions.

Hydrothermal systems may produce ammonia or ammonium in reducing environments.

Iron minerals can mediate nitrogen reactions.

How important each pathway was on early Earth remains uncertain.

Recent isotope work has strengthened the case that lightning supplied fixed nitrogen in ancient environments.

Other studies explore hydrothermal ammonia flux and iron-mediated abiotic nitrogen chemistry.

But none gives us one global Hadean nitrogen budget.

In an anoxic ocean, nitrate would not have behaved as it does in today's oxygenated surface waters.

Ammonium could be much more important in reduced environments.

Later, once biological nitrogen fixation evolved, the entire cycle changed.

That belongs to later chapters.

For now, the prebiotic problem is clear.

Earth may have had a large inventory of nitrogen while still having limited supplies of chemically accessible nitrogen compounds.

The atmosphere could be full of N₂ and yet chemistry at the surface could still be nitrogen-starved.

That is why elemental abundance can mislead.

A planet can possess the right atom and still keep it locked behind a chemical door.
