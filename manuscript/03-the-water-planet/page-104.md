### SULFUR: ONE ELEMENT, MANY REDOX STATES

Sulfur refuses to stay chemically simple.

It can exist in highly reduced forms such as sulfide.

It can exist in oxidised forms such as sulfate.

It can enter gases.

It can dissolve in water.

It can precipitate with metals.

It can become locked in minerals such as pyrite.

That range of oxidation states makes sulfur one of Earth's most useful redox tracers.

Volcanoes supplied sulfur gases to the early atmosphere.

Hydrothermal systems supplied reduced sulfur species to the ocean.

Ultraviolet light drove atmospheric sulfur photochemistry.

Minerals buried sulfur in the crust.

Weathering returned it.

But the sulfur cycle of the Archean ocean was not the modern one.

Today's seawater contains abundant sulfate because an oxygen-rich atmosphere allows oxidative weathering of sulfide minerals on land.

Before atmospheric oxygen accumulated, that source was much weaker.

Modern reviews therefore reconstruct Archean seawater sulfate as generally far below today's roughly 28 millimolar concentration.

Estimates vary by time and environment, but background concentrations through much of early Earth history were probably in the micromolar-to-low-millimolar range rather than modern values.

Reduced sulfur was correspondingly important in anoxic environments.

Hydrogen sulfide could react with dissolved iron.

Iron sulfides precipitated.

Sulfur isotope patterns later became one of the most important tools for reconstructing atmospheric oxygen and microbial metabolism.

Especially striking are sulfur-isotope signatures produced by ultraviolet photochemistry in an atmosphere lacking a strong oxygen/ozone shield.

Those signatures survive in ancient rocks.

So sulfur is not merely another dissolved nutrient.

It is a recorder.

Its minerals and isotopes preserve evidence about atmosphere, ocean and redox state.

And because sulfur participates in so many electron-transfer reactions, it also supplies chemical pathways that will matter greatly once metabolism enters our story.

But first, the ocean still contains another element in quantities modern seawater barely allows.

Iron.
