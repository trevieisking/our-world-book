# CHAPTER THREE — THE WATER PLANET
## Page 099 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### CHEMISTRY LOVES A BOUNDARY

Uniform environments are chemically quiet.

Boundaries are not.

At a river mouth, fresh water meets seawater.

At the sediment surface, oxygen-poor pore water can meet a chemically different water column.

At a hydrothermal vent, hot reduced fluid meets cooler ocean water.

At a shoreline, water meets air, rock and sunlight.

At a tidal pool, the same patch of chemistry can be flooded, heated, concentrated, dried and flooded again.

Every boundary changes the rules.

Temperature changes reaction rates.

pH changes which molecules carry charge.

Salinity changes solubility.

Redox conditions determine which electron-transfer reactions are favourable.

Mineral surfaces can adsorb some molecules more strongly than others.

Precipitation removes certain ions from solution.

Dissolution puts them back.

Flow continuously supplies new reactants and removes products.

This is why geological environments can behave like crude reactors without anything resembling laboratory glassware.

They do not control one variable at a time.

They impose many at once.

That is a nuisance for experimental chemistry.

It may also be exactly what makes natural chemistry interesting.

The early Earth contained an enormous variety of such interfaces:

wave-washed volcanic shores,

sediment pores,

river mouths,

evaporating pools,

fractured basalt,

hot springs,

deep hydrothermal systems,

mineral chimneys,

atmospheric droplets,

and ice or cold-region brines if local climate permitted them.

Not all of these environments necessarily existed everywhere or at the same time.

Not all are equally plausible for every chemical pathway.

And none can claim victory merely because a laboratory reaction works under vaguely similar conditions.

The important principle for this chapter is simpler:

**the water planet created repeated disequilibria.**

Places where materials were brought together.

Separated.

Concentrated.

Diluted.

Heated.

Cooled.

Wetted.

Dried.

Oxidised.

Reduced.

The chemistry of a planet happens disproportionately at boundaries.

Open water matters.

Interfaces decide what happens to it.
