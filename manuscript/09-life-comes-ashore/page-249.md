### LIFE BETWEEN WATER AND AIR

Where, exactly, does an organism cross from water into life on land?

A lake margin can be submerged today and exposed tomorrow. A film of moisture can keep cells alive on a surface that appears dry from a distance. An animal may cross a beach yet return to water to reproduce.

The boundary is a set of changing conditions, not simply a line on a map.

For this chapter, we must distinguish several achievements: surviving exposure, growing outside a water body, reproducing there and becoming part of a terrestrial food web. Evidence for one does not automatically demonstrate the others.

The challenges become clearer if we begin with a photosynthetic organism.

Its cells still require water for their chemistry. On an exposed surface, evaporation can remove that water faster than it can be replaced. Remaining close to damp ground or surviving periods of drying offers different possibilities from keeping a tall body continuously supplied.

Protection creates its own problem. A barrier that slows water loss must also allow the exchange of gases needed for photosynthesis and respiration. The plant cuticle, an outer covering containing waxes and other materials, helps regulate this exchange with the environment. Its properties vary; it is not a perfectly sealed wrapper.

Water is only part of the challenge.

Exposed organisms encounter changing temperature and light, including ultraviolet radiation that can damage biological molecules. Living plants and their algal relatives contain chemical systems that sense or respond to such stresses. Some algae also produce compounds that help shield them from ultraviolet light.

Those comparisons suggest that parts of the biological equipment for terrestrial life developed before the bodies we recognise as land plants. They do not reveal a complete ancestral organism or the exact day it occupied an exposed surface.

Reproduction supplies a further test.

Many living non-seed plants inhabit land while retaining swimming sperm. Fertilisation requires a continuous layer of moisture through which those cells can reach an egg. Spores can disperse beyond that immediate wet patch, but dispersal and fertilisation are separate stages.

Living ashore therefore need not mean becoming independent of water at every point in a life cycle. Later reproductive innovations changed the degree and timing of that dependence.

How can we follow these changes through deep time?

Fossil bodies reveal structures. Reproductive remains may record organisms whose larger parts have vanished. Sediments help distinguish a lake, a floodplain or a marine deposit receiving material carried from land. Comparisons among living organisms supply another line of evidence, with their own assumptions and limits.

Together, these records can establish a sequence of possibilities becoming realised. They rarely provide one uninterrupted biography of the transition.

Plants, fungi and animals also have different histories. Combining their earliest known occurrences into a single arrival would erase the very changes we are trying to understand.

The first task is smaller and closer to the ground.

Before stems rose into the air or roots penetrated deeply, what inhabited the thin, wet meeting place between rock, sediment and atmosphere?
