### A WORLD THAT COULD RENEW ITSELF

Leaving the water did not mean abandoning the need for it. An embryo still required a suitable fluid environment. The evolutionary problem was how that environment could be maintained while the parent lived—and perhaps reproduced—away from a pond or stream.

The **amniotes**, the group containing mammals and reptiles, including birds, share a history of specialised tissues surrounding the embryo. The amnion encloses its immediate fluid-filled space. Associated membranes participate in exchange, nourishment and handling waste. Their arrangement is more fundamental than the familiar hard shell of a bird's egg.[1]

A hard shell is therefore not the defining object to search for at the beginning of this history. Different amniotes lay eggs with different coverings or retain developing young internally. Reconstructing the ancestral condition requires comparing those reproductive modes, not choosing one living animal as an unchanged model.[1]

A 2023 study combined a much younger, Cretaceous fossil with comparisons across extinct and living amniotes. Its analyses supported prolonged retention of embryos within the parent early in amniote evolution, potentially including live birth. That is a reconstruction of an ancestral condition, not a Carboniferous embryo preserved during delivery. The authors explicitly identified the scarcity of early reproductive fossils as a limitation.[1]

Even the date at which amniotes first appear is open to new evidence. In 2025, researchers described early Carboniferous tracks from Victoria, Australia. Five-toed impressions and distinct claw marks supported their interpretation of an amniote maker, probably on the reptile side of the family tree. The attribution moved the proposed minimum age of that group substantially earlier.[2]

Here, again, the evidence has limits. The slab preserves foot contacts, not reproductive organs. An amniote interpretation would connect the maker to a lineage with a particular evolutionary history; it would not reveal its shell thickness, breeding site or length of pregnancy. Those details cannot be drawn into the scene merely because the footprints are persuasive.

The distinction returns us to the whole chapter. Plants had to acquire water while exposing tissues to air. Fungi entered partnerships and decay systems. Arthropods fed, breathed and moved in habitats whose preservation varied. Vertebrates assembled limbs, respiratory capacities and body supports in different combinations. The surviving evidence does not place all these changes at a single threshold.[3]

Nor was land an empty stage awaiting the arrival of the most familiar actor. By the time digit-bearing vertebrates enter this account, earlier pages have already followed rooted vegetation, microbial associations, decomposers and consumers. Becoming terrestrial meant joining—and changing—communities, not beginning biological history on an unused surface.[3]

The records remain uneven. A plant's reproductive structure may survive without its embryo. A track may survive without its maker. A collection of bones may represent only one age group. Each can establish something important without supplying an entire life story.

Taken together, they reveal the construction of ecosystems able to persist across generations. That persistence should not be confused with permanence. A functioning community can still be disrupted; lineages with long histories can disappear. Having traced how the continents became inhabited, we now face another question: how do we recognise the loss of a world, and distinguish that loss from another silence in the rocks?
