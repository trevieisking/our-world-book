### THE WALK THE JOINTS WOULD NOT ALLOW

An animal can look capable of walking until someone tries to fit its bones into the required positions. A drawing may place a foot almost anywhere. A joint cannot. The surfaces connecting two bones impose constraints that an attractive reconstruction must obey.

*Ichthyostega*, another Devonian tetrapod, became a test of that difference. In 2005, investigators re-examined original specimens alongside newly collected material and reconstructed its backbone. They found pronounced differences between regions of the vertebral column, rather than a uniform series of interchangeable joints.[1]

The shapes of the arches above the vertebrae suggested that important movements occurred up and down rather than predominantly from side to side. This was not simply a matter of adding legs to a body that continued bending in the familiar way. The trunk itself had a distinctive mechanical organisation.[1]

A 2012 study then examined the limbs in three dimensions. Stephanie Pierce, Jennifer Clack and John Hutchinson reconstructed the skeleton and compared joint mobility with that of living tetrapods. Their analysis found that *Ichthyostega* could not produce the usual alternating sequence of steps characteristic of many walking four-limbed animals.[2]

An important restriction involved rotation around a limb bone's length. Bend your elbow and then turn your upper arm: these are different movements. Moving an appendage forwards and backwards is not equivalent to rotating it into every position needed to support and advance a body. Counting possible directions is less informative than checking how they combine.

The reconstructed joints lacked rotations needed for the familiar walking pattern. This does not mean that *Ichthyostega* was incapable of moving, or that it never contacted exposed ground. Excluding one gait is not the same as excluding every means of locomotion. The result narrows the possibilities rather than supplying a film of the animal's life.[2]

Such a model is strongest when its assumptions remain visible. The fossil preserves hard structures much better than all the tissues that once surrounded them. A reconstructed range of movement must therefore be understood as a claim about a body assembled from surviving evidence, not an independent observation of the living animal.

The comparison also reaches back to the footprints. The 2012 investigators concluded that animals sharing this skeletal arrangement and restricted mobility were unlikely makers of some Middle Devonian trackways. An appealing match between an early footprint and a famous early skeleton can fail a mechanical test.[2]

That mismatch need not be a failure of evolutionary explanation. It can reveal diversity that a simple sequence of textbook pictures has concealed. One lineage may retain or develop capacities that another lacks. A well-preserved species is especially informative, but preservation does not appoint it representative of every contemporary relative.

The investigation has now moved through several kinds of evidence: internal fin bones, supported postures, tracks, breathing structures, growth histories and joint geometry. They answer different questions about the transition and do not all place the same boundary between water and land.

What happened after these Devonian combinations? For many years, a sparse stretch of the fossil record made the next part of the story difficult to follow. The investigation must now enter that apparent gap.
