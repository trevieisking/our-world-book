### PATHWAYS THROUGH A STEM

A growing tip rises above damp ground. How does water reach it?

A small plant can exchange materials across short distances. As a body extends, its different parts become separated from the resources they need. Internal transport allows water acquired below to reach tissues exposed to the air, while products of photosynthesis can supply other parts of the plant.

The surviving evidence lies within the stem.

In living vascular plants, **xylem** carries water and dissolved minerals. One of its important cell types is the **tracheid**, an elongated cell that conducts water after its living contents have disappeared. Reinforced walls help it retain its shape.

**Phloem** transports organic substances, including sugars. Its conducting cells remain living. Together, these tissues connect regions of a plant that perform different tasks.

Those modern arrangements give us questions to ask of fossils, rather than a template to impose on every ancient body.

A 1991 study examined conducting cells in *Rhynia* and *Asteroxylon* from the Rhynie chert using thin sections and scanning electron microscopy. The investigators found different kinds of wall architecture.

*Asteroxylon* possessed cells comparable in several respects to the water-conducting cells of living vascular plants. *Rhynia* had a different organisation, with distinctive thickenings and wall layers.

The contrast made a central point visible: a conducting strand is not one unchanging invention.

Other Rhynie plants, including *Aglaophyton*, had elongated conducting cells without the same characteristic secondary thickenings. Water transport could therefore exist without the complete combination of features familiar from modern tracheids.

Shape was only part of the investigation.

Lignin is a complex material deposited in many vascular-plant cell walls. It contributes properties important to support and water transport. But a thickened fossil wall does not automatically establish that its original chemistry matched a living tracheid.

In 2003, researchers compared the chemical remnants of different tissues in *Aglaophyton*, *Rhynia* and *Asteroxylon*. Carbon-isotope measurements and X-ray analyses suggested that some early conducting tissues were weakly lignified or possibly unlignified.

Their results also supported possible lignification outside the central conducting strand. The components of transport and support need not have appeared in their modern locations together.

Burial alters organic chemistry, so such conclusions depend on comparisons and controls. They are interpretations of surviving chemical differences, not direct measurements of pristine Devonian lignin.

The food-transport system introduces a further revision.

A study published in 2026 re-examined tissues previously described as phloem-like in Rhynie plants. Their anatomy differed from modern phloem, although possible sieve pores were identified in *Asteroxylon*.

The authors proposed calling these tissues food-conducting cells. Their interpretation supports gradual assembly of phloem characteristics, on a different evolutionary timetable from xylem.

Fossils thus reveal combinations of traits rather than a single moment when a modern vascular system arrived fully formed. Some pathways are clearer because reinforced walls preserve better than delicate cells.

Transport also connects the inside of a plant with its surface.

The next evidence is a tiny opening through which gases and water vapour could pass.
