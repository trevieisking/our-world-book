### AN OPENING TO THE AIR

A protective surface creates a problem of its own. A plant needs carbon dioxide from the air to build sugars through photosynthesis. A surface that restricts water loss can also restrict the arrival of that gas. What evidence survives of the openings through which early plants met the air?

A **stoma**, plural **stomata**, is a microscopic pore bordered by specialised guard cells. In a flowering plant, changes in these cells' internal water pressure alter the opening. Carbon dioxide can enter, while water vapour escapes. The pore connects the atmosphere to spaces within the plant rather than simply exposing every cell to the outside.

Those connections survive in stone. Sections through the Rhynie plants *Rhynia* and *Aglaophyton* reveal pores with channels leading into chambers beneath the surface. These plants did not need broad leaves to possess stomata: their green axes carried them. Across the Rhynie flora, the size, position and surrounding anatomy of these openings varied. The fossils preserve a diversity of arrangements, rather than one uniform early design.

This is strong evidence for an anatomical connection with the air. It is less direct evidence for the movements that once regulated it. A fossil can preserve a pore and the walls around it; it cannot be watched opening in sunlight or closing when water becomes scarce. Inferring its behaviour requires comparisons, and the choice of living comparison matters.

Consider a moss capsule, the structure containing its spores. In a 2016 experiment, researchers disrupted genes required for stomatal development in a living moss. Capsules without stomata opened later than ordinary capsules. That result links stomata with the timing of spore release in the tested plant. The same study identified relationships between developmental regulators in mosses and flowering plants, supporting a deep evolutionary connection in how these structures form.

Their behaviour is more contentious. A 2022 synthesis argued that active responses to environmental signals were ancestral to land plants. In 2025, another research team reported measurements of more than 25,000 apertures across twelve moss species. Using divided capsules to compare treated and untreated tissue, they found no aperture response to the external cues they tested. Their interpretation favours a major role in capsule drying and a later, gradual acquisition of active regulation.

That newer study does not measure an extinct Devonian plant. It does show why the presence of related genes cannot, by itself, establish identical physiological behaviour. A shared developmental inheritance can persist while the resulting structure performs different jobs. Experimental sampling and the condition of the tissues also affect what can be inferred.

The secure conclusion is that early plants had already developed organised openings and internal air spaces. Exactly how the first stomata balanced carbon uptake, water loss and reproduction remains harder to reconstruct. There was no single problem called “living on land” solved by one innovation.

The surface connected a plant to the air; its conducting tissues linked distant parts within the body. A third connection reached into the ground. To understand that one, we must ask what makes a root a root.
