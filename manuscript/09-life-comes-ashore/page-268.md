### A HEAD WITHOUT ITS HISTORY

A small fossil can carry a very large claim. Place it on one branch of the animal family tree and it extends the history of insects. Move it to another and the same fragment tells us something different. The rock has not changed; the identification has.

The Rhynie chert preserves *Rhyniella*, a springtail. Springtails belong to the broader six-legged group called **hexapods**, but they are distinguished from insects in the narrower classification used here. Recognising an ancient hexapod therefore does not automatically establish an ancient insect, still less an insect capable of flight.[1]

Another Rhynie fossil, *Rhyniognatha hirsti*, became central to that distinction. In 2004, Michael Engel and David Grimaldi interpreted its fragmentary mouthparts as those of an insect. They identified features shared with winged insects and suggested that the history of wings might reach further back than previously recognised.[1]

Notice the steps in that argument. The preserved object was a fragment, not an insect with wings spread across a slab. Its proposed relationship supplied the inference about flight. That inference depended on identifying the anatomical features correctly and placing their owner on the right evolutionary branch.

A 2017 re-examination challenged that placement. Carolin and Joachim Haug used three-dimensional imaging to investigate the same incomplete remains. They identified additional parts of a head and another pair of mouthparts, and questioned earlier interpretations of other structures. Their reconstruction favoured a **myriapod**, the group containing centipedes and millipedes, possibly an early centipede.[2]

That alternative was not a newly discovered complete body. It was a different reading of a difficult fossil, supported by newly resolved structures. The responsible conclusion is not that the rock contains a proven flying insect, nor that every uncertainty has vanished under the replacement name. Its insect identity remains disputed.

A study published in August 2026 added a different kind of evidence. Researchers described *Chosha praecursor* from the Carboniferous Tesnus Formation in Texas, about 324 million years ago. Images made with crossed polarising filters revealed a combination of features including an egg-laying structure and a terminal filament, together with jointed abdominal appendages modified into paddle-like forms.[3]

An analysis of evolutionary relationships favoured its placement on an early branch of insect history. The authors interpreted its unusual, gill-like appendages as evidence for a semiaquatic life. The specimen was much younger than the Rhynie fossils; its importance lay in the combination of structures, not in replacing every older fossil with a single new starting date.[3]

This finding also changes what we should look for. An early relative need not carry the complete arrangement familiar from living members of its group. Conversely, an unfamiliar arrangement does not become ancestral merely because it looks unusual. The proposed relationship must survive comparison across many anatomical characters.

Small terrestrial animals thus leave two intertwined investigations: how their bodies worked, and where those bodies belong in the tree of life. Neither can be settled by attaching a dramatic label to one fragment. Among vertebrates, the same problem will become visible in a different material: the bones inside a fin.
