### THE GAP THAT FILLED WITH BONES

What does an empty stretch of the fossil record contain? Perhaps fewer animals. Perhaps rocks that rarely preserved them. Perhaps specimens that nobody has yet recognised. These possibilities can produce a similar blank on a diagram while describing very different histories.

The sparse record following the Devonian became known as **Romer's Gap**. It covered an important part of the early Carboniferous, when the descendants of the animals we have just examined were diversifying. The problem was not an absence of geological time. It was an uneven supply of fossils through which to investigate it.[1,2]

In 2006, Peter Ward and colleagues proposed an environmental explanation. Their comparison of terrestrial arthropod and vertebrate records, combined with a model of ancient atmospheric composition, associated the gap with low oxygen. They argued that conditions had restricted the expansion of air-breathing animals.[1]

This was a testable proposal, not a measurement made by placing an instrument in Carboniferous air. It joined two reconstructed patterns: the occurrence of fossils and the estimated history of oxygen. If either pattern changed with new evidence, the proposed connection required another examination.

At Blue Beach in Nova Scotia, the evidence arrived in fragments. A study published in 2015 described a diverse collection of early Carboniferous tetrapod bones. The energetic conditions under which many had accumulated tended to separate skeletons and favour remains from larger individuals. Identifying an isolated bone could therefore depend on comparison with a more complete animal found elsewhere.[2]

Collection mattered too. Regular visits by local collectors recovered material as erosion exposed it. The study showed that a place long known to contain fossils could become much more informative when the frequency of searching changed. Discovery was not simply a matter of locating the right rock once.[2]

The fragments indicated several evolutionary groups, although many could not be assigned to a particular species. Small tracks and isolated bones also suggested inhabitants poorly represented among the larger remains. A collection can therefore reveal substantial diversity without becoming a complete census of its community.[2]

Scottish discoveries supplied another view. A study published online in 2016 described five new tetrapods from the Tournaisian, the earliest division of the Carboniferous. Anatomical comparisons placed them on different branches of the evolving tetrapod tree, supporting diversification well within the supposed gap.[3]

Their surroundings were informative. Fossil-bearing sandy siltstones commonly lay above ancient wetland soils. The investigators interpreted the animals as probably inhabiting vegetated surfaces later affected by flooding. Their atmospheric reconstruction also challenged the idea of a severe oxygen barrier across the Devonian–Carboniferous boundary.[3]

Together, these discoveries weaken a simple equation between few known fossils and few living animals. They do not prove that every region supported the same abundance, or that environmental changes were unimportant. They show that preservation, recognition and sustained collecting can account for a substantial part of the apparent silence.[2,3]

The gap became a research programme rather than an empty chapter. As it filled, another distinction remained: an animal might move successfully on land without reproducing there. Establishing a terrestrial generation required more than feet. Plants, too, faced the problem of protecting the next stage of life.
