### AN EXCHANGE INSIDE THE PLANT

Finding a fungus inside a plant does not automatically reveal a partnership. The fungus might have infected living tissue, consumed a dead body, or participated in an exchange beneficial to both organisms.

The arrangement of the surviving structures helps distinguish these possibilities.

In 1994, researchers described fungal threads inside *Aglaophyton*, an Early Devonian plant preserved in the Rhynie chert of Scotland. Within a particular zone of its outer tissues, the threads formed finely branched structures called **arbuscules**.

Each had a basal trunk dividing into progressively smaller branches. The resemblance was to arbuscules in living plants, where fungal and plant tissues form an interface for nutrient exchange.

Here was more than a fungus and a plant buried beside one another. The fossil preserved their intimate anatomical association.

That structure strongly supports an ancient mycorrhizal partnership. It does not preserve a measurement of phosphorus moving into the plant, or carbon moving into the fungus. Those processes must be investigated through comparisons with living organisms.

A plant needs mineral nutrients as well as sunlight, water and carbon dioxide. Fungal threads can reach into soil beyond the immediate absorbing surfaces of their host. In many living mycorrhizal associations, fungi supply mineral nutrients while receiving carbon compounds produced by the plant.

Experiments allow researchers to follow the exchange.

A 2012 study used radioactive forms of carbon and phosphorus as tracers in living liverworts and vascular plants. These labels made it possible to quantify carbon allocated to fungal networks and phosphorus delivered to plant tissues.

The researchers also compared growth under two carbon dioxide concentrations. The efficiency of phosphorus gained for carbon invested changed differently in the examined liverworts and vascular plants.

The result shows why a partnership cannot be reduced to one permanent rate of exchange. Its operation depends on the organisms and conditions being tested. A growth chamber can examine a proposed mechanism; it cannot recreate the whole Devonian world.

Genes provide another approach.

In 2021, researchers investigated the living liverwort *Marchantia paleacea*. They demonstrated lipid transfer from plant to fungus and identified regulation by WRINKLED, a protein controlling gene activity. When its function was disrupted, normal arbuscules failed to form and the mutualism was aborted.

Lipids are carbon-rich molecules that include fats. Their transfer gives the fungus another route to resources made by its host.

A 2025 study investigated signalling genes in the same liverwort. Disrupting any of three central genes impaired its association with arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi. Comparisons with flowering plants supported the conservation of this signalling machinery across widely separated plant lineages.

Such findings support an ancient inherited capacity for partnership. They do not identify the exact first plant–fungus pair or date its first exchange. Living liverworts are present-day relatives, not specimens recovered unchanged from the beginning of land vegetation.

The fossils preserve architecture. Experiments reveal function. Evolutionary comparisons connect their histories.

To understand why such delicate associations survived at Rhynie, we must now examine the place that preserved them.
