### THE FOREST FLOOR AT GILBOA

A fallen trunk can travel. Water may carry it far from the place where it grew, so a deposit containing trees is not necessarily a preserved forest. Roots connected to their bases, still arranged within an ancient ground surface, provide a different kind of evidence.

At Gilboa in New York, Middle Devonian fossils include sandstone casts of enlarged tree bases called *Eospermatopteris*. A cast forms when sediment occupies a space left by an original structure. It records shape without necessarily retaining the tissues that produced it. These bases revealed substantial upright plants, but for a long time their upper parts remained uncertain.

The distinction between a fossil part and a whole organism becomes crucial here. An isolated base and an isolated crown may receive different scientific names before anyone establishes their connection. Joining them because they occur in similar rocks would be insufficient. The stronger test is a specimen preserving their attachment.

In 2007, researchers reported fossils from Schoharie County that linked an intact crown identified as *Wattieza* with an *Eospermatopteris* trunk and base. The discovery supplied evidence for the architecture above the familiar stumps. A previously disconnected set of fossil parts could now be understood as components of a tree.

These were cladoxylopsids, extinct vascular plants with a distinctive internal organisation. Their relationship to living plant groups remains under investigation. A review published in September 2026 emphasises both their structural diversity and unresolved relationships with other major lineages. A resemblance to a familiar tree silhouette cannot settle those relationships.

That caution gives the fossils greater significance. They preserve combinations of anatomy and growth that living forests do not show directly. Reconstruction must follow the attached parts and internal tissues, rather than borrowing the complete body of a modern fern or palm.

The forest itself became clearer when work at Riverside Quarry exposed its ancient rooting surface again in 2010. A study published in 2012 mapped about 1,200 square metres. Numerous *Eospermatopteris* root systems remained in their growth positions. Their placement turned separate bases into evidence for a stand of trees.

Other plants complicated the picture. Large woody horizontal axes ran between the trees, with roots and aerial branch systems. The researchers identified them as aneurophytalean progymnosperms, an extinct group of woody, spore-producing plants. They proposed that some may have climbed into the trees. Evidence also indicated tree stature in a lycophyte at the site.

The mapped forest was therefore more varied than a collection of identical trunks. Its sediment and shallow rooting horizon supported interpretation as a wetland on a coastal plain, subject to disturbance. Probable climbing remains an ecological inference; preserved connections and root positions supply the firmer observations.

This surface represents a particular place and interval. Burial preserved some structures better than others, and the exposed area samples only part of the ancient landscape. It cannot establish the composition of every Devonian forest.

Gilboa shows how research moves from a stump to a body, and from a body to a community. Other buried surfaces would reveal another major difference among early forests: the depth and reach of their roots.
