### WHEN FOOTPRINTS OUTRUN BONES

A skeleton preserves parts of an animal. A track preserves a meeting between an animal and a surface. Neither record is complete, but they can challenge one another. Sometimes the movement appears in the rocks before we find the body that could have made it.

At Zachełmie Quarry in Poland, early Middle Devonian surfaces carry tracks interpreted as those of four-limbed vertebrates. Some impressions include digit marks. Their arrangement supports the passage of an animal using paired limbs rather than simply resting a fish-like body against the bottom.[1]

These surfaces are older than the Late Devonian rocks containing *Tiktaalik*. That does not make its anatomy irrelevant. It means that a fossil displaying a useful combination of features need not sit immediately before the first animal possessing a later combination. Different branches can persist together, while the surviving specimens sample them unevenly.[1,3]

The surroundings require their own investigation. A 2018 study proposed temporary lakes on a low coastal plain, rather than the predominantly marine-influenced setting favoured in earlier interpretations. Drying cracks, ancient soil horizons and root traces supported episodes of exposure within the succession. The reconstruction nevertheless allowed occasional incursions from the nearby sea.[1]

Crucially, the authors placed the preserved vertebrate tracks underwater. They inferred travel over land from the proposed landscape and the resources available around it. The footprints themselves were not a direct record of an animal crossing a dry lake bed.[1]

This distinction is easy to lose in a picture. An exposed surface somewhere in a sequence is not necessarily the surface on which a particular animal walked. Evidence has an address: a bed, a specimen, a position within the rocks. Moving it silently to a neighbouring layer can change the story without adding a single observation.

A different Polish discovery makes the need for separation even clearer. In 2025, researchers described Lower Devonian traces from Ujazd and Kopiec, not Zachełmie. These included linked impressions attributed to a body, tail and fins, together with depressions interpreted as contacts made by a snout.[2]

The investigators proposed a lungfish-like maker moving while partly out of water. In their reconstruction, pressing the snout against the ground helped anchor the animal as the rest of its body advanced. Comparison with living lungfish informed that interpretation; no skeleton lay at the end of the trail to identify its owner.[2]

Such movement is not the same claim as walking with digit-bearing limbs. An animal can exploit contact with the ground through its trunk, head or fins. Discovering one mechanism does not establish another, and showing a capability does not identify the ancestor of every animal that later used the land.

Tracks therefore enrich the evolutionary history without turning it into a procession of named species. They supply evidence of actions, while anatomy constrains possible makers and geology constrains setting and age. Where those lines agree, the reconstruction strengthens. Where they do not, the disagreement becomes a question to investigate.
