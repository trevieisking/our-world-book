### THREADS IN THE GROUND

A plant can build organic matter using sunlight. A fungus must obtain it from elsewhere.

Many living fungi grow as fine tubes called **hyphae**. Their branching network is a **mycelium**. By extending through a substrate, these threads reach resources beyond the point where growth began. Some fungi release enzymes that break complex material into substances they can absorb.

This makes decomposition one possible way of life. Others feed on living hosts or form partnerships with them. A thread-shaped body alone cannot tell us which relationship an ancient fungus had.

That problem matters because the history of fungi reaches beyond the familiar mushroom.

In 2019, researchers reported microscopic fossils from the Grassy Bay Formation in Arctic Canada, in rocks dated to approximately 1,010–890 million years ago. They named the organism *Ourasphaira giraldae*.

Its branching, partitioned filaments connected with rounded structures. The investigators combined shape, microscopic wall structure and chemical analysis to argue for a fungal identity. Chemical signatures consistent with chitin, an important component of fungal cell walls, strengthened the case.

The argument rests on these observations together. It does not require a recognisable mushroom or a match with every feature of a living species.

The rocks also place a limit on the ecological story.

They are interpreted as estuarine deposits, formed where a river met the sea. Preservation in that setting does not establish that the organism spent its life on permanently exposed ground. Nor does the age of a fossil reveal when its lineage first evolved.

*Ourasphaira* therefore extends the evidence for ancient fungal life without supplying a dated scene of fungi conquering dry land.

Much younger fossils show a different level of organisation.

*Tortotubus protuberans* occurs in Silurian and Devonian assemblages. A 2016 study examined material from New York, Gotland and Scotland, comparing specimens that appeared to record different growth stages.

Secondary branches grew back along a principal filament and proliferated around it. The resulting structure contained entwined threads, rather than a single undifferentiated tube. This provided evidence for an organised, cord-forming body.

Such organisation invites comparisons with living fungi. But a comparison can be useful without fixing the fossil's exact position on the family tree.

The original study placed *Tortotubus* within the **Dikarya**, the broad fungal group containing familiar moulds, yeasts and mushrooms. A published response agreed that its branching filaments probably represented fungal hyphae, while challenging that narrower assignment.

Similar structures can evolve separately. To establish shared ancestry, researchers must ask how the relevant characters are distributed across related groups, not merely identify a resemblance.

The disagreement leaves a meaningful result: probable fungi with differentiated filamentous growth were present in early terrestrial assemblages. It leaves other questions open, including precise relationships and the resources particular individuals consumed.

We cannot turn these fossils into a complete account of ancient decay. We can recognise organisms whose growth differed from that of the plants beside them.

The next evidence brings those separate bodies into contact.

What happens when fungal threads are preserved inside a plant?
