### WHAT MAKES A ROOT?

An underground branch is not automatically a root. A plant can anchor itself or absorb water through structures that differ from the roots beneath a modern tree. The distinction matters because calling every buried axis a root would hide the changes we are trying to explain.

Botanists examine both structure and development. A **meristem** is a region whose dividing cells produce new growth. In a typical living vascular-plant root, a protective **root cap** covers the growing tip. Root hairs increase the absorbing surface, while the organ's branching pattern helps distinguish it from a shoot. These features must be investigated individually when only fossilised tissues remain.

The Rhynie plant *Asteroxylon mackiei* supplies an unusually revealing test. It belongs to an early branch of the lycophytes, the group that includes living clubmosses. Its body contained leafy shoots and specialised structures that reached into the substrate. Which of these structures possessed the defining anatomy of roots?

A 2018 investigation examined preserved growing tips. The researchers found an outer tissue layer continuing across the tip, rather than a separate cap. Cell arrangements also differed from those that generate a root cap in living relatives. The absence was supported by preserved anatomy, rather than inferred merely because a fragile feature had failed to fossilise. They called these organs rooting axes: structures performing a rooting role without the complete combination found in living roots.

The next problem was their attachment. Isolated sections can show what an organ contains while leaving uncertain where it belongs. In 2021, researchers used thirty-one successive rock sections to reconstruct a connected part of an *Asteroxylon* plant digitally. Following tissues through neighbouring slices distinguished three types of axis: leafy shoots, root-bearing axes and the finer rooting axes attached to them.

Branch junctions and additional developmental evidence supported a particular growth mechanism. A growing axis divided into unequal branches, one becoming a rooting axis. This is different from inserting a modern tree's root system into an ancient reconstruction because the plant seems to need one. The connections were traced through the fossil.

Together, these findings support the assembly of root characteristics in stages. An organ could enter the ground before acquiring a cap over its growing tip. Its combination of traits was functional in its own setting; “transitional” describes its evolutionary position, not a plant waiting to become complete.

Comparisons across the plant family tree add another implication. A 2019 synthesis placed these fossils beside evidence from other vascular-plant lineages and supported at least two independent origins of roots: one in lycophytes and another in the lineage containing ferns and seed plants. That conclusion depends on reconstructed relationships and character histories, rather than a preserved record of every intermediate generation.

A connected fossil can therefore change both a plant reconstruction and an account of evolution. Below ground, familiar organs emerged through unfamiliar combinations. Above ground, another familiar structure was also taking different forms. Green stems already caught light, but what would distinguish a leaf from a small outgrowth or a flattened branch?
