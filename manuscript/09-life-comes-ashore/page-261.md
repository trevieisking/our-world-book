### WOOD BEFORE THE FOREST

Wood and trees are easy to associate. Yet evidence for the earliest wood comes from small plants, and some early trees enlarged their trunks in ways unlike those of an oak. The history becomes clearer when we investigate a tissue separately from the growth form it later helped support.

Wood is **secondary xylem**, conducting tissue added by a **vascular cambium**, a growth-producing layer. Its production can increase an axis's thickness. In fossils, botanists look for organised rows of conducting cells, tissue extending radially across them, and patterns indicating the addition of new cell rows. A thick stem alone does not establish that this growth process occurred.

In 2011, researchers described simple wood in two small Early Devonian plants, from deposits roughly 407 to 397 million years old. Their secondary xylem included simple rays, the radial tissue running across the conducting system. These discoveries placed wood in plants that were not towering forest trees. Any explanation of its origin must account for that smaller setting.

One of these early woody plants, *Armoricaphyton chateaupannense*, is known from western France. A 2014 investigation used intense X-rays from a synchrotron, a particle-accelerator facility, to build three-dimensional images of its fossil wood. The technique allowed researchers to examine internal structure without relying only on exposed cuts.

They measured the conducting cells and used their geometry to calculate hydraulic properties. The results suggested wood capable of effective water conduction, with relatively low resistance to the forces associated with water under tension. These are anatomical estimates, rather than measurements of sap flowing through a living *Armoricaphyton*. They make the conducting role central to the investigation instead of treating wood only as scaffolding for height.

The evidence also keeps expanding through comparison. A study first published in 2025 developed measurements of conducting-cell size at different positions within wood. Tested against living material and then applied to Early Devonian fossils, the method helped distinguish forms that otherwise shared similar anatomy. It shows how fragments can yield information about diversity even when complete plants remain unavailable.

Later Devonian trunks reveal another possibility. Fossils of *Xinicaulis lignescens* from China, about 374 million years old, preserve a tree built around numerous separate, interconnected xylem strands. Researchers reported in 2017 that wood grew around individual strands. Expansion involved changes in the surrounding tissues and the separation of some connections within the network.

This was a different construction from the single expanding woody cylinder familiar in many living trees. The sections preserve its components; the sequence of enlargement is reconstructed by comparing their anatomy. Its complexity prevents us from treating an early tree as merely a simpler version of a modern one.

The biological changes that enabled greater stature included transport, support and continued growth, combined differently in different plants. Wood could exist before a large trunk, and a large trunk could develop through an unfamiliar arrangement of wood.

A fragment of conducting tissue establishes an anatomical capability. A forest requires another kind of evidence: plants preserved together, and clues to where they stood. The next investigation moves from cells within stems to trunks on a buried ground surface.
