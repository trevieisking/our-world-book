### MORE THAN A GREEN SURFACE

A green stem can photosynthesise. Early plants therefore did not need leaves before they could feed themselves using light. Leaves changed the organisation of that work, but the fossil record does not show one leaf design appearing once and passing unchanged to every later plant.

*Asteroxylon* carried small outgrowths along its shoots. Conducting strands extended towards them without producing the fully vascularised arrangement of later lycophyte leaves. Such combinations complicate any boundary drawn solely from outward shape. An appendage can look leaf-like while preserving only part of the anatomical package familiar in its living relatives.

Other plant lineages preserve different sequences. Branch systems became unequal, with a main axis overtopping smaller branches; some lateral systems became flatter, and tissue could occupy the spaces between their divisions. These are components of proposed routes towards broader leaves. Placing fossils on the plant family tree supports multiple origins, rather than a single universal progression from a tiny projection to a fern frond and then an oak leaf.

Developmental genetics adds an apparent puzzle. If leaves originated independently, must each lineage have invented an entirely new mechanism for making them? A 2005 study compared gene activity in a lycophyte with evidence from flowering plants. Experiments transferring gene function between species, together with evolutionary comparisons, supported the recruitment of a conserved developmental mechanism into independently evolved leaves.

An old biological process can therefore contribute to a new organ in more than one lineage. Shared genetic machinery is evidence about developmental inheritance; it does not erase the fossil evidence for distinct organ histories. This is another reason to distinguish the age of a mechanism from the age of the structure using it.

Even the arrangement of appendages resists a single template. **Phyllotaxis** means the pattern in which lateral organs are positioned around a stem. A 2023 study measured these patterns in fossil *Asteroxylon*. It found both whorls and spirals. The measured spirals differed from the familiar Fibonacci patterns, whose spiral counts follow a sequence made by adding the two preceding numbers. Leaves and reproductive structures also occupied the same positional series, suggesting similarities in their development.

Those observations describe an extinct plant, rather than a mathematical rule imposed on it. They reveal how much variety was already present near the early history of leafy shoots.

A broader surface also changes the physical problem of catching light. It intercepts energy and must lose heat. In 2001, researchers combined fossil characteristics with calculations of gas exchange and heat balance. Their simulations suggested that broad leaves with sparse stomata could overheat under the assumed Early Devonian conditions. They proposed that changing carbon dioxide levels and increased stomatal density helped make broader leaves viable.

The calculation identifies a plausible constraint. It does not measure an ancient leaf's temperature, establish a single cause for every leaf origin, or make atmospheric change a substitute for developmental evidence.

The emerging plant body was a combination of surfaces, transport routes and growth patterns. More photosynthetic area could be carried outward or upward. Supporting that area at greater height would require another change inside the stem.
