### A LANDSCAPE HELD IN STONE

The fungal branches inside *Aglaophyton* survived because an unusual setting preserved more than the outline of a plant.

Near the present village of Rhynie in northeastern Scotland, hot springs once discharged water rich in dissolved silica. Their deposits accumulated within an Early Devonian basin, roughly 410 million years ago.

That rounded date describes a geological interval. Researchers have dated both volcanic rock and minerals associated with the hydrothermal system. Relating those measurements to the fossil-bearing layers remains part of the interpretation; there is no clock attached to an individual preserved stem.

The silica-rich water supplied a remarkable route to fossilisation.

Minerals coated organisms and permeated their tissues. Over time, the deposits became chert, a rock composed largely of silica. Some plants survived in three dimensions, with cellular organisation still recognisable.

They had not all been reduced to a dark impression on a bedding surface.

The resulting record includes plants, fungi, algae and arthropods. It permits questions about bodies and associations that a collection of detached branches could scarcely answer. The arbuscules on the previous page are one example of the fine structures this preservation retained.

Yet exceptional detail needs its own explanation.

Comparisons with active hot springs show that a geothermal landscape contains different habitats. Water temperature, chemistry and access to water vary across it. Plant-rich Rhynie deposits are interpreted as having formed largely in cooler marginal wetlands, influenced by geothermal water and river flooding.

These comparisons also warn against imagining instant preservation of every cell.

Experiments in modern hot-spring settings have found that mineral permeation of plant tissues can take months. The condition of a fossil can depend on exposure, decay and the progress of mineral deposition. That does not give every Rhynie specimen the same timetable; it identifies processes that a reconstruction must consider.

Good preservation also does not guarantee a representative sample.

Silicification favours organisms present where silica-rich water can reach them. A wetland can preserve delicate bodies extraordinarily well while leaving the surrounding vegetation much less visible.

Spores provide a way to investigate that missing context.

A 2018 study compared spores retained within Rhynie plants with dispersed spores recovered from the wider basin's sedimentary sequence. Matching reproductive structures helped connect known plants with the broader record.

The dispersed assemblages indicated a more diverse regional flora than the plants preserved in the hot-spring deposits alone. Only part of the basin's vegetation inhabited that particular setting.

The study also supported a wider distribution for at least some Rhynie plants. Their presence near the springs did not mean that every species was confined there.

This changes how we read the site.

Rhynie offers an unusually detailed view of local communities, with anatomical relationships preserved inside the rock. It does not provide a complete census of Early Devonian land, or the beginning of every lineage found there.

Its plants instead let us examine how terrestrial bodies were organised.

Among the next questions are how water moved through them, how their growing parts were supported, and how they became attached to the ground.
