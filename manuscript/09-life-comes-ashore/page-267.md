### THE MEAL LEFT BEHIND

A list of animals is not yet a food web. To connect its inhabitants, we need evidence of something passing between them: a fragment swallowed, a plant decomposed, a body equipped to capture another. Some of the most revealing remains at Rhynie are not bodies at all.

They are **coprolites**: fossilised droppings. A study of the Early Devonian chert distinguished small pellets with different shapes and contents. Some held recognisable particles; others contained material without a clear internal structure. The variety supported several kinds of consumer rather than one animal eating one food.[1]

Plant spores occurred in varying abundance. It would be tempting to turn every spore-rich pellet into proof of an animal deliberately harvesting spores. The researchers resisted that shortcut. Comparisons with modern millipede droppings showed that consumers of mixed dead material could also swallow abundant spores. Within the fossil collection, spore-rich and spore-poor pellets graded into one another.[1]

A meal therefore poses two questions. What entered the gut? And was that material selected for itself, or taken in with something else? Preserved contents can answer the first more securely than the second. Nor does a pellet automatically identify its maker. Food, feeding strategy and animal identity are separate parts of the reconstruction.

Decomposition supplied another route through the community. In thin slices of Rhynie chert, researchers described a minute organism called *Perexiflasca tayloriana* attached to the remains of plant cells. Its small, sac-like structures occurred singly and in clusters within degrading tissues. The authors interpreted it as a probable participant in decay, while leaving its precise fungal relationship unresolved.[2]

This is an ecosystem viewed from the inside of a collapsing stem. The plant was not simply scenery surrounding animals. Its tissues became a place where other organisms lived and obtained resources. Preserving that association makes the proposed connection more specific than finding an isolated microbial fossil nearby.

Evidence of predation takes a different form. A 2024 investigation reconstructed the mouthparts of the arachnid *Palaeocharinus* using laser microscopy and comparisons with living relatives. Around its feeding apparatus were successive meshes of bristles: an outer, coarser filter and an inner, finer one.[3]

Together with grasping and cutting structures, those filters supported feeding on prey processed outside the mouth, with liquid food drawn through the apparatus. The anatomy makes a predatory way of life strongly supported. It does not identify the particular animal eaten on a particular day.[3]

Placed together, these records give us a community connected by consumption and decay. Yet the connections have different strengths. A swallowed particle is direct evidence of ingestion. An organism attached to degraded tissue supports a more local ecological association. A reconstructed feeding mechanism establishes capability rather than a witnessed encounter.

The missing links matter, but they do not erase the surviving ones. Life ashore was becoming more than a collection of pioneers. It included ways of obtaining food, processing remains and exploiting other inhabitants. The next question is how confidently those small inhabitants themselves can be identified.
