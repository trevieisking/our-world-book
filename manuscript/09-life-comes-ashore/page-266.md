### STRUCTURES THAT COULD HOLD AIR

A line of footprints can record movement beyond water. A respiratory structure can show how the animal's body was equipped for that movement. The distinction changes the question from where an organism passed to what its anatomy allowed it to do.

The millipede *Pneumodesmus newmani*, from Cowie Harbour in Scotland, preserves small openings interpreted as **spiracles**, entrances to an air-conducting respiratory system. Their position in the body wall supplies evidence of air breathing. A preserved opening is more informative about respiration than a familiar outline of legs alone.

Its age illustrates a separate problem. Dating a fossil requires dating the rocks that contain it and establishing their relationship to other exposures. In 2017, investigators analysed uranium and lead in zircon grains from beds above and below the fossil-bearing horizon. Their results favoured an earliest Devonian age, with uncertainties spanning several million years.

Other work supported an older Silurian assignment. A 2025 geological review examined why the estimates differed: the sampled sections lay in separate blocks divided by faults and differed in sediments and fossils. The reviewers supported a latest Silurian to earliest Devonian placement for the millipede-bearing succession.

Anatomy and chronology therefore need separate checks. Reconsidering the age changes where the fossil fits into the history of land occupation. It does not erase the breathing openings. Equally, recognising those openings does not turn an uncertain geological correlation into an exact date for the origin of air breathing.

The Rhynie chert preserves another kind of respiratory evidence. A study published in 2008 examined an extinct arachnid called *Palaeocharinus*, belonging to the trigonotarbids. Arachnids include living spiders and scorpions, though a trigonotarbid was neither of those familiar animals.

Its **book lungs** contained thin sheets arranged like pages. Researchers combined images taken at successive focal depths through translucent chert to reconstruct the structures in three dimensions. Tiny pillars kept spaces between neighbouring sheets open.

Those supports matter because the spaces could accommodate air. Detailed comparison with living arachnid lungs strengthened the interpretation of a respiratory organ suited to terrestrial life. The evidence concerns a functioning arrangement of surfaces and spaces, rather than a resemblance in overall body shape.

Yet air breathing can also serve an animal that retains an aquatic life. In 2020, investigators scanned an exceptionally preserved Carboniferous eurypterid, one of the extinct arthropods commonly called sea scorpions. The specimen was much younger than the Devonian examples.

Its respiratory sheets carried pillar-like supports that the authors interpreted as permitting breathing outside water. The fossil shows why capacity and habitat cannot be treated as identical. Structures useful during an excursion may exist before an animal spends its whole life ashore.

The comparison reveals several routes through the same physical challenge. Openings, internal air spaces and supports can establish capabilities, while sedimentary setting and other fossils help reconstruct ecological use. Neither a single organ nor a single deposit answers every question.

Once respiration is established, another investigation becomes possible. What were these animals eating, and what connected their lives to the plants, fungi and other organisms around them?
