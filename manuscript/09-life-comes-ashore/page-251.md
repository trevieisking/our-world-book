### RELATIVES IN THE WATER

Which living organisms are closest to land plants?

The answer is not obtained by selecting the alga that looks most like a small tree.

Researchers compare inherited characters, including DNA and the genes expressed by cells, to reconstruct relationships. A broad 2019 study assembled gene-expression data from more than a thousand species across green plants and other related algal lineages. Such sampling helps test relationships across the whole group rather than relying on a few familiar representatives.

Repeated large comparisons support a particular algal group as the nearest living relatives of land plants: the **Zygnematophyceae**, often called zygnematophyte algae.

They include organisms made of single cells and others forming filaments. Their bodies can look much simpler than those of some more distant algal relatives.

This is a relationship between branches of a family tree. Living zygnematophytes have their own long evolutionary history. None is an unchanged specimen of the organism from which land plants arose.

The distinction becomes especially useful when we look inside their cells.

In 2024, researchers reported genomes from four filamentous zygnematophyte algae, including strains of *Zygnema*. A genome is an organism's inherited DNA complement; comparing genomes reveals similarities that outward appearance can conceal.

The algae and land plants shared major enzymes involved in building and modifying cell walls. The study also examined networks of genes whose activity varied together, linking environmental responses with growth and development.

An enzyme is a molecule, usually a protein, that helps a chemical reaction proceed. Finding related enzymes in several branches can support an older origin for parts of their shared cellular equipment.

But a list of genes is not a complete explanation of a body.

When and where genes act, how their products interact and how cells divide all affect what an organism becomes. The authors used the comparisons to infer features of a common ancestor; they did not recover that ancestor's genome directly.

The larger evolutionary pattern also resists a tidy staircase from single cells to filaments to increasingly complex plants.

A 2026 synthesis describes repeated gains, reductions and losses of multicellular organisation within the broader streptophyte group, which includes these algae and land plants. A simple-looking modern body may therefore follow a history involving more complex ancestors.

The detailed reconstructions remain hypotheses tested against the tree and available characters. Their value is that they replace an assumption of inevitable progress with questions about particular changes.

What was retained? What was modified? Which features arose independently?

These comparisons make the transition to land biologically less mysterious. Evolution could alter inherited systems for growth, cell walls and environmental response while also producing new arrangements and functions.

They leave another question open: when were organisms carrying land-plant traits actually present?

DNA can suggest relationships and, with additional models and fossil calibration, approximate times of divergence. Rocks provide a different test: something physically preserved from a particular interval.

For early plants, some of the most informative survivors are much smaller than a stem.

They are spores.
