### A BODY THAT BRANCHED

A slender axis divides into two. Each branch may divide again, ending in a structure that once contained spores.

This is the basic organisation preserved in fossils assigned to *Cooksonia*, among the best-known early branching land plants. Compared with a modern tree, the surviving body seems spare: no spreading crown of leaves, no flower, no woody trunk.

The branches carried spore-producing structures at their tips. Several could belong to one connected body, revealing an organisation that isolated spores cannot show.

Cooksonia-like bodies occur in Silurian rocks, broadly around 430 million years ago and later. They do not mark the beginning of all plant life on land. The older reproductive record has already taken us beyond that point.

Nor does a familiar silhouette settle every anatomical question.

Compression fossils can preserve outlines while losing internal tissues. A dark central line might suggest a conducting strand, but identifying particular cell types requires better preservation.

In 1992, researchers described Early Devonian specimens of *Cooksonia pertoni* containing central tubes with differentially thickened walls characteristic of water-conducting cells. That discovery supplied direct anatomical support for its vascular organisation.

**Vascular** plants possess specialised tissues for internal transport. This finding belongs to the examined species and specimens; it cannot simply be transferred to every older branching fragment that resembles them.

Size introduces a further complication.

The visible spore-bearing plant is one generation in the life cycle, the **sporophyte**. Another generation, the **gametophyte**, produces the sexual cells. In different living plant groups, these generations differ greatly in size and in how much they depend on one another.

A fossil of a sporophyte does not guarantee that its gametophyte has also survived.

In 2008, a physiological analysis asked how much tissue a very narrow Cooksonia-like axis could contain. Protection, support and transport all require space. In the smallest axes, there might be too little room left for enough photosynthetic tissue to sustain the body independently.

The proposed implication was dependence on an unpreserved gametophyte. Larger axes allowed a different possibility: sufficient space for photosynthetic independence. A 2018 study of a Silurian cooksonioid from the Czech Republic supported that possibility for its comparatively substantial sporophytes.

These are anatomical and physiological arguments, not measurements of photosynthesis in a living fossil plant. They show why one reconstruction cannot stand for every size and species.

Even the dates need their own evidence.

A 2024 study examined zircon grains from Cooksonia-bearing sediments in Wales and Ireland. Uranium–lead dating of the youngest suitable grains constrained the maximum age of deposition: sediment cannot have accumulated before its constituent crystals formed.

Such a limit is not an exact age measured from the plant itself. It must be considered alongside the fossils and geological context.

Branching bodies, conducting cells, size and dated sediment each answer a different question. Together they reveal more of terrestrial plant life without filling every missing part.

Other inhabitants of these developing ecosystems grew by a different means.

Their history leads us into fungi.
