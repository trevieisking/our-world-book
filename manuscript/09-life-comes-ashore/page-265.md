### TRACKS WITHOUT THEIR MAKERS

An animal can leave evidence of a journey without leaving its body. Rows of impressions in stone record contacts between moving limbs and a surface. These are **trace fossils**: preserved products of activity, rather than the remains of the organism itself.

A trackway immediately raises two questions. What made it, and what was the surface like when it was made? Both matter when investigating the arrival of animals on land. Walking across a submerged bottom and crossing exposed ground can leave broadly similar evidence.

In 2002, researchers described arthropod trackways in the Nepean Formation near Kingston, Ontario. **Arthropods** are animals with jointed appendages and an external skeleton. The relevant sandstones belong to the Cambrian–Ordovician record, long before the Devonian forests already examined.

The surrounding rock carries evidence of wind: dune bedding and structures produced as sand moved or adhered to damp surfaces. These features support movement across a dune landscape, probably near the sea. The authors interpreted the makers as amphibious arthropods, able to operate between water and exposed ground. Their precise identity remained uncertain.

Many impressions were **undertracks**, disturbances transmitted into sediment below the actual walking surface. What survives may therefore be an indirect version of a footfall, filtered through the ground beneath it.

Experiments help investigate that filter. A 2007 study compared tracks made by living cockroaches, scorpions, spiders, millipedes and woodlice on substrates differing in grain size and moisture. Changing the surface changed which impressions appeared and how clearly they survived.

Different animals could produce similar patterns under some conditions. Conversely, one kind of animal could leave tracks that looked different as the substrate changed. The experiments also examined disturbances beneath the surface. A fossil trackway's appearance consequently reflects the animal, its movement and the material receiving the impressions.

Sedimentary context can alter an entire interpretation. A 2019 investigation revisited Ordovician tracks in northern England's Borrowdale Volcanic Group. Researchers found the traces on layers of volcanic ash, with structures indicating deposition underwater. In one example, tracks crossed short exposed portions of ripple crests.

The authors interpreted this as evidence that the makers could tolerate brief movement outside water. It did not demonstrate a community living permanently on dry ground. Their argument depends on combining the traces with the enclosing deposits, rather than treating a recognisable pattern of feet as sufficient proof of habitat.

A broader 2022 review likewise distinguished evidence of terrestrial activity from the timing of fully terrestrial lineages. Molecular estimates can suggest histories older than the surviving body fossils, but estimated evolutionary splits do not directly reveal the setting of a particular journey.

These records make the transition tangible while keeping its stages separate. A track confirms activity where its maker passed. It cannot alone establish where the animal fed throughout its life, reproduced or obtained oxygen.

The next evidence must come closer to the animal itself. If a fossil preserves an opening or an internal structure used in respiration, it may tell us more about the environment in which that body could function.
