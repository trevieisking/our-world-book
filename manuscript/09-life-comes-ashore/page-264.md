### A LANDSCAPE THAT LIFE COULD CHANGE

A forest map records where plants stood. A landscape history asks what changed because they stood there. Roots, fallen material and fungal partners could affect the movement of water and the chemistry of the ground. Investigating those effects requires evidence beyond the outlines of individual plants.

One route follows ancient rivers. A 2012 synthesis compared river deposits across the Palaeozoic, finding changes in channel form alongside the spread of land vegetation. Late Silurian and Devonian deposits increasingly record confined channels, meandering rivers and muddy floodplains.

Roots can strengthen a bank by binding sediment. Fine sediment also contributes cohesion, so vegetation works within a physical system rather than determining its shape alone. Modern observations and laboratory channels support this mechanism. Their value is experimental: they help test how bank strength changes flowing water's work. They do not reproduce every condition of an ancient river.

Another route begins at a mineral surface. **Chemical weathering** changes minerals through reactions, including dissolution that releases their constituents. Plants and fungi can influence these reactions while obtaining nutrients.

In experiments reported in 2015, researchers grew a living liverwort with and without fungal partners and compared mineral changes with plant-free controls. Calcium and phosphorus release from basalt increased in the planted systems; fungal associations altered the response. The controlled comparison connects biological activity with measurable mineral change.

The liverwort is a living experimental organism, not a recovered Devonian plant. The study also considered how much ground different kinds of vegetation could influence. A shallow system and a deep forest root network cannot be assigned the same landscape effect simply because both produce weathering.

These processes connect with a larger proposal, the **Devonian plant hypothesis**: expanding forests and complex roots helped change the long-term carbon cycle by increasing mineral weathering. Weathering of particular silicate minerals can contribute to atmospheric carbon dioxide removal through a chain of reactions involving water, dissolved material and eventual carbonate formation.

Testing that chain requires links between different records. A 2015 research framework proposed combining fossil plants, ancient soil chemistry and experiments with living organisms. A root trace establishes occupation of soil; it does not by itself measure the rate of mineral dissolution or the amount of carbon dioxide removed worldwide.

Climate adds another interaction. In a coupled climate and carbon-cycle model published in 2011, vegetation-related changes in soil properties counteracted cooling associated with simulated carbon dioxide decline. The result depended on the processes and conditions represented in the model. It illustrates why a forest's climatic influence cannot be inferred from one chemical pathway alone.

The evidence therefore has different scales. Experiments can isolate a mechanism. Sediments and fossil soils can preserve local consequences. Global calculations explore whether those mechanisms, extended across reconstructed landscapes, could produce broader change. Moving between these scales requires assumptions that must remain visible.

Vegetation helped create new terrestrial settings, while rivers and soils continued to shape where plants could grow. The next investigation turns to the animals within those changing settings. What evidence shows how they moved, fed and exchanged gases on land?
