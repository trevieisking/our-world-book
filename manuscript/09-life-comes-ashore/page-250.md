### GROUND BEFORE ROOTS

What remains of a landscape whose inhabitants were mainly microscopic?

Sometimes a dark layer draped over ancient gravel.

In South Africa, rocks of the Moodies Group preserve carbon-rich layers among conglomerates and sandstones roughly 3.22 billion years old. A conglomerate contains rounded pebbles or larger fragments held within finer material.

In a 2018 study, researchers interpreted particular deposits as the products of rivers. The dark layers were identified as fossil microbial mats: communities growing together at a sediment surface.

The case combined evidence from the outcrop, microscopic examination, carbon chemistry and Raman spectroscopy, a method that uses scattered light to investigate material structure. These tests supported both the biological interpretation and the conclusion that the organic material belonged to the ancient deposits, rather than arriving much later.

Nearby mats in tidal deposits offered a comparison. Their carbon and nitrogen isotope compositions differed from those in the interpreted river setting, suggesting differences in biological cycling between habitats.

That chemical contrast does not identify every microbe. Nor does it establish a permanently dry community. Rivers are continental environments containing water, and mats can occupy wet surfaces within them.

The significance is still considerable: biological activity in a continental landscape long before roots, leaves or land animals.

Another archive lies beneath former ground surfaces.

A **palaeosol** is an ancient soil preserved in the geological record. Researchers identify it through combinations of structure, mineral changes, layering and its relationship to surrounding rocks. In younger examples, roots and burrows can provide further clues. Those features cannot simply be expected in a world before the organisms that made them.

Weathering itself also requires care. Water can dissolve or transform minerals without any organism taking part. An altered rock surface therefore does not establish life merely because it resembles soil.

Organic matter supplies additional evidence, but its survival is selective. Association with minerals can help preserve it, while later chemical alteration can remove or modify it. A carbon-poor ancient soil may have lost evidence of organisms that once occupied it.

Conversely, surviving organic material needs checks on its origin and history before it becomes a reliable biological record.

Living microbial communities help explain what such small inhabitants can accomplish.

Studies of young volcanic surfaces show microbes establishing themselves before a developed plant cover. Some use chemical energy from their surroundings; others contribute organic matter through photosynthesis. Their activities can alter mineral surfaces and influence the conditions encountered by later arrivals.

These modern systems provide mechanisms to investigate, not a photograph of an ancient ecosystem. Their atmosphere, organisms and geological circumstances differ from those billions of years ago.

The ground was thus already an arena of chemistry and biology before plants built taller habitats above it.

The next change did not require photosynthesis to be invented again. It required a lineage already capable of using light to acquire new ways of growing, developing and reproducing.

To follow that lineage, we turn to the living relatives that still connect the plant world with algae.
