### THE HAND INSIDE THE FIN

Follow a human arm outward from the shoulder and its skeleton changes from one long bone to two, then to smaller bones at the wrist and fingers. The beginning of that arrangement did not originate with a hand reaching across dry ground. Corresponding bones occur inside the paired fins of lobe-finned fishes.[1]

The harder question lies near the tip. How did a fin's internal skeleton become a structure bearing fingers and toes? A fossil can preserve an intermediate combination without telling us that its owner was our direct ancestor. What matters first is the arrangement of the parts.

For years, *Panderichthys*, a Devonian fish close to the fin-to-limb transition, seemed to lack small outer bones comparable with digits. In 2008, a computed-tomography study examined an undisturbed pectoral fin. The scan showed that a plate-like structure in earlier reconstructions was misleading. Smaller bones called **radials** were present toward the fin's outer end.[1]

This was a correction to the evidence, not simply a change of vocabulary. A supposedly missing part of the evolutionary pattern had been concealed by the way the specimen was understood. Scanning allowed investigators to test that interpretation while the bones remained in their original arrangement.

A more extensive discovery followed in a study published in 2020. A connected specimen of *Elpistostege watsoni* from Canada measured 1.57 metres long. High-energy scans revealed successive rows of bones within its pectoral fin, including outer elements arranged as digits and possible digits.[2]

Yet fin rays remained beyond those internal bones. The appendage was not a free hand with bare fingers projecting from it. The importance of the specimen lay in that combination: a hand-like skeletal arrangement enclosed within an aquatic fin. The authors proposed that major components of the vertebrate hand arose in this setting.[2]

Bones preserve the result of development. Experiments on living animals can investigate part of the machinery that produces it. A 2025 study compared DNA regions controlling **Hoxd genes**, genes involved in organising developing structures, in zebrafish and mice.[3]

Removing a particular control region from zebrafish did not abolish the relevant gene activity in the outer developing fin, unlike the corresponding effect in mouse digits. It did remove activity in the fish's cloacal region, associated with the rear outlets of the body. Related regulatory activity was also found in the mouse urogenital system.[3]

The researchers proposed that an older system controlling this body region had been recruited into digit development during tetrapod evolution. This is a hypothesis about the reuse of developmental regulation, not a claim that fingers grew from a cloaca. The experiments establish functions in the animals tested; reconstructing the evolutionary sequence requires comparison and inference.[3]

Together, fossils and experiments replace an abrupt transformation with a set of questions that can be tested separately. Which bones were already present? How were they rearranged? Which developmental controls changed their work? And when did those altered appendages become capable of supporting a body against the ground?

That final question requires more than recognising a finger-like shape. It leads to a fish whose shoulder, joints and fins reveal another part of the transition.
