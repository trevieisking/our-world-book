### DIFFERENT FORESTS, DIFFERENT ROOTS

A canopy can conceal differences beneath it. Two forests with upright trees may draw on the ground through very different structures. To investigate an extinct forest's ecological reach, the height of its trunks is only a beginning.

Near Cairo in New York, researchers mapped roots within a **palaeosol**, an ancient soil preserved in the geological record. Their study, published in a 2020 journal issue, identified root systems comparable to those of the Gilboa cladoxylopsids. It also revealed much more extensively branching systems assigned to the group containing *Archaeopteris*.

Large structural roots extended metres from their centres, divided repeatedly and connected with smaller roots. Cores drilled through the soil showed associated root traces reaching beyond a metre in depth. The combination of surface mapping and vertical sampling matters: a plan of the quarry floor alone cannot reveal the entire underground volume.

The identification combines root form with knowledge of other fossils. It does not mean that every Cairo root retains diagnostic wood or an attached leafy crown. Nevertheless, the branching and scale provide strong evidence for an underground system capable of occupying much more ground than a compact cluster around a trunk base.

*Archaeopteris* helps explain why familiar and unfamiliar characteristics can coexist. Anatomically preserved remains studied in 1999 showed branching and wood arrangements comparable in several respects to later trees. Yet the plant reproduced through spores. Similarity in growth does not make it a seed plant or establish it as the direct ancestor of a living species.

A different forest emerged from investigations in Svalbard. In 2015, researchers described dense stands of tree-sized lycophytes preserved upright, with swollen bases and small, ribbon-like roots. Spores in the surrounding deposits supported an early Late Devonian age, revising an earlier assignment to the very end of the period.

Here, the association between standing plants, roots, spores and sediments supplies both an ecological picture and a chronological test. Wet-soil vegetation in a local basin differed from the extensively branching systems at Cairo. Neither site represents a compulsory stage through which every forest passed.

The sequence of discoveries also changes what “earliest” means. A 2024 study reported forest evidence in the Hangman Sandstone of southwest England, from an earlier part of the Middle Devonian, around 390 million years ago. Fossils attributed to *Calamophyton* occurred alongside rooting features and structures recording interactions between plants and sediment.

That finding extends the surviving forest record beyond the ages of the New York sites. It does not date the first moment trees grew together. A newly found surface can move the known record backward without identifying the evolutionary origin itself.

The English deposits also illustrate a sampling problem. Different parts of their river system preserved different amounts of evidence for vegetation. Where conditions favoured burial, plant debris and altered sediment survived; elsewhere, the same kinds of interaction could leave a poorer record.

The comparison therefore has two results. Early forests differed in plant membership and rooting strategy, and their visibility depends on geological preservation. Their roots and debris did more than occupy a landscape. They helped change what water carried, what soil retained and how the landscape developed.
