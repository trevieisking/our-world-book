### EIGHT DIGITS, STILL IN WATER

Count the digits of *Acanthostega* and a familiar expectation breaks down. Its forelimbs and hindlimbs bore eight, not five. This Late Devonian animal from East Greenland possessed unmistakably digit-bearing appendages, but their presence did not make its body a ready-made version of a modern land vertebrate.[1]

A detailed anatomical study described a flattened hindlimb suited to a paddle-like role. Other parts of the skeleton combined features associated with fishes and with limbed vertebrates. The combination matters more than an attempt to divide the animal into two halves. Its structures belonged to one functioning body.[1]

The number eight also cautions against reading evolution backwards. Five digits may look like the obvious foundation when we begin with our own hands. A fossil with another arrangement shows that the history was not constrained to begin with our particular outcome. Recognising this does not require us to know the exact developmental process that set each ancient digit in place.

Evidence of breathing supplies a different test. In 1991, investigators described bones supporting the gill region of *Acanthostega*. Some carried grooves comparable with those accommodating blood vessels in living fishes. A plate along the shoulder girdle resembled a structure supporting the rear of a fish's gill chamber.[2]

Together, these features supported retention of internal gills and aquatic respiration. The evidence was not a soft gill still exchanging oxygen. It was a skeletal arrangement whose relationship to breathing could be tested through anatomical comparison. Limbs with digits and a fish-like breathing apparatus existed in the same animal.[2]

Even that body has another dimension: age. A large specimen is not necessarily an adult. To investigate growth rather than infer maturity from size alone, researchers examined upper-arm bones from the Stensiö Bjerg mass-death deposit using highly detailed X-ray imaging.[3]

Their 2016 study reconstructed microscopic structures inside the bones and concluded that the sampled animals, including the largest individuals in the deposit, were juveniles. Much growth preceded substantial bone formation in the limbs. A later, slower-growing juvenile stage with bony limbs lasted at least six years in some individuals.[3]

The delayed development of those bones supported a prolonged aquatic juvenile life. Differences between individuals also warned against using a single body measurement as a universal age indicator. The study concerned the animals preserved in that assemblage; it did not reveal every stage of the species' life.[3]

This changes the questions we ask of a fossil collection. Were adults living elsewhere? Did habitat use change during growth? Did one event preferentially preserve a particular age group? An assemblage can be rich in specimens yet selective in the lives it represents. More bodies do not automatically supply the missing ages.

The evidence therefore joins rather than cancels the earlier discoveries. Digit-bearing limbs could be useful within an aquatic life, while other lineages assembled different capacities. Neither an underwater footprint nor a juvenile skeleton supplies a universal account of every early tetrapod.

To learn how another body moved, we must turn from counting digits to measuring joints. The next animal had limbs too, but could it actually place them like a walking salamander?
