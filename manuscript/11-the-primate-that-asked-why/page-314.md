### UPRIGHT IS NOT ONE MOVEMENT

A body held upright can be supported in several ways. Arms may carry much of its weight beneath branches, while legs support it from below in another posture. Recognising the capacity for one arrangement does not establish every movement that a reconstructed animal might be shown performing.

The Miocene ape *Danuvius guggenmosi*, described in 2019 from Bavaria, brought these distinctions into focus. Complete limb bones occurred within a fossil sample assigned to about 11.6 million years ago. Madelaine Böhme and colleagues interpreted their combination with trunk remains as evidence of movement with extended arms and legs.[1]

They called the proposed behaviour extended-limb clambering. In their reconstruction, features relevant to suspension accompanied others associated with extended hips and knees. The resulting animal offered a possible model for earlier stages in the shared history of great apes and humans.[1]

A model for an ancestor, however, is not an identified ancestor. Nor is the proposed ability to use extended limbs equivalent to a modern human walking across open ground. Both the mechanics and the evolutionary placement need to be established before the wider story can follow.

In 2020, Scott Williams and colleagues challenged the evidence for the locomotor interpretation. A reply from Böhme and co-authors identified the disputed points: hip mechanics, the orientation of the lower shin bone at the ankle and the reconstruction of the lower back from surviving vertebrae.[2,3]

These are not disagreements about whether the fossils exist. They concern how the parts fitted and what movements that arrangement permitted. Assigning a vertebra differently within the backbone can change the reconstruction of the trunk even when everyone is examining the same piece of bone.

The published exchange therefore belongs beside the proposed gait. It prevents a striking reconstruction from becoming more certain merely through repetition. The appropriate question is which anatomical observations distinguish the alternatives, rather than which imagined movement most resembles our own.

A different fossil offers evidence from the pelvis. Also in 2019, Carol Ward and colleagues described a partial hipbone attributed to *Rudapithecus hungaricus*, from Hungary, roughly ten million years old. They measured three-dimensional models and compared the preserved regions with those of other primates.[4]

Its hip socket and pelvic structure supported an upright trunk and movements involving climbing and suspension. Yet it lacked the elongated lower part of the ilium, the broad upper pelvic bone, found in living great apes. The proposed body therefore differed from the modern forms commonly used to reconstruct it.[4]

The researchers argued that some pelvic specialisations of living great apes arose separately in their lineages. This strengthened the case against assuming that our shared ancestor had exactly the body of a living chimpanzee. It did not show that *Rudapithecus* was that ancestor or recover its entire daily range of movements.[4]

The two investigations leave us with a branching set of anatomical possibilities, not a single ladder of increasingly human poses. A posture can be ancient while a particular way of walking is later, and similar functions can involve different combinations of structures.

To connect these bodies with the living branches, we need another comparison. Genomes preserve ancestry on a scale no single joint can reveal—but their different regions do not always record precisely the same tree.
