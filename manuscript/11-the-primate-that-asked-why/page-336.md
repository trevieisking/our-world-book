### A DIFFERENT BODY, NOT YET OURS

By around two million years ago, the hominin record contains another form whose importance is easy to exaggerate in the wrong way.

**Homo erectus** was not the first biped. It was not the first hominin to make stone tools. It may not even have been the first member of **Homo**.

What changed was the combination.

At **Drimolen** in South Africa, the juvenile cranium DNH 134 comes from deposits dated to roughly **2.04–1.95 million years ago**. Its preserved anatomy has clear *Homo erectus* affinities, making it one of the earliest known crania attributed to the species.[1]

In East Turkana, Kenya, the fragmentary occipital KNM-ER 2598 provides another very early record. Reinvestigation of its original locality supports an age older than about **1.85 million years**, consistent with earlier estimates near 1.9 million years.[2]

Already, therefore, the earliest record is geographically broad.

But skull fragments do not explain why *H. erectus* would become so important to later human history.

For that, one of the most informative fossils is **KNM-WT 15000**, the juvenile skeleton from Nariokotome in Kenya, roughly 1.5–1.6 million years old.

Its limbs show a profound shift from the proportions seen in australopiths. The legs are long relative to the trunk. Biomechanical work has shown that such proportions are compatible with efficient upright walking and carrying over greater distances than the shorter-legged australopith condition.[3]

This does not mean the entire body had become modern.

A 2020 three-dimensional reconstruction of the Nariokotome thorax found a **short, wide and deep rib cage**, substantially different from the shallower thorax of living *Homo sapiens*.[4] The authors argued that the fully modern human trunk shape evolved later.

That is a critical correction.

Pictures of *Homo erectus* often emphasise long legs and a recognisably human silhouette. Those features are real, but they can hide the remaining anatomical differences. Evolution changed limb proportions, pelvis, trunk, growth and skull anatomy on partly independent schedules.

Even Nariokotome's adult size is not a fixed number. Because the individual died young and did not follow exactly the same growth schedule as a modern child, estimates of final height and mass depend on the growth model used.[5]

The safer conclusion is functional rather than cosmetic.

Compared with australopiths, **Homo erectus had a body increasingly suited to sustained terrestrial movement across larger landscapes**. It was still not simply a modern human with a smaller brain.

And the geography soon becomes as important as the anatomy.

Within a surprisingly short interval of the early African *H. erectus* record, hominin fossils and artefacts appear far beyond Africa.

The next page follows them to **Dmanisi in Georgia**, where five very different skulls force another uncomfortable question: how much variation can one travelling human species contain?
