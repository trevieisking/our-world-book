### A CROSSING NO ONE SAW

An ocean crossing leaves an unusual evidential problem. We may find the descendants on one continent and their relatives on another without recovering anything from the journey itself. The route must be reconstructed from relationships, geography and time, rather than supplied by the apparent boldness of the undertaking.

The living monkeys of Central and South America belong to the platyrrhine branch. Their history is not necessarily the history of every anthropoid that reached the continent. Some immigrants may have founded lineages that later disappeared, leaving no living representatives to reveal their presence.[1,3]

In 2020, Erik Seiffert and colleagues described *Ucayalipithecus* from Santa Rosa in Amazonian Peru. Their analysis placed it within the parapithecoids, a group otherwise known from Afro-Arabia, rather than within the branch containing living New World monkeys.[1]

That result supported a separate arrival. The reconstructed geography required a substantial sea crossing, and the researchers proposed transport on floating vegetation. The relationship supplied evidence for an African connection; rafting supplied a mechanism that might explain it. Neither a boat-like mat nor the animals travelling on it had been fossilised at the destination.[1]

The absence of a witnessed journey does not make every route equally plausible. A useful explanation must fit the family tree and the geography, and must allow living animals to reach the other side. But estimating the possibility of a crossing is different from recovering its departure point, duration or passengers.

The rocks introduced a further complication. Santa Rosa had initially been assigned to the Eocene, largely through comparisons of its mammals with other faunas. In 2021, Kenneth Campbell and colleagues analysed zircon grains carried into the deposit and argued instead for an Oligocene age.[2]

Their reported composite maximum was approximately 29.6 million years, while some grains were younger. Such a constraint is not an exact date when a primate died. Transported minerals can predate their final sediment, and a dated fossil locality can still be much younger than the dispersal that first established its inhabitants.[2]

A Brazilian tooth added another proposed branch in 2023. Laurent Marivaux and colleagues described *Ashaninkacebus simpsoni*, assigning its distinctive upper molar to a group with Asian and African affinities. Their analysis suggested that a further anthropoid lineage had reached South America.[3]

The specimen's context imposes a particular limit. It was recovered from recently redeposited river sediment, and its original source farther upstream was unknown. Comparisons with other fossils and evolutionary dating informed its proposed ancient age; the surrounding young sediment was not evidence that this extinct primate had survived into recent times.[3]

The finding expands the question without closing it. A molar can preserve enough detail to support a relationship, yet leave both the original burial layer and much of the animal's body unknown. More complete material could test the placement on which the additional dispersal depends.

These records make South America's primate history richer than one crossing followed by an uninterrupted march towards living monkeys. They also preserve the distinction between evidence that immigrants arrived and certainty about how each arrived. We now return to another anthropoid branch: the shared history from which Old World monkeys and apes emerged.
