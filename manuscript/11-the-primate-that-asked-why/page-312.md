### MORE THAN ONE APE LANDSCAPE

The place where a fossil is found and the environment in which its owner lived are different kinds of evidence. Neither automatically identifies the birthplace of a lineage. Early ape history requires us to investigate all three without drawing a complete ancestral landscape around a single jaw.

In March 2026, Shorouq Al-Ashqar and colleagues described *Masripithecus* from northern Egypt. Its lower-jaw remains came from Early Miocene deposits assigned to approximately 17–18 million years ago, adding an ape to a regional record previously dominated by other kinds of primates.[1]

The researchers combined anatomical characters, molecular information from living species and fossil ages in a dated evolutionary analysis. Their result placed the new form closer to the group containing living apes than the East African fossil apes of comparable age included in the comparison.[1]

They proposed that this living-ape group might have originated in northeastern Afro-Arabia. The fossil strengthened the importance of a poorly sampled region, but did not prove an exact birthplace or identify the common ancestor itself. A new point on the map can expose the limitations of the old map without supplying every missing point.

Ecological reconstruction requires evidence of the surroundings. At Rusinga Island in Kenya, a study published in 2014 connected ape teeth with an ancient soil containing casts of tree stumps and roots. Fossil leaves provided another record of the vegetation.[2]

The combined evidence supported a dense seasonal tropical forest with a closed canopy and several vegetation layers. Instead of placing an ape into a habitat inferred from a distant or unrelated deposit, the investigation linked its remains to the local forest record.[2]

Even that association is not a preserved feeding episode. It cannot tell us which particular tree the animal climbed or whether every part of its range had the same cover. Its strength is more specific: the fossil and the environmental evidence can be considered within the same geological context.

Moroto in Uganda revealed a different arrangement. In 2023, Laura MacLatchy and colleagues combined environmental indicators with fossils from a site dated to about 21 million years ago. Their reconstruction supported seasonally dry woodland containing abundant grasses, rather than an unbroken closed forest.[3]

The ape *Morotopithecus* showed evidence interpreted as leaf feeding on vegetation affected by water stress. Bones from the site also supported locomotor capacities associated with apes. The authors connected these findings with a proposed origin of versatile movement during feeding in heterogeneous woodland.[3]

That proposal does not make every early ape a woodland leaf-eater. Nor does abundant grass establish a grass diet for the ape or the absence of trees. The environmental mixture and the animal's requirements must be distinguished before one can explain the other.

The Kenyan and Ugandan records are not competing photographs of the same forest. They sample different places, animals and intervals. Taken together, they show why a habitat explanation must accommodate variation, while the Egyptian discovery shows why the geographical sample itself remains incomplete.

To understand the emerging bodies, we must also turn from surroundings to development. One conspicuous feature shared by living apes is something missing: an external tail. How can bones and inherited DNA help investigate a loss?
