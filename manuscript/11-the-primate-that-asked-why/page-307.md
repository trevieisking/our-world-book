### A WARMING WORLD, AN UNCERTAIN ROUTE

A small primate can leave fossils on several continents without leaving a record of the journey between them. Reconstructing its spread requires two comparisons at once: whether the animals really belong together, and whether the rocks containing them can be placed in a reliable sequence.

Near the beginning of the Eocene, roughly 56 million years ago, a pronounced warming interval affected the planet. The event is called the **Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum**. Early records of the primate *Teilhardina* occur in rocks associated with this disturbance in Asia, Europe and North America.[1,2]

In 2006, Thierry Smith, Kenneth Rose and Philip Gingerich compared these occurrences using changes in carbon-isotope composition. The changing proportions of carbon's lighter and heavier forms provided a pattern against which fossil-bearing layers could be correlated across continents.[1]

Their reconstruction placed Asian specimens earlier in the sequence, followed by European and then North American ones. Anatomical comparisons supported an Asian origin and westward dispersal. The authors estimated that the sequence fitted within about 25,000 years, a remarkably brief interval in this part of the fossil record.[1]

That number was not a measured journey time for an individual animal. It depended on correlations among deposits and the interpretation of the fossils within them. A route linking sampled places also need not include every population, detour or unsampled region through which the lineage passed.

More teeth changed the anatomical comparison. Paul Morse and colleagues reported a much larger sample of *Teilhardina brandti* in a study published online in 2018 and in a 2019 journal issue. Previously unrecognised variation blurred distinctions that had separated some named species.[2]

Their revised analysis did not uniquely favour an Asian birthplace. It gave similar support to an origin in Asia or North America. The fossils had not stopped occurring on different continents; what changed was the relationship inferred among them and, consequently, the geographical history mapped onto it.[2]

The environment also resisted a simple story. A warmer planet might permit movement into regions formerly too cold for a population, but warmth alone does not guarantee suitable forest all along the route. Vegetation must be investigated independently rather than added as a continuous green background.

A 2026 study led by Regan Dunn reconstructed forest canopy density and plant composition through the thermal maximum in Wyoming. Leaf-based indicators and microscopic plant remains supported an abrupt opening of forest canopies, increased erosion and a shift towards communities richer in ferns and palms.[3]

This is evidence from a particular regional record, not proof that every primate crossed an identical damaged landscape. It does show why the environmental consequences cannot be reduced to universally improved habitat. Temperature, forest structure and food availability are separate conditions, and each may change on a different schedule.

The proposed dispersal therefore sits at the meeting of taxonomy, chronology and ecology. Greater precision in one does not repair uncertainty in the others automatically. A newly identified species, a revised correlation or a better vegetation record can alter the reconstructed route without erasing the evidence for widespread early primates.

The next fossil offers a different advantage. Instead of extending a distribution through more isolated teeth, it preserves much of a small primate's body together. What can that association reveal about the early branches of the primate family tree?
