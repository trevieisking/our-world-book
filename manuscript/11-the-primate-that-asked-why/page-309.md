### SMALL TEETH, WIDE CONNECTIONS

A tooth may fit in a fingertip while connecting the histories of two continents. Its ridges and hollows provide characters that can be compared with those of other animals. The challenge is to discover whether a resemblance records common ancestry, similar feeding demands or an incomplete view of a more complicated body.

The anthropoid branch contains monkeys and apes, including humans. Its early history cannot be reconstructed simply by beginning with the places where its living members occur. Fossils from Asia and Africa preserve combinations that no living species represents unchanged.[1,2]

In 2012, Yaowalak Chaimanee and colleagues described *Afrasia djijidae* from the Eocene of Myanmar. Its teeth closely resembled those of *Afrotarsius*, known from northern Africa, while retaining less specialised features. Their evolutionary analysis placed the two as close relatives within an early anthropoid group.[1]

That relationship supported a connection between populations on opposite sides of the geographical divide. The authors favoured dispersal from Asia into Africa. The fossils did not preserve the crossing, identify a particular travelling population or make one named species the direct ancestor of the other. Those are further propositions, not details contained automatically in a similar molar.

A discovery published in 2019 broadened the Asian evidence. *Aseanpithecus myanmarensis* preserved part of an upper jaw, including the front margin of an eye socket, with a distinctive combination of facial and dental features. Some resembled characters recorded in African anthropoids and their close relatives.[2]

A matching lower-jaw fragment and a molar were found nearby. Their size and anatomy supported attribution to the same species, but the researchers considered it unlikely that all the pieces came from one individual. Proximity did not turn separate discoveries into an associated skeleton.[2]

The distinction affects how the body is reconstructed, while the character comparisons affect where it belongs in the tree. The authors argued that several anthropoid lineages, rather than one founding population alone, could have moved into Africa. Different branches might have contributed different combinations of inherited features.

New African evidence continued to change that comparison. In 2026, Jean-Jacques Jaeger and colleagues described *Saharopithecus salemi* from Dur At-Talah in Libya. Two upper molars distinguished the new form, increasing the documented anthropoid diversity at the locality to at least four taxa.[3]

Its mixture of dental features suggested connections with more than one early African group. The authors retained uncertainty over its precise evolutionary placement while favouring multiple dispersals from Asia. The alternative—a single arrival followed by substantial diversification within Africa—remained a scenario against which the evidence had to be tested.[3]

A richer fossil assemblage therefore does two jobs. It establishes more local diversity, and it changes the comparisons used to explain how that diversity arose. The first is not dependent on recovering every detail of the second. Uncertainty over the route does not erase the teeth or their differences.

These discoveries concern the deep history of anthropoids, not the much later geographical origin of our own species. Keeping those scales distinct prevents a continental label from concealing tens of millions of years of branching. The next geographical problem reaches farther still: how did anthropoid relatives become established across an ocean, in South America?
