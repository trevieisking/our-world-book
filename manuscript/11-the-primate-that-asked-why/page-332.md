### WHEN FLAKES BECAME A SYSTEM

A sharp-edged stone can be produced by accident. A repeated pattern of cores, flakes and striking decisions is harder to explain that way.

Around **2.6 million years ago**, such patterns become much easier to see across eastern Africa.

For many years, the classic early record came from **Gona**, Ethiopia. In 1997, Sileshi Semaw and colleagues reported assemblages securely bracketed around 2.6–2.5 million years ago. The artefacts showed controlled fracture of stone and resembled much younger Oldowan technology closely enough that the authors were struck by how accomplished the early knappers already appeared.[1]

In 2019, another site changed the lower boundary again.

At **Bokol Dora 1** in the Ledi-Geraru research area, David Braun and colleagues excavated systematically flaked artefacts from a horizon constrained to approximately **2.61–2.58 million years ago**. Quantitative comparisons placed the assemblage closer to Oldowan variation than to the older Lomekwi 3 material or stone products made experimentally by modern nonhuman primates.[2]

The distinction matters.

Lomekwi, at about 3.3 million years ago, includes heavy pounding and large-core reduction. Bokol Dora and Gona show a more systematic production of sharp flakes. That does not require one technology to have descended directly from the other. The known archaeological record is too sparse to demonstrate an unbroken chain of teaching from Lomekwi to the Oldowan.[2]

Nor is “Oldowan” one fixed recipe.

Different raw materials fracture differently. Different tasks reward different edges and hammering surfaces. Even the oldest assemblages contain variation. A technological tradition can therefore be recognisable without every toolmaker making identical objects.

The dating itself also became part of a methodological discussion. Braun and colleagues used volcanic ash and magnetic polarity to place Bokol Dora just before the Gauss–Matuyama magnetic reversal. Critics responded that small differences in stratigraphic placement and chronological interpretation mattered greatly when claiming an “earliest” site separated from Gona by only a short interval.[3]

That criticism illustrates why ranking sites is less important than understanding the behaviour.

Since the Bokol Dora publication, Nyayanga has extended the known early Oldowan record farther back within a roughly 3.03–2.58-million-year deposit sequence. The title “earliest known Oldowan” therefore changed as new evidence appeared.

The behavioural threshold is more durable than the ranking.

By around 2.6 million years ago—and apparently earlier at Nyayanga—hominins repeatedly selected stone, struck cores and produced useful sharp edges. This was no longer one isolated object whose accidental origin had to be excluded. It was an archaeological pattern repeated across places.

But we still cannot attach that pattern securely to one genus.

The first fossil assigned to **Homo** predates Bokol Dora. *Paranthropus* occurs beside early Oldowan material at Nyayanga. Tools tell us that somebody possessed the behaviour.

They do not tell us that only one branch did.

The next fossils make that biological diversity impossible to ignore. Around two million years ago, several forms conventionally called early **Homo** appear together—and deciding how many species they represent becomes its own problem.
