### A BODY IS NOT A FAMILY TREE

An associated skeleton resolves a problem that isolated fossils leave open: which parts belonged together? It can establish that a particular foot accompanied a particular skull. But even a remarkably complete body does not arrive with its evolutionary relationships written on the surrounding stone.

In 2013, Xijun Ni and colleagues described *Archicebus achilles* from the early Eocene of China. Much of its skeleton survived together, with some bones still articulated. Splitting the enclosing rock had divided the specimen between two complementary surfaces, each preserving bones and impressions of the other side.[1]

Synchrotron X-ray imaging revealed details still concealed within the rock and allowed a three-dimensional reconstruction. The technique helped investigators examine a fragile skeleton without physically extracting every tiny part. Association and imaging together made a combination of anatomical features available for comparison.[1]

The feet resembled those of small monkeys in several respects, while other regions showed a different combination. The authors' analysis placed the fossil on an early branch of the tarsier lineage, near its separation from anthropoids, the group containing monkeys and apes, including humans.[1]

A resemblance to monkeys did not make the animal a monkey, and a position near a branching point did not identify it as the common ancestor. The placement depended on comparing characters across the tree. Similarity in one region could be inherited, independently acquired or retained after other branches changed.

The diagnosis subsequently faced scrutiny. In the expanded dental comparison discussed on the previous page, Paul Morse and colleagues argued that the features distinguishing *Archicebus* from *Teilhardina* required revision. Greater knowledge of variation within one group had changed the test applied to another.[2]

That criticism did not make the associated skeleton disappear, nor by itself settle every proposed relationship. It identified a specific problem: whether the observed differences justified the taxonomic distinction. Completeness improves the available evidence without exempting the interpretation from revision.

A separate investigation returned to smaller fragments. In 2018, Doug Boyer and colleagues described seven isolated toe-tip bones from four early Eocene localities in Wyoming. Their shapes resembled bones supporting the specialised grooming structures on the second toes of living lemurs and tarsiers.[3]

These **grooming claws** differ from the broad, flattened nails on other digits and are used by living animals in cleaning their own fur. Size, anatomy and the associated fossil fauna supported assigning the bones to several early primate genera, including *Teilhardina*. The specimens were not parts of the Chinese *Archicebus* skeleton.[3]

The distribution supported grooming claws as an early primate feature, rather than treating flat nails on every digit as an original package. But the fossils preserved supporting bones, not the animals performing the behaviour. Their attribution also remained a different kind of evidence from a toe still attached to its own foot.[3]

Large and small discoveries therefore complement one another. An associated body establishes combinations; isolated bones can extend the distribution of a feature; broader comparisons test relationships. None supplies the whole history independently, and a revised name need not erase an anatomical observation.

The next question moves along the branching tree rather than towards a predetermined human destination. Which fossils identify the early anthropoid lineage, and what do they establish about its distribution between Asia and Africa?
