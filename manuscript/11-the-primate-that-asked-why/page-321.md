### WALKING, BUT NOT AS WE DO

The name “Lucy” can make one fossil feel like a person standing at a familiar point on a road towards us. Her skeleton tells a less comfortable—and more interesting—story.

A.L. 288-1, an **Australopithecus afarensis** skeleton from Hadar, Ethiopia, lived about 3.2 million years ago. Enough of the pelvis and limbs survived to make one conclusion difficult to avoid: Lucy was a biped on the ground. But the mechanics of that bipedality, and the role trees still played in her life, have remained subjects of anatomical testing rather than assumptions.[1]

The pelvis is particularly revealing. In a 1991 analysis, Yoel Rak argued that Lucy's very broad pelvic inlet and its orientation helped control movement of the body's centre of mass during walking. The reconstruction was unmistakably bipedal and highly terrestrial—but it was not simply a scaled-down modern human pelvis. Rak described a locomotor solution with its own geometry rather than a halfway shape between a chimpanzee and ourselves.[1]

Evidence from the foot strengthened the terrestrial case. In 2011, Carol Ward, William Kimbel and Donald Johanson described a complete fourth metatarsal of *A. afarensis* from Hadar. This bone did not belong to Lucy herself, but to another member of her species. Its torsion and joint orientations indicated transverse and longitudinal arches, while its base lacked the geometry expected for an ape-like midfoot break. The authors interpreted the foot as a rigid propulsive structure belonging to a committed terrestrial biped.[2]

That does not mean every part of *A. afarensis* had stopped being useful in trees.

Christopher Ruff and colleagues tested Lucy's limb bones in 2016 using micro-CT measurements of structural strength. Bone remodels in response to repeated mechanical loading during life. Lucy's humerus-to-femur strength proportions fell between those of modern humans and chimpanzees, which the authors interpreted as evidence that her arms experienced substantially more loading than ours. They concluded that climbing was an adaptively significant part of her locomotor repertoire.[3]

The same study also found evidence that Lucy's terrestrial gait differed subtly from that of later *Homo*. Features of the hip and femur were consistent with greater side-to-side movement of the body's centre of mass over the supporting leg, potentially making walking less efficient than in modern humans.[3]

Those results need their own caution. Bone strength records loading, not a diary. It cannot tell us which tree Lucy climbed on a particular day or what fraction of her life was spent above the ground. Nor does an inherited climbing-friendly shape automatically prove that it was still used. The 2016 study is valuable precisely because it examined developmentally responsive bone structure rather than relying only on inherited proportions.[3]

So two claims can be true together.

**Australopithecus afarensis was strongly adapted for habitual terrestrial bipedality.** Its pelvis, knee and foot show that clearly.

**Its locomotion was not necessarily identical to ours, and at least some individuals retained substantial arboreal behaviour.**

Evolution had not exchanged one complete locomotor package for another in a single step.

The next evidence is different again. Bones tell us what a body was built to do. At Laetoli, volcanic ash captured what feet actually did for a few moments—leaving a trail before the walkers themselves disappeared.
