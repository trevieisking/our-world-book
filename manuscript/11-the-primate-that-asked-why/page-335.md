### THE NUTCRACKER THAT DID NOT LIVE ON NUTS

A skull can tell us what a feeding system was capable of. It cannot, by itself, tell us what passed between the teeth every day.

Few hominins demonstrate that distinction better than **Paranthropus boisei**.

Its anatomy looks extreme. The cheek teeth are enormous and flat, with very thick enamel. The face and jaws are built to transmit substantial chewing forces, and the skull provided large attachment areas for the muscles of mastication. The famous Olduvai skull OH 5 acquired the nickname **“Nutcracker Man”** because the machinery seemed almost designed for breaking hard foods.

Then researchers examined what the teeth had actually been doing.

Microscopic wear accumulates on a tooth's working surface during feeding. Different diets can leave different combinations of pits and scratches. In 2008, Peter Ungar, Frederick Grine and Mark Teaford analysed microwear textures on seven *P. boisei* molars.[1]

None showed the high surface complexity expected from repeated consumption of very hard objects immediately before death. Nor did the sample resemble the strongly directional wear expected from some very tough-food specialists. The teeth had powerful architecture, but the short-term wear record did not support a habitual diet dominated by hard nuts and seeds.[1]

Stable isotopes added a longer-term chemical record.

In 2011, Thure Cerling and colleagues measured carbon isotopes in *P. boisei* tooth enamel from East Africa. The values showed that its diet was dominated by **C4-derived resources**—from plants such as tropical grasses and sedges, or foods based upon those plants.[2]

Its C4 contribution was greater than that then measured in any other hominin sample in the study.[2]

This changed the meaning of the skull.

Powerful chewing anatomy may have allowed *P. boisei* to process large quantities of mechanically demanding or low-quality foods, including resources important during difficult periods, rather than proving that hard objects were always its preferred meal. Functional capacity and habitual diet are different questions.

Even the isotope evidence has limits.

Carbon values distinguish broad photosynthetic pathways, not a menu. They cannot tell us that one individual ate a particular grass blade rather than a sedge organ, or whether the food was eaten raw, processed, or combined with other resources.

And then Nyayanga adds another complication.

Very early *Paranthropus* teeth occur in deposits containing Oldowan tools used on animal and plant tissues.[3] We still cannot identify the toolmaker, but their presence makes it harder to imagine *Paranthropus* simply as a specialised chewing machine living outside the technological world around it.

Evolution did not divide the landscape into “tool-using clever humans” on one side and “big-toothed failed cousins” on the other.

*Paranthropus* survived for well over a million years. Its anatomy represented a successful strategy for its own environments, even though its lineage ultimately disappeared.

At roughly the same time, another hominin form was emerging with a very different combination of skull and body.

**Homo erectus** would not be the first biped, the first tool user, or necessarily the first member of *Homo*.

Its importance lies elsewhere: in a body and geographical range increasingly unlike anything we have seen so far.
