### THE GRASP AND THE GAZE

A foot capable of gripping a branch need not belong to an animal with every other feature we associate with primates. The important question is not whether a fossil has completed a checklist. It is which combinations actually existed, and what those combinations can reveal about the sequence of change.

In 2002, Jonathan Bloch and Doug Boyer described a skeleton of the Paleocene mammal *Carpolestes simpsoni*. Unlike an isolated tooth or ankle, it preserved much of the skull together with numerous bones from the rest of the body. Its long fingers and opposing big toe supported effective grasping.[1]

The big toe carried a nail. Yet the eye sockets did not show the strong forward convergence familiar from many later primates, and the ankle lacked specialisations for leaping. Grasping therefore existed in this animal without that complete visual and locomotor combination.[1]

The authors proposed that feeding near branch tips helped explain the ancestral arrangement. That was a functional and evolutionary inference, not a preserved animal reaching for a particular fruit. Nor does an eye socket reveal everything about how its owner experienced space. A skeletal difference should not become an unsupported claim of sensory incapacity.

Additional Paleocene skeletons widened the comparison. In 2007, Bloch and colleagues combined skull, tooth and body characters from extinct forms and living relatives. Their analysis supported close relationships between plesiadapiforms and primates of modern aspect, and found no support for interpreting the sampled extinct forms as specialised gliders.[2]

The wider comparison mattered because one animal can combine inherited features with specialisations of its own. Reconstructing an ancestor requires comparing branches, not simply selecting whichever fossil looks suitably intermediate. More anatomical information can change both the proposed relationship and the sequence of traits mapped onto it.

The adaptive explanation remains debated. In a 2026 review, Matt Cartmill and Kaye Brown defended the idea that visually guided predation helped shape the characteristic primate combination. They also identified implications requiring further comparative tests. Their review supplied an argument about existing evidence, not a newly observed ancestral hunt.[3]

Living sensory systems provide additional ways to investigate change. A 2025 study led by Hai Chi compared visual and smell receptors, their functional properties and associated brain structures across primates. The results supported different sensory changes in different branches, rather than a simple exchange in which better vision always meant an equally reduced sense of smell.[4]

The authors interpreted the pattern as a redistribution of sensory specialisations. This concerns mechanisms and relationships reconstructed through living species. It does not provide the receptor proteins or complete sensory experience of *Carpolestes*, and it cannot alone identify what first favoured grasping.[4]

These explanations need not be compressed into a choice between an animal that saw and an animal that smelled, or between feet for food and eyes for food. Different features could be useful in overlapping activities, while changing at different times. What matters is whether a proposed sequence fits the anatomical and comparative evidence.

The investigation has now assembled parts of a body rather than an inevitable ancestor. To place the next fossils in context, we must also examine their changing surroundings. Near the beginning of the Eocene, a sharp environmental disturbance coincided with primates appearing across widely separated regions.
