### A THIGH BONE BETWEEN WORLDS

A skull can suggest how a head was balanced. A thigh bone records different constraints. It must transmit forces between hip and knee, and its shape is repeatedly loaded by the ways an animal stands and moves. For **Orrorin tugenensis**, found in Kenya's Tugen Hills, that fragment of anatomy became the centre of a much larger argument about the beginning of human bipedality.

Brigitte Senut and colleagues named *Orrorin* in 2001 from fossils recovered at several localities in the Lukeino Formation. The material included teeth and jaws, pieces of arm and hand, and femora. Geological work placed the fossil-bearing formation at about six million years old. The original team interpreted the femora as evidence that *Orrorin* walked on two legs on the ground, while an upper-arm bone and a finger bone retained evidence of arboreal adaptation.[1]

That combination already warns against a simple transition from “ape” to “human.” A body can be adapted to more than one locomotor setting. It can also preserve old features while acquiring new ones.

The proximal femur—the part nearest the hip—has therefore been examined repeatedly. In 2008, Brian Richmond and William Jungers compared its morphology with living apes, humans and fossil hominins. Their analysis found that *Orrorin* differed from both living apes and *Homo* and most strongly resembled the pattern seen in *Australopithecus* and *Paranthropus*. They concluded that the femur supported bipedality.[2]

That result also tested a more specific evolutionary claim. The original discoverers had proposed that *Orrorin* might stand closer to *Homo* than the australopiths did. Richmond and Jungers found that the femur did **not** support that arrangement. A trait indicating bipedality is not a passport identifying which later branch an animal belongs to.[2]

In 2013, Sergio Almécija and colleagues widened the comparison to include fossil Miocene apes as well as living species. In their geometric-morphometric analysis, *Orrorin* occupied an intermediate region of shape space between some Miocene apes and australopiths. Its proximal femur was a mosaic: some features resembled earlier apes, others later hominins.[3]

That finding changed the comparison itself. Living chimpanzees and gorillas are not frozen models of the ancestor from which hominins emerged. The 2013 reconstruction suggested that both modern great-ape femora and hominin femora evolved away from more primitive forms represented by fossil apes. Using a living chimpanzee as though it were an unchanged six-million-year-old ancestor can therefore mislead us.[3]

The safest conclusion is narrower and more useful. *Orrorin* provides strong anatomical evidence consistent with terrestrial bipedality at about six million years ago, while other parts of its skeleton indicate that climbing still mattered. Its femur does not tell us exactly how many hours it spent in trees, whether its gait matched a later australopith, or whether its population lies directly on the line leading to us.

Near the human–chimpanzee separation, evolution does not present a clean sequence of finished designs. *Sahelanthropus* gave us one contested mosaic. *Orrorin* gives us another.

The next fossils take us into Ethiopia, where the evidence becomes even more revealing: teeth retain part of an older ape-like cutting system while a toe hints at the mechanics of pushing away from the ground.
