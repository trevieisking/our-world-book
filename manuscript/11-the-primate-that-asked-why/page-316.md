### NEAR THE DIVIDE

The genetic histories on the previous page carry us towards a separation that no fossil preserves as a single moment. Somewhere in Africa, populations ancestral to humans and chimpanzees ceased to share one evolutionary history. Around that boundary, the question becomes unusually difficult: what would the first member of the human branch actually look like?

One candidate comes from a place that widened the map. In 2002, Michel Brunet and colleagues described fossils from Toros-Menalla in Chad, about 2,500 kilometres west of the East African Rift. They named the species **Sahelanthropus tchadensis**. Its best-known specimen, the distorted but remarkably complete cranium called Toumaï, combined a small braincase with a face, teeth and canines that the describing team regarded as a distinctive mosaic of primitive and derived features.[1]

Age alone cannot answer the ancestry question. Later cosmogenic-nuclide work on sediments associated with the locality placed *Sahelanthropus* close to seven million years ago.[2] That puts it near estimates for the human–chimpanzee separation, but being old enough to stand near a branching event is not the same as being the population in which that event occurred. A fossil species may lie on one branch, close to it, or represent a side branch whose exact relationship remains unresolved.

Locomotion became one of the central tests. A virtual reconstruction of the crushed cranium examined the orientation of its base and argued that the head was carried in a manner compatible with upright bipedality.[3] A skull, however, does not preserve a stride.

Postcranial bones from the same locality changed the discussion. In 2022, Guillaume Daver and colleagues described a femur and two ulnae that they attributed to *Sahelanthropus*. Their analysis judged the femur most consistent with habitual bipedality while the arm bones retained evidence of substantial movement in trees.[4] The picture was not a miniature modern human walking away from an ape-like past. It was a body combining terrestrial and arboreal signals.

That interpretation was challenged. Marine Cazenave and colleagues argued that the available postcranial evidence did not securely demonstrate habitual bipedalism and that important features had alternative functional interpretations.[5] The disagreement matters because very early fossils are incomplete, comparative samples differ, and locomotion must be inferred from structures that may serve more than one mechanical role.

In 2026, Scott Williams and colleagues returned to the limb bones with additional comparisons. They found their size and geometric shape closest to chimpanzees, but reported more hominin-like limb proportions, confirmed features associated with hominin-like hip and knee function, and identified a femoral tubercle that in their comparative sample occurred only among bipedal hominins. They concluded that the evidence supports an early biped that still retained an ape-like body in important respects.[6]

The newer analysis strengthens the bipedal interpretation. It does not make every earlier uncertainty disappear. Nor does bipedality by itself prove direct ancestry: locomotor traits can be shared by related branches, and the earliest hominin record is too sparse to arrange every named species into a single ladder.

That is the discipline required at the edge of the split. **Anatomical diagnosis, inferred locomotion, geological age and direct ancestry are four different claims.** *Sahelanthropus* brings all four into the same investigation, but it does not collapse them into one answer.

And it is not alone. A little later, in Kenya, another fragmentary body leaves us a different part of the same question—written not mainly in a skull, but in the architecture of a thigh bone.
