### TECHNOLOGY BEFORE HOMO?

For decades, the oldest stone tools seemed to belong comfortably within a familiar story: the genus **Homo** appeared, brains enlarged, and deliberate stone manufacture followed.

Lomekwi disrupted the order.

In 2015, Sonia Harmand and colleagues reported an assemblage from **Lomekwi 3**, west of Lake Turkana in Kenya, that they dated to about **3.3 million years ago**. The stones included large cores, flakes, anvils and battered pieces. Experimental and technological analysis led the team to argue that their makers were intentionally striking and pounding stone rather than merely producing accidental fractures.[1]

If the dating and archaeological association are correct, the implications are substantial.

The site predates the oldest widely accepted **Homo** fossils by hundreds of thousands of years. It therefore breaks the old equation **stone-tool manufacture = Homo**. The makers could have belonged to another hominin lineage living in the region, but no hand is preserved gripping a tool and no skeleton lies attached to a core. **Kenyanthropus platyops** is geographically and temporally relevant, yet proximity does not identify the toolmaker.[1]

The technology is also not simply a smaller version of a later Oldowan toolkit.

Many Lomekwi pieces are large and heavy. The assemblage combines deliberate flake production with percussive activities—striking one stone against another or using stones against a hard surface. Harmand and colleagues proposed the term **Lomekwian** for this technological pattern.[1]

A 2025 review still treats Lomekwi as the beginning of the currently documented archaeological record and places it within the first million years of known stone technology, alongside the later Oldowan. That review also emphasises that early stone behaviour includes both sharp-edge production and pounding, and that several hominin genera overlap in time with the earliest archaeological industries.[2]

But the age and context deserve the same scrutiny we applied to cave dates.

Critics have questioned how the word **“in situ”** was used for some of the Lomekwi material. Surface processes, slope deposits and redeposition can bring younger artefacts into contact with older sediments. A 2020 critique argued that the extraordinary 3.3-million-year claim depends separately on three things being demonstrated: that the stones are genuinely artefacts, that they belong to the dated deposit, and that the deposit itself is correctly dated.[3]

Those objections do not make the assemblage imaginary. They define the evidential burden.

Current reviews continue to discuss Lomekwi as the earliest known stone-tool technology, but the archaeological context is not made stronger by hiding its published challenges.[2]

The most important lesson therefore survives either way. Tool manufacture cannot be assumed to belong to a taxonomic label merely because later members of that genus became skilled toolmakers. Behaviour has to be demonstrated from archaeological evidence and linked to a maker only when the association allows it.

At Lomekwi, stones may preserve technology without preserving the technologist.

At Dikika in Ethiopia, the problem reverses. There, no manufactured tools were recovered with two ancient animal bones—but marks on those bones were claimed to record what a stone did.
