### THE FOOT THAT WAITED FOR A NAME

Laetoli showed that different feet may have crossed the same ancient landscape. In Ethiopia, another foot carried that possibility from footprints into bone.

In 2009, researchers working at Burtele in the Woranso-Mille study area recovered eight bones from the front half of a hominin right foot. The specimen, BRT-VP-2/73, was published in 2012 and dated to about 3.4 million years ago.[1]

That age was important because **Australopithecus afarensis**—Lucy's species—was already present in the region. Its foot was adapted to habitual terrestrial bipedality, including an aligned big toe and a relatively rigid midfoot. The Burtele foot was different.

Its big toe was strongly divergent and capable of grasping. Several joints and proportions resembled the earlier **Ardipithecus ramidus** more than *A. afarensis*. Yet the lateral foot also showed features compatible with loading during bipedal walking. The 2012 authors therefore concluded that more than one kind of hominin locomotor adaptation existed in eastern Africa at the same time.[1]

What they did **not** do was give the foot a species name.

There were no directly associated teeth or skull bones. Anatomy showed that the foot did not fit the known *A. afarensis* pattern, but difference alone could not reveal exactly which taxon made it. For more than a decade, the specimen remained one of the clearest pieces of evidence for Pliocene locomotor diversity while its owner stayed uncertain.

That changed in 2025.

Yohannes Haile-Selassie and colleagues reported new jaws and teeth from the same Burtele localities and stratigraphic horizons. They assigned those dentognathic fossils to **Australopithecus deyiremeda**, a species originally named in 2015 from nearby Woranso-Mille material.[2][3]

The new fossils did not physically connect a jaw to the old foot. Instead, they changed the inference through context. The researchers found no compelling evidence for another hominin species at the Burtele localities and argued that the simplest explanation is that the foot and the diagnostic jaws belong to the same species. On that basis they attributed BRT-VP-2/73 to *A. deyiremeda*.[2]

The distinction matters. **An associated skeleton** would show directly that a particular foot and a particular jaw came from one individual. **A taxonomic attribution by provenance and population sampling** is a different kind of inference, even when it is well supported.

If the assignment is correct, *A. deyiremeda* retained substantially more pedal grasping capacity than contemporary *A. afarensis*. The 2025 study interprets this as evidence that climbing remained a significant part of its positional behaviour.[2]

That does not mean the species failed to walk upright. The original foot already combined grasping anatomy with features consistent with bipedal loading. The important point is that **bipedality itself came in more than one anatomical form**.

At roughly 3.4 million years ago, therefore, East Africa may have held hominins that shared landscapes but used them differently.

The next question is what the teeth of *A. deyiremeda* tell us. Different feet imply different movement. Different diets may tell us whether these neighbours were also dividing the landscape in other ways.
