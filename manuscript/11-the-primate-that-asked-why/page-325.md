### A FACE CRUSHED BY TIME

A fossil can be different because the animal was different. It can also look different because millions of years of burial have bent, cracked and expanded the bone.

That problem sits at the centre of **Kenyanthropus platyops**.

In 2001, Meave Leakey and colleagues described a roughly 3.5-million-year-old cranium from Lomekwi, west of Lake Turkana in Kenya. The specimen, KNM-WT 40000, combined a small braincase with an unusually flat subnasal face, forward-positioned cheekbone roots and relatively small molars. The team argued that this combination differed sufficiently from contemporary **Australopithecus afarensis** to justify a new genus and species.[1]

The claim immediately faced a preservation problem.

KNM-WT 40000 is badly distorted. Cracks filled with sediment expanded parts of the face; the cranium was skewed after burial. Critics therefore asked whether the apparently flat face might partly be a geological artefact and whether the specimen could fall within the variation of *A. afarensis* once distortion was considered.

That is exactly the kind of question a fossil diagnosis must survive.

In 2010, Fred Spoor, Meave Leakey and Louise Leakey returned to the maxilla using computed tomography, detailed preparation and geometric morphometrics. CT images allowed them to inspect tooth roots and crack geometry inside the fossil, estimate post-mortem expansion and compare both the preserved and distortion-corrected shapes with fossil hominins and living apes.[2]

Their result supported the original diagnosis. Even after correction, the maxilla retained a relatively flat and upright subnasal region, an anteriorly placed zygomatic process and a combination of small postcanine teeth that fell outside the sampled *A. afarensis* range.[2]

That does not make every question disappear.

A species is a population, not one unusual skull. The number of fossils securely attributable to *Kenyanthropus* remains small. Dental remains from Lomekwi dating between about 3.5 and 3.2 million years ago add information about local hominins, but a 2020 study concluded that most of them could not be confidently assigned to *K. platyops* because the holotype preserves too little directly comparable dental anatomy.[3]

So the evidence leaves us with an unusual asymmetry.

The best-known skull is distinctive enough that distortion correction supports a separate morphology. Yet the population behind that skull is still poorly sampled.

That is not a failure of science. It is the difference between **showing that one specimen is unusual** and **fully characterising the species to which it belonged**.

The broader message is harder for a straight-line family tree. Around the same time that *A. afarensis* walked in eastern Africa, *A. deyiremeda* may have used a more grasping foot and a different dietary niche, while *Kenyanthropus* carried a different facial architecture near Lake Turkana.

The middle Pliocene was not running one experiment.

And soon, in southern Africa, a child's skull would force scientists to confront another old assumption: perhaps large humanlike brains did not come first.
