### THE FACE THAT BROKE THE STRAIGHT LINE

By the time **Australopithecus anamensis** appears in East Africa, the evidence for habitual bipedality is less dependent on one disputed feature. A shin bone from Kanapoi, Kenya, carries the loads of walking in its joint surfaces and shaft. Yet the species also teaches another lesson: even when locomotion becomes clearer, ancestry can become less so.

The Kanapoi tibia, KNM-KP 29285, comes from deposits a little over four million years old. Its knee and ankle geometry place the leg beneath the body in a way associated with single-leg support during terrestrial bipedal walking. Detailed anatomical comparison has therefore treated *A. anamensis* as habitually bipedal, while other known upper-limb features retain a more primitive mosaic.[1]

The fossils were originally known mostly from jaws, teeth and scattered postcranial bones. Work at Kanapoi and Allia Bay confirmed material at roughly 4.2 to 4.1 million years ago and helped establish *A. anamensis* as an early species of **Australopithecus**.[2] For years, the face of that species remained largely missing.

Then a herder in Ethiopia's Woranso-Mille study area found a cranium.

The specimen, MRD-VP-1/1, was described in 2019. Yohannes Haile-Selassie and colleagues assigned the nearly complete skull to *A. anamensis* using features of the canine, upper jaw and temporal bone. Independent geological work bracketed the fossil-bearing deltaic deposits at approximately 3.8 million years old.[3][4]

MRD did more than give the species a face. It altered a familiar evolutionary story.

Before the discovery, *A. anamensis* was often modelled as gradually transforming into **Australopithecus afarensis**: one species becoming the next through time, without the two existing side by side. This mode of change is called **anagenesis**.

The 3.8-million-year-old cranium complicated that picture because *A. afarensis* fossils are known from slightly older than 3.8 million years ago. The 2019 analysis concluded that the two species overlapped for at least about 100,000 years and were more cranially distinct than previously appreciated.[3]

Overlap does not mean that the two lineages were unrelated. Nor does it prove that *A. anamensis* could not have contributed ancestry to *A. afarensis*. A population can branch, one descendant lineage can persist while another emerges, and the fossil record can sample both. What the evidence weakens is the simplest version of a complete, sequential replacement in which the whole earlier species turns into the later one before any temporal overlap is possible.

The dates also need care. The sediment containing MRD was constrained using volcanic layers, palaeomagnetism and an age–stratigraphic model. Those measurements date the geological context of the fossil; they do not give an exact lifespan for the individual or a single birth date for the species.[4]

So *A. anamensis* advances two parts of our investigation in opposite ways. Its leg strengthens the case that habitual terrestrial bipedality was established. Its skull makes the family tree less linear.

That is exactly what a growing fossil record should be allowed to do.

The next species gives us one of the most famous skeletons ever found. “Lucy” would make upright walking unmistakable—but her arms and bones would keep alive a question that began with the earliest hominins: how completely had life in the trees been left behind?
