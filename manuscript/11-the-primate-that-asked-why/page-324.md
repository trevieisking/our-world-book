### DIFFERENT TEETH, DIFFERENT MENU

Two hominins can share a landscape without using it in the same way.

The jaws and teeth assigned to **Australopithecus deyiremeda** were first described in 2015 from deposits between about 3.5 and 3.3 million years old at Woranso-Mille, Ethiopia. They differed from contemporary **Australopithecus afarensis** in a combination of jaw shape, tooth size and the position of the cheekbone root.[1]

Those differences were enough for the describing team to name a separate species. But naming a fossil species from a small sample is never the same as demonstrating the boundaries of a living population. Later reviews have stressed that Middle Pliocene taxonomy remains difficult because some proposed species are represented by only a few individuals, leaving normal variation, geography and sex incompletely sampled.[2]

New fossils in 2025 strengthened the *A. deyiremeda* case by adding more diagnostic jaws and teeth. They also revealed something beyond taxonomy.

Tooth enamel carries a chemical record of food.

Plants using different photosynthetic pathways leave different proportions of carbon isotopes in the tissues of animals that eat them. **C3 plants** include trees, shrubs and many herbs. **C4 plants** include many tropical grasses and some sedges. Enamel carbon-isotope values cannot tell us the name of every food item, but they can distinguish broad sources of dietary carbon.[3]

The newly analysed *A. deyiremeda* teeth showed relatively low and narrowly distributed carbon-isotope values. The 2025 team interpreted them as a diet dominated by C3 resources—closer to patterns measured in the earlier *Ardipithecus ramidus* and *Australopithecus anamensis* than to the broader resource use of *A. afarensis*.[3]

That comparison is important because *A. afarensis* did not simply eat whatever was most abundant in one habitat. Carbon-isotope measurements from Hadar and Dikika show substantial individual variation and clear use of C4 or CAM-derived resources as well as C3 foods. Some individuals incorporated much more of those resources than others.[4]

So the two contemporary forms may have differed in more than their feet.

*A. deyiremeda*, if the current taxonomic interpretation is correct, combined a more grasping foot and strong arboreal component with a diet heavily based on C3 resources. *A. afarensis* had a more terrestrial foot and a broader isotopic dietary range that included substantial C4/CAM foods.[3][4]

That does not prove they never competed. It does not prove that locomotion caused diet. Nor does an isotope identify whether the C3 carbon came from fruit, leaves, underground plant parts or an animal that had eaten those plants.

But the pattern raises a powerful ecological possibility: contemporaneous hominins may have divided their environment partly through different combinations of movement and feeding.

The Middle Pliocene is becoming less like a single ancestral population and more like a community.

West of Lake Turkana, another 3.5-million-year-old face makes that diversity problem even harder.
