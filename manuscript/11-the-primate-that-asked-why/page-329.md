### WHEN A SCRATCH BECOMES EVIDENCE

An ancient stone can look deliberately struck. An ancient bone can carry a line that looks deliberately cut.

The difficulty is that nature can make convincing imitations.

At **Dikika** in Ethiopia, two animal-bone fragments from deposits older than about 3.39 million years carry a small number of conspicuous surface marks. In 2010, Shannon McPherron and colleagues interpreted several as stone-tool cut marks made while removing flesh and others as percussion damage produced while breaking bone for marrow.[1]

If correct, the marks would push stone-assisted carcass processing deep into the time of **Australopithecus afarensis**.

But there was no manufactured stone-tool assemblage beside the bones.

That absence does not prove the interpretation wrong. A hominin might have used an unmodified sharp stone, carried a tool away, or left artefacts somewhere not preserved. What it does is remove one powerful independent line of evidence. The marks themselves must therefore distinguish human action from natural damage.

Critics focused on **equifinality**: different processes can produce similar-looking traces.

In a 2010 analysis, Manuel Domínguez-Rodrigo, Travis Pickering and Henry Bunn compared the Dikika modifications with experimentally trampled bones. They argued that abrasion caused when animals step on bones against coarse sediment could reproduce important features of the marks, and that this natural explanation had not been excluded.[2]

The original research team later tested that criticism against the wider Dikika fossil assemblage. In 2015, Jessica Thompson and colleagues examined a much larger sample of surface damage from the site. Trampling marks were common—but generally much smaller and carried a different combination of characteristics from the marks on the two disputed specimens. They concluded that trampling was **not the most parsimonious explanation** for the DIK-55 marks.[3]

That did not end the argument.

New classification methods have shown how difficult mark identification can be. A 2020 study using machine-learning image classification on experimental and archaeological bone modifications assigned several published Dikika marks to trampling or tooth-mark categories rather than cut or percussion damage.[4] The approach has its own training-set and image limitations, but it demonstrates that seemingly diagnostic surface features can remain ambiguous when analysed by a different method.

The discovery of the **3.3-million-year-old Lomekwi** stone assemblage changed one part of the debate. Early stone manufacture is no longer implausible merely because the oldest previously accepted tools once dated to about 2.6 million years ago.

But Lomekwi does **not** authenticate Dikika.

A tool found hundreds of kilometres away cannot tell us what scratched a particular bone. Each archaeological claim has to carry its own evidential weight.

That makes the Dikika controversy valuable rather than embarrassing. It shows what scientists mean when they ask whether an explanation has excluded plausible alternatives.

The honest status is therefore narrower than either headline:

**The bones preserve unusual marks consistent with a published butchery interpretation, but their cause remains contested.**

To move from possibility toward a stronger behavioural record, we need sites where artefacts, animal damage, geology and hominin presence begin to converge more tightly.

By about three million years ago, that convergence becomes clearer—and it raises another surprise: some of the earliest Oldowan tools occur beside fossils that are not **Homo** at all.
