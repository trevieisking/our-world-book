### A GENOME HOLDS MORE THAN ONE HISTORY

A fossil joint preserves the shape of one part of a body. A genome preserves inherited sequences whose histories extend through populations. Bringing those records together requires a distinction that a simple branching diagram can hide: different stretches of DNA need not share exactly the same genealogy.

Even reading the sequences presents a challenge. Repeated stretches can be difficult to place correctly, like similar passages appearing at several locations in a long text. Earlier ape reference genomes left some of these regions incompletely assembled or difficult to compare.[1]

In 2025, DongAhn Yoo and colleagues produced near-complete reference assemblies for six ape species, including chimpanzees, bonobos, gorillas, orangutans and a siamang. The work opened previously difficult regions to comparison without forcing every sequence into the arrangement of the human reference genome.[1]

Some gaps remained, and representative genomes did not capture all variation within each species. Improving the record therefore did not mean completing every historical question. It meant that evolutionary comparisons could include more of the material whose earlier absence had limited them.

One important complication had already emerged clearly from the gorilla genome. In 2012, Aylwyn Scally and colleagues reported regions whose inferred genealogies linked gorilla sequences more closely with human or chimpanzee sequences than the latter were linked with one another.[2]

This does not require a different complete family tree for every region. Variants can persist within an ancestral population through successive separations. Descendants may inherit different versions, so tracing one stretch backwards can reach its common ancestor in a different order from the branching of the populations themselves.[2]

The process is called **incomplete lineage sorting**. It makes a prediction about variation across the genome, rather than requiring every sequence to tell an identical story. Reconstructing a species relationship and reconstructing the ancestry of a particular DNA segment are related but distinct tasks.[2]

Later interbreeding supplies another possible source of mixed histories. In 2016, Marc de Manuel and colleagues compared the genomes of 75 wild-born chimpanzees and bonobos. Several lines of evidence supported gene flow from bonobos into ancestors of central and eastern chimpanzee populations after the lineages had separated.[3]

That inference concerns later exchange, unlike the persistence of older variation through a split. Neither process should be assigned automatically to every unexpected resemblance. The observed pattern must be tested against the alternatives, and the inferred contact is not a preserved encounter between particular animals.[3]

These findings do not turn a living ape into the ancestor of another living species. They describe populations with their own changing histories. Nor can a present-day genome show us the exact body of a shared ancestor without additional anatomical evidence.

Dates require another step. Sequence differences must be interpreted through assumptions about mutation rates and population history, with fossils supplying constraints. A well-supported relationship need not yield an equally precise calendar date for its beginning.[2]

The distinction carries us towards the human branch without making it the intended destination of the tree. To recognise its earliest members in the rocks, we must return to teeth, skulls and limbs—and ask which features can distinguish a hominin from another ape living near the same evolutionary separation.
