### A TOE AND AN OLD BITE

The earliest hominin candidates are frustrating because the features we most want rarely survive together. A skull may lack legs. A femur may arrive without a face. In Ethiopia's Middle Awash, **Ardipithecus kadabba** gives us another scattered record: jaws and teeth, a few postcranial bones, and one small toe whose importance has sometimes become larger than the bone itself.

In 2001, Yohannes Haile-Selassie described fossils from deposits dated between about 5.8 and 5.2 million years ago. They came from a relatively wet, wooded setting rather than an exclusively open grassland.[1][2] At the time the material was named as a subspecies of *Ardipithecus ramidus*. Its mixture was striking. Some dental features linked it with younger hominins, while other teeth and postcranial characters remained more primitive.[1]

Additional teeth changed the taxonomy. In 2004, Haile-Selassie, Gen Suwa and Tim White argued that the older material should be recognised as the separate species *Ardipithecus kadabba*. The canine–premolar system was especially informative. Its morphology and wear retained more of the ancestral honing arrangement in which the upper canine interacted with the lower third premolar than was seen in *Ar. ramidus*.[3]

That does not make *kadabba* simply “more ape-like.” Evolution does not update every anatomical system at once. The teeth instead preserve a useful sequence: parts of an older cutting mechanism persisted while other features were changing. The authors also cautioned against multiplying late-Miocene hominin branches too confidently from sparse samples. Similar teeth can make supposedly separate taxa look less separate; incomplete material can make one evolving lineage look like several.[3]

Then there is the toe.

A fourth proximal pedal phalanx from a younger part of the *kadabba* record, about 5.2 million years old, has a joint surface whose orientation was interpreted as permitting the extension used during toe-off in bipedal walking.[1] That made a tiny bone part of the evidence that upright locomotion existed before the better-known australopiths.

But the inference has limits. A later review of fossil feet noted that dorsal canting of lateral toe bones also occurs in the non-bipedal Miocene ape *Ekembo*. The *kadabba* phalanx remains compatible with bipedal locomotion, especially when considered with the larger hominin record, but that particular feature is not uniquely diagnostic by itself.[4]

This distinction matters. **A structure can support a hypothesis without being sufficient to prove it alone.** The toe does not preserve the pelvis, knee, ankle or complete foot that would tell us how the whole stride worked. Nor does it tell us whether bipedality was frequent on the ground, occasional, or combined with extensive climbing.

What *Ardipithecus kadabba* gives us is therefore not a finished walking machine. It gives us two evolutionary clues developing at different speeds: a dentition still retaining an old canine–premolar relationship, and a foot bone consistent with a changing way of loading the ground.

The next species gives us much more of the body. At 4.4 million years ago, *Ardipithecus ramidus* carries the argument from an isolated toe to a pelvis, femur, foot, hand and ankle—and shows how difficult it is to fit early bipedality into any single modern category.
