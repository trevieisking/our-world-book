### MORE THAN ONE EARLY HUMAN

If every early member of **Homo** belonged to one highly variable species, then very different skulls from the same time and region would have to represent ordinary variation—perhaps sex, age or geography.

Koobi Fora made that increasingly difficult.

One fossil, **KNM-ER 1470**, has dominated the argument since the 1970s. Its reconstructed face is broad and relatively flat beneath the nose, with cheekbone roots positioned far forward. Other early-*Homo* crania from the Turkana Basin, such as KNM-ER 1813, have markedly different facial and dental proportions.

Was 1470 simply a large individual?

That question could not be settled from one exceptional skull. A single specimen can always sit at the edge of a species' variation.

In 2012, Meave Leakey, Fred Spoor and colleagues described three additional fossils from Koobi Fora, dated between about **1.95 and 1.78 million years ago**.[1]

The most visually informative was **KNM-ER 62000**, a well-preserved face from a late juvenile. It strongly resembled 1470 in facial architecture but was notably smaller. That combination weakened the idea that 1470's unusual shape could be explained simply by being an exceptionally large individual.[1]

Two jaws added another part of the pattern.

KNM-ER 60000 and KNM-ER 62003 possessed short dental arcades that were relatively flat across the front, with small incisors. Those features were consistent with the facial group represented by 1470 and 62000, and unlike the morphology reconstructed for the **Homo habilis** type specimen OH 7.[1][2]

The 2012 team concluded that eastern Africa contained **two contemporary species of non-erectus early Homo**, in addition to **Homo erectus** itself.[1]

One of those forms is commonly called **Homo rudolfensis**, with KNM-ER 1470 as its best-known representative. The other includes fossils conventionally placed in *Homo habilis*.

The names remain less secure than the anatomical observation.

Species boundaries in fossils depend on how much variation researchers allow within one species and which characters they consider diagnostic. We cannot test reproduction directly. We sample scattered individuals separated by distance and thousands of years.

But the newer fossils change the structure of the problem. They show that 1470's facial pattern recurs in a smaller individual. It is therefore harder to explain all of the difference as body size or sex alone.

The 2015 reconstruction of OH 7 pushes in the same direction: its long, narrow jaw is morphologically incompatible with the short, broad arcade associated with the 1470 group.[2]

Yet brain size does not sort these groups neatly. Estimates overlap widely among early-*Homo* fossils. Evolutionary divergence can therefore be pronounced in feeding anatomy while remaining much less obvious in endocranial volume.

This is another warning against turning human evolution into a single escalator.

Around two million years ago, several hominin experiments coexisted.

Some belonged to *Paranthropus*, with extraordinary chewing anatomy.

Some belonged to different early-*Homo* lineages.

And another form—**Homo erectus**—was beginning to combine a different body plan with a geographical reach that would soon extend beyond Africa.
