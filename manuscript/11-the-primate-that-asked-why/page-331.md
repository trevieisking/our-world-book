### THE FIRST JAW WE CALL HOMO

A genus does not begin when a palaeontologist finds its oldest fossil. It begins somewhere in populations whose earlier members may never have been preserved.

At **Ledi-Geraru** in Ethiopia's Afar region, a partial lower jaw gives us the oldest fossil currently assigned to **Homo**.

The specimen, **LD 350-1**, was reported in 2015 by Brian Villmoare and colleagues. Geological evidence places it between about **2.80 and 2.75 million years ago**.[1][2]

Only part of the mandible and several teeth survive, yet the anatomy is informative. Some features remain primitive and resemble earlier australopiths. Others—including aspects of the jaw and teeth—fall closer to later early *Homo*. The describing team therefore interpreted LD 350-1 as evidence that changes away from the australopith dentognathic pattern had begun by roughly 2.8 million years ago.[1]

“Mosaic” is again the important word.

The fossil does not arrive with a large braincase, a long-legged skeleton or a tool kit attached to it. Those traits, often bundled together in textbook images of early *Homo*, did not need to appear at exactly the same time.

Nor does the name **Homo** solve the ancestry question.

LD 350-1 is the earliest specimen currently recognised as *Homo* in the fossil record. That does **not** make it the first individual of the genus, the direct ancestor of every later human, or proof that the lineage originated exactly where the jaw was found. Fossil first appearance and biological origin are different events.

Its environment generated another hypothesis.

Erin DiMaggio and colleagues reconstructed the Ledi-Geraru sediments and associated animals. Compared with somewhat earlier Afar environments, the fauna indicated more open and probably more arid conditions. That pattern was consistent with proposals that environmental change played a role in hominin evolution around the emergence of *Homo*.[2]

A decade later, the climate question remained open rather than settled. A 2025 analysis of mammal communities from Ledi-Geraru again found evidence for continuing aridification and increasing seasonality around the time of early *Homo*, while also emphasising that local environmental variation persisted and that other hominins survived under the same changing conditions.[3]

That last point prevents an easy story.

If drying climate alone “created Homo,” then every hominin exposed to the same environmental pressure should have followed the same evolutionary route. They did not.

Environmental change can alter available foods, water, vegetation and mobility. It can change selection pressures without dictating one inevitable anatomical outcome.

So LD 350-1 marks a threshold in our evidence, not a magical boundary in nature.

By 2.8 million years ago, a jaw with a combination recognised as early **Homo** existed in the Afar.

By about 2.6 million years ago, stone technology was becoming increasingly visible across eastern Africa.

The next question is whether those two records can actually be joined—or whether tools and taxonomic names are still running on partly independent tracks.
