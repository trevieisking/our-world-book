### A CAVE WITH MORE THAN ONE CLOCK

Volcanic ash can sometimes bracket a fossil between datable eruptions. A cave is less orderly.

At **Sterkfontein** in South Africa, sediment entered openings, accumulated, hardened, was partly dissolved, and was later crossed by younger mineral deposits. Fossils could be buried in one phase while flowstone entered cracks during another. If those events are treated as though they happened at the same time, the clock can be read incorrectly.

The problem became famous around **StW 573**, the remarkably complete australopith skeleton known as “Little Foot.”

In 2015, Darryl Granger and colleagues used cosmogenic aluminium-26 and beryllium-10 preserved in cave sediment to calculate a burial age of about **3.67 ± 0.16 million years** for the Member 2 deposit containing the skeleton.[1] The method dates when quartz-bearing material was shielded from cosmic radiation after burial, rather than dating a later carbonate layer.

That result placed Little Foot much earlier than several previous estimates.

In 2022, the same general approach was applied higher in Sterkfontein's **Member 4**, source of the site's richest australopith assemblage. An isochron burial age of about **3.41 ± 0.11 million years**, together with other samples around 3.5–3.6 million years, led the authors to argue that much of Sterkfontein's australopith record belongs in the middle Pliocene and overlaps in time with *Australopithecus afarensis* in East Africa.[2]

Why had younger dates been obtained?

Some uranium–lead and palaeomagnetic estimates came from **flowstones** within the breccia. The 2022 study argued that these carbonate sheets intruded after the fossil-bearing sediment had accumulated, meaning they supply younger geological events rather than the burial age of the fossils themselves.[2]

The argument is not completely over. Biochronological approaches—which estimate age by comparing associated animal fossils or evolutionary trends—have produced younger ranges for parts of Sterkfontein. A 2024 reassessment explicitly reviewed those disagreements while obtaining a roughly 3.6-million-year estimate for Little Foot by a different biochronological model.[3]

Then there is the skeleton's name.

Ron Clarke and Kathleen Kuman have argued that StW 573 represents **Australopithecus prometheus**, distinct from *A. africanus*, citing its cranial and postcranial anatomy.[4] Other researchers have rejected that assignment. In 2025, Jesse Martin and colleagues compared Little Foot with the type specimen historically associated with *A. prometheus* and concluded that the two do not share the unique anatomical combination required to place them in the same species. They argued that the name *A. prometheus* should remain a synonym of *A. africanus*, while also noting that Little Foot itself differs in meaningful ways from conventional *A. africanus* specimens.[5]

That is an unusually useful result.

A skeleton can be **well preserved yet taxonomically uncertain**. A deposit can be **directly measured yet chronologically debated**. Completeness does not remove the need for comparison, and a numerical date does not become trustworthy until we know what geological event the measurement actually dates.

Sterkfontein expands the middle-Pliocene map far south of the East African sites. But its deepest lesson is methodological: before arranging fossils in time, we must first reconstruct the history of the rocks that carried them.

The next evidence is not a bone at all.

Near Lake Turkana, stones struck against other stones would move the history of technology hundreds of thousands of years earlier than **Homo**.
