### STEPS WITHOUT BONES

A skeleton records a lifetime of growth and loading. A footprint records something much shorter: a foot pressing into a surface, weight moving across it, and then lifting away.

At **Laetoli** in northern Tanzania, volcanic ash preserved such moments from about 3.66 million years ago. Mary Leakey and Richard Hay reported the famous Pliocene tracks in 1979. Among thousands of animal prints were trails made by hominins walking upright.[1]

The best-known tracks are at **Site G**. Unlike a femur whose function must be inferred from its architecture, these impressions are direct traces of bipedal locomotion. Later three-dimensional work has recovered information from the overlapping G2/G3 trail as well as the clearer G1 series, allowing researchers to compare footprint shape rather than treating part of the trackway as unusable.[2]

The usual attribution is **Australopithecus afarensis**. Fossils of that species are known from Laetoli at the appropriate time, and its skeleton elsewhere is strongly adapted to bipedality. But a footprint contains no skull, teeth or DNA. The taxonomic identification therefore combines track anatomy, geological age and the hominins otherwise known from the site. It is a powerful inference, not a name written into the ash.

In 2016, new tracks from **Site S** expanded the scene. Fidelis Masao and colleagues described footprints of two more bipedal individuals moving across the same palaeosurface and in the same general direction as the Site G walkers. One trackmaker was substantially larger than estimates based on the previously known tracks. The authors interpreted the combined evidence as consistent with considerable body-size variation within *A. afarensis*, while acknowledging that body-size and social interpretations depend on the taxonomic assignment.[3]

Then another trail complicated the picture.

Five prints at **Site A** had actually been discovered before the famous Site G tracks. Their unusual form led to decades of debate, including suggestions that they might have been made by a bear walking briefly on two legs. In 2019 researchers relocated and re-excavated the trackway; the resulting analysis was published in 2021.[4]

Ellison McNutt and colleagues compared the prints with bears, chimpanzees and humans. They concluded that Site A is better explained by a bipedal hominin. Yet its proportions, shape and cross-stepping gait differ markedly from Site G. The authors argued that the tracks were probably made by a hominin with a different, more primitive foot and that at least two hominin taxa may therefore have coexisted at Laetoli.[4]

That conclusion is important—and appropriately narrower than saying we know the second species. Footprints can demonstrate that a differently shaped bipedal foot crossed the ash. They do not supply the skull or teeth required to attach a secure species name.

Laetoli therefore gives us something fossils rarely preserve: behaviour itself, frozen for seconds. It confirms that bipedal hominins were walking across this landscape long before *Homo*.

But even a line of footprints does not restore a simple line of descent.

The deeper we move into the Pliocene, the more the evidence points toward **diversity**: different bodies, different mixtures of climbing and walking, and perhaps different hominins sharing landscapes that once looked like a single-file march towards humanity.
