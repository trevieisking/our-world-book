### WALKING BEFORE THINKING BIG

For much of the early twentieth century, many researchers expected humanity's defining transformation to begin with the brain. A large brain, they imagined, would free the hands, encourage tool use and eventually reshape the rest of the body.

A child's skull from South Africa suggested a different order.

In 1924, fossil-bearing rock from the Taung limeworks reached anatomist **Raymond Dart** at the University of the Witwatersrand. Within it was the face, jaw and natural endocast of a juvenile primate. In February 1925, Dart named **Australopithecus africanus**.[1]

The specimen was clearly not human. Its braincase was small, and it belonged to a child. Yet Dart argued that the skull combined ape-like and humanlike characters. One feature was especially important: the **foramen magnum**, the opening through which the spinal cord connects with the brain, lay beneath the skull rather than far towards its rear.[1][2]

That position affects how the head balances on the vertebral column. In a habitual quadruped with the trunk held more horizontally, the spinal connection is positioned differently from that of an animal whose head is regularly balanced above a more upright trunk. The Taung Child therefore supplied cranial evidence consistent with bipedal posture even though its brain remained small.[2]

Later discoveries would make the bipedal interpretation of **Australopithecus** far stronger than one juvenile skull ever could. Pelves, femora, tibiae, feet and eventually footprints showed that upright walking was established in several early hominins long before the large brains of later **Homo**.

That changes the evolutionary sequence.

Bipedality did not wait for a modern-sized brain. Hands could become less necessary for terrestrial locomotion while the brain remained within an ape-like size range. Whatever selective pressures produced later human encephalisation, they acted on a lineage whose experiments in upright locomotion were already ancient.

Taung also teaches a second lesson: an important fossil can be interpreted correctly in one respect and overinterpreted in another.

Dart believed details of the natural endocast indicated unusually humanlike brain organisation. Endocasts can preserve aspects of brain size and external surface form, but translating folds and impressions into cognitive abilities is difficult, particularly in a juvenile and in a brain unlike that of a modern human.[3] The strongest historical importance of Taung does not require us to recover the child's thoughts.

Nor did scientific acceptance arrive immediately. The fossil challenged expectations that human origins should be sought primarily in Eurasia and that brain enlargement came early. The shadow of the fraudulent Piltdown fossil, with its apparently large humanlike braincase, helped make Taung look wrong to researchers whose model was wrong.[2]

A century later, Africa's central place in early hominin evolution is supported by fossils spanning millions of years and many species. Taung was not the proof by itself. It was the clue that forced the question into the right continent and the right anatomical order.

But South Africa would not yield only one skull.

At Sterkfontein, caves packed with australopith fossils would create a new problem: **how old are the bones when the cave itself has been repeatedly filled, dissolved and refilled?**
