### WALKING WITHOUT LEAVING THE TREES

An isolated toe can suggest what a joint was capable of. A partial skeleton can make the contradictions harder to ignore.

At about 4.4 million years ago, **Ardipithecus ramidus** preserves enough of the pelvis, leg, foot and upper limb to show that early hominin locomotion did not have to resemble either a modern human or a modern chimpanzee. The best-known partial skeleton, ARA-VP-6/500—“Ardi”—became central to arguments about what bipedality looked like before *Australopithecus*.

In 2009, C. Owen Lovejoy and colleagues reconstructed the pelvis and femur as a combination of upright walking and movement in trees. Parts of the upper pelvis were shortened and reorganised in ways the authors linked to balance during bipedal walking, while other regions retained anatomy useful in climbing. They concluded that *Ar. ramidus* was a more primitive biped than later australopiths.[1]

Its foot made the mixture even clearer. The big toe was widely divergent and capable of grasping, unlike the aligned human hallux that contributes to our propulsive push-off. Yet the 2009 foot analysis also identified midfoot features that the authors argued provided rigidity during terrestrial bipedality.[2] The animal therefore did not need a human foot to walk on two legs.

The original reconstruction went further. Its authors argued that the hand, wrist and foot lacked several highly specialised features used by living African apes in knuckle-walking, suspension and vertical climbing. They proposed a comparatively cautious form of climbing and warned that chimpanzees and gorillas should not simply be treated as preserved versions of the human–chimpanzee ancestor.[2]

More fossils supported the basic locomotor mixture while changing some details. Postcranial remains from Gona, described in 2019, reinforced a combination of terrestrial bipedality and arboreal clambering. They also suggested that the ankle and big toe possessed somewhat greater adaptations to bipedality than had been recognised from the original Aramis skeleton alone.[3]

Then the ankle became part of a new argument.

In 2025, Thomas Prang and colleagues analysed the *Ar. ramidus* talus—the bone that transmits weight between the leg and foot. They reported similarities with chimpanzee and gorilla tali associated with vertical climbing and plantigrade quadrupedalism, alongside derived features consistent with an enhanced push-off mechanism. Their reconstruction therefore included an early form of bipedalism **and** more African-ape-like vertical climbing than the original 2009 model had proposed.[4]

The disagreement does not reduce *Ardipithecus* to uncertainty about everything. Several analyses converge on a creature that could move bipedally on the ground while remaining substantially capable in trees. The disputed part concerns the mechanics and evolutionary history of those arboreal behaviours—and what they imply about the last common ancestor of humans and chimpanzees.

That distinction is valuable. Fossils do not merely fill gaps in a predetermined sequence. New bones, new comparisons and new methods can change the model used to interpret bones already known.

By 4.4 million years ago, then, walking on two legs was not necessarily a commitment to life on the ground. The human branch could acquire one mode of movement without immediately surrendering another.

The next fossils carry bipedality forward into **Australopithecus**. But even there, the family tree refuses to become a straight line.
