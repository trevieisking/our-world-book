### TOOLS BESIDE BIG TEETH

At Nyayanga in southwestern Kenya, stone tools, butchered animal bones and hominin teeth occur in the same ancient landscape. The combination is powerful because it breaks an assumption that once seemed almost automatic: if the tools are Oldowan, their maker must have been **Homo**.

Thomas Plummer and colleagues described the Nyayanga material in 2023. The fossil-bearing deposits span approximately **3.03 to 2.58 million years ago**, placing some of the assemblage among the oldest known Oldowan records.[1] More than three hundred stone artefacts and over a thousand animal fossils were recovered from the relevant deposits.

The tools include flakes, cores and pounding implements. Use-wear and fracture evidence indicate that they were employed for more than one task. Some sharp edges cut animal tissue. Heavy stones were also used for pounding, and microscopic traces are consistent with plant processing.[1]

Two hippopotamus carcass assemblages are especially striking. Bones preserve cut and percussion damage associated with removal of flesh and access to internal tissues. These were not small prey. The site shows hominins using stone technology to process a very large animal while also exploiting plant resources.[1]

Beside that evidence were two hominin molars.

They were assigned not to *Homo*, but to **Paranthropus**.[1]

The association does not prove that *Paranthropus* made the tools. Fossils and artefacts from the same deposit can belong to different individuals and even different species using a place at different times. Early *Homo* could have been present without leaving identifiable bones. But the teeth remove another lazy assumption: *Paranthropus* cannot simply be excluded from the list of possible toolmakers because its skull and teeth look specialised for powerful chewing.

Its own diet was complex. Carbon-isotope values from the Nyayanga *Paranthropus* teeth indicate heavy use of C4 resources, consistent with substantial reliance on grasses, sedges or foods ultimately based on those plants.[1] Large chewing teeth and stone tools therefore need not represent mutually exclusive ways of obtaining food.

Later work made the technology still more interesting.

In 2025, Emma Finestone and colleagues analysed the geological sources of Nyayanga stone. By at least about **2.6 million years ago**, many tools were being manufactured from high-quality rocks that did not occur locally. Some suitable raw materials came from sources as far as approximately **13 kilometres** away.[2]

That distance matters because the pattern is not well explained by simply picking up whatever stone lay beside a carcass. The authors argue that toolmakers selectively transported useful material across the landscape, perhaps anticipating later processing needs.[2]

“Planning” must be used carefully. We do not know the thoughts of the toolmakers, the route they followed or whether a single individual carried one cobble the full distance. But the archaeological pattern shows that stone choice and food location had become connected across kilometres of landscape.

Nyayanga therefore gives us three separate conclusions.

**Oldowan technology was already versatile.  
The behaviour was not securely tied to Homo.  
And by at least 2.6 million years ago, stone could be carried significant distances because some rock was more useful than others.**

The next fossil finally gives us a jaw that researchers have assigned to the earliest known **Homo**. But even there, one mandible cannot tell us when an entire genus began.
