### THE PROBLEM WITH “HANDY MAN”

Names can preserve an idea long after the evidence becomes more complicated.

In 1964, Louis Leakey, Phillip Tobias and John Napier named **Homo habilis** from fossils at Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania. The name means “handy man.” The type specimen, **OH 7**, included parts of a juvenile skull and lower jaw together with hand bones, from deposits roughly 1.8 million years old.[1]

The name fitted an attractive story.

Oldowan stone tools occurred at Olduvai. The new hominin seemed anatomically closer to later humans than the robust australopith also present there. It was tempting to connect the two and imagine the first member of our genus as the maker whose clever hands explained the archaeological record.

But a species name is not behavioural evidence.

We now know that stone manufacture began long before OH 7. Lomekwi reaches back to about 3.3 million years ago. Nyayanga places Oldowan technology beside *Paranthropus* fossils. The archaeological record therefore prevents “habilis” from being read literally as **the** first toolmaker.

The fossil itself became more complicated too.

OH 7 was distorted after burial, especially its mandible. In 2015, Fred Spoor and colleagues used CT imaging and virtual reconstruction to restore its jaw and cranial fragments more closely to their original shapes.[2]

The reconstructed mandible was surprisingly primitive. Its dental arcade was long and narrow, more like **Australopithecus afarensis** than the broader, more parabolic arrangement of later *Homo*.[2]

Its reconstructed brain told a different story.

The team's endocranial estimate, approximately **729–824 cubic centimetres**, was larger than older estimates for OH 7 and overlapped substantially with the range of other early *Homo* fossils, including forms usually separated taxonomically.[2]

That combination undermines another simple progression.

A larger brain did not automatically arrive with a uniformly modern-looking jaw. Nor did different early *Homo* forms separate cleanly by brain size. Spoor and colleagues found more striking diversity in jaw shape than in endocranial volume.[2]

The reconstruction also sharpened a taxonomic problem.

OH 7's jaw differed strongly from fossils commonly assigned to **Homo rudolfensis** and from an older Hadar maxilla already attributed to *Homo*. The authors argued that this amount of shape variation was difficult to fit into one highly variable early-human species and instead supported multiple deep-rooted lineages within early *Homo*.[2]

This conclusion remains an interpretation of fragmentary populations, not a census of species. Fossil samples are small; sex, age, geography and time can all contribute to variation.

But the type specimen now makes one point especially difficult to avoid:

**Early Homo was not one neat package of a bigger brain, smaller teeth, humanlike jaws and stone tools appearing together.**

Different anatomical systems and behaviours were changing on different schedules.

The next fossils from Kenya make that diversity visible in two very different skulls—one with a broad, large face and another much smaller—once placed uneasily under the same early-human label.
