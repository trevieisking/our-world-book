### THE EVIDENCE FOR SOMETHING MISSING

A skeleton without tail bones does not automatically belong to a tailless animal. Small elements can be lost before burial, overlooked during collection or separated from the rest of the body. To establish an evolutionary loss, the investigation needs more than an empty space in a museum reconstruction.

In 2004, Masato Nakatsukasa and colleagues re-examined vertebrae attributed to the early ape then called *Proconsul heseloni*. Two had been interpreted as tail elements. Their damaged shapes instead supported identification as bones from the lower back.[1]

A third specimen came from the sacrum, the part of the backbone associated with the pelvis. Despite distortion, its anatomy supported the absence of a substantial external tail. The argument therefore concerned the structure that would have connected with a tail, rather than merely a failure to find one.[1]

The species was later transferred to *Ekembo* in a systematic revision separating two sets of early ape fossils. Following that change matters when comparing studies: the older name and the newer one do not refer to two independently discovered examples of tail loss.[2]

Comparisons with living animals can test how much an incomplete skeleton predicts. Sayaka Tojima studied bones from 89 hybrid macaques whose tails varied considerably in length. Measurements near the tail's base supported equations for estimating relative tail length, with further tests using other catarrhines.[3]

Such calibration gives anatomical interpretation a measurable basis. It also establishes limits. An equation developed from a particular range of animals does not become a direct measurement of every extinct species, especially when the relevant bones are incomplete or deformed.

DNA approaches the problem differently. In 2024, Bo Xia and colleagues identified an inserted repetitive DNA element within the gene *TBXT*. Acting with another nearby element, it altered how the gene's RNA message was assembled, producing a version that omitted one coding segment.[4]

Mouse experiments tested the consequences of producing altered forms of that message. Some designs generated shortened or absent tails, but simply transferring the paired inserted elements did not produce an obvious adult-tail change. The results supported a developmental contribution while showing that molecular context mattered.[4]

This moves the explanation beyond a resemblance between sequences. A proposed link can be altered and its consequences examined. Yet a successful experimental mechanism is not a complete reconstruction of the historical population in which the change arose.

The difference between how and why remains important. Demonstrating that a developmental change can reduce a tail does not demonstrate the advantage that favoured it, how it spread or whether other changes were required. Those questions need their own evidence rather than answers borrowed from the experiment.

Fossils and genes consequently address different parts of the loss. One establishes anatomy in an ancient body; another identifies a mechanism capable of changing development. Neither alone makes tail reduction the cause of every later feature of an ape.

The next step is therefore not to imagine that removing a tail creates a modern human walker. We must examine the rest of the body: which joints could support an upright posture, which could move below branches, and how confidently either capacity can be reconstructed.
