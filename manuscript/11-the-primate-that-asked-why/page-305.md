### RECOGNISING A RELATIVE

The search for our history does not begin with an ancient face that looks almost human. It begins with comparisons: which features are shared, which were inherited, and which appeared independently? A familiar outline can suggest a relationship. Establishing that relationship requires evidence capable of surviving a less forgiving test.

Living primates offer one starting point. In 2023, Lukas Kuderna and colleagues assembled genome data from 233 species. They used those sequences, together with fossil calibrations, to reconstruct relationships and estimate when branches separated. The study also found differences in mutation rates between species.[1]

The resulting tree was not a sequence in which each living species represented an earlier stage of another. Genetic comparisons concern shared ancestry. They do not turn a surviving relative into an unchanged ancestor, or provide DNA from every extinct animal whose place we would like to determine.

For fossils near the beginning of the primate lineage, the evidence may be much smaller than a face. Teeth assigned to *Purgatorius* were described in 2021 from early Paleocene deposits in northeastern Montana. Differences among the specimens supported the presence of at least two species soon after the end-Cretaceous boundary.[2]

The researchers argued that this early diversity favoured an origin extending into the Cretaceous. The specimens themselves, however, came from younger rocks. An inferred history before their burial is not a newly discovered Cretaceous fossil, and a local first appearance is not automatically the first existence of the group.[2]

The distinction between stem and crown matters here too. A **stem primate** lies outside the group descended from the last common ancestor of living primates, but closer to that group than to its living relatives. The study treated *Purgatorius* and related extinct forms within this broader primate history, not as miniature versions of modern monkeys.[2]

Movement required another archive. In 2015, Stephen Chester and colleagues described ankle bones from Paleocene Montana that they attributed to *Purgatorius*. Their joint surfaces indicated a mobile ankle consistent with moving through trees, and analyses incorporating those features supported primate affinities.[3]

The bones were not attached to diagnostic teeth in one articulated skeleton. Their attribution depended on size, anatomical comparisons and the fossil assemblage in which they occurred. The evidence therefore contains two linked propositions: what kind of ankle the bones represent, and which animal possessed it.[3]

Keeping those propositions visible makes the reconstruction stronger, not weaker. A later associated skeleton could test the identification. A better comparison of joint function could refine the movement inferred. Neither investigation requires first pretending that the missing body has already been found.

The scale is small, but the historical question is substantial. The oldest preserved fragments can help establish the sequence in which anatomical capacities appeared. They cannot, by themselves, tell us which individual stood at the beginning of every later branch, or reconstruct every task performed by the living animal.

This chapter will follow that distinction as the evidence grows from isolated parts into more complete bodies. Our own place in the story gives us a reason to ask the questions; it does not supply their answers. Next comes an animal whose foot and skull preserve a revealing mismatch: effective grasping without the complete visual arrangement familiar from living primates.
