### TWO BRANCHES, NOT TWO STEPS

The relationship between Old World monkeys and apes is a fork, not a succession of living animals. Both belong to the broader catarrhine group. To investigate their separation, we need fossils that establish combinations near the fork and others that can be assigned to its developing branches.[1,2]

A partial skull from Saudi Arabia supplied one such combination. Described in 2010 as *Saadanius hijazensis*, it came from a succession assigned to roughly 29–28 million years ago. Its ear region included a bony tube, while the skull lacked the distinctive derived features needed to place it securely within either the ape or Old World monkey branch.[1]

The researchers instead placed it among advanced stem catarrhines. That made it useful for reconstructing anatomy near the shared ancestry of the two groups. It did not identify the individual species from which both necessarily descended. Nearness in a reconstructed tree is not the same as a documented parent–descendant relationship.

Nor can the age of a stem relative automatically set the latest possible date before a crown group began. An older-looking combination can persist after another branch has acquired different features. To decide when a split happened, the distribution of characters must be compared with the chronology rather than substituted for it.

In 2013, Nancy Stevens and colleagues reported two informative fossils from Tanzania's Rukwa Rift. *Rukwapithecus* was represented by a partial lower jaw with teeth supporting its placement among early ape relatives. A lower third molar distinguished *Nsungwepithecus*, assigned to the Old World monkey branch.[2]

The fossil-bearing stratum was dated to about 25.2 million years ago. Provided the anatomical assignments hold, both branches already existed by then. The date is therefore a constraint on their separation, not a measurement of the moment when one ancestral population became two.[2]

The bodies behind those teeth require further evidence. In 2019, Sergio Almécija and colleagues examined an upper thigh bone attributed to *Aegyptopithecus*, an Oligocene stem catarrhine from Egypt. Its size and the other primates known from the locality supported the attribution; it was not attached to a diagnostic skull.[3]

The upper end of the thigh bone forms part of the hip joint. Three-dimensional comparisons placed its shape outside the range of living anthropoids. Evolutionary modelling supported subsequent changes in both Old World monkeys and apes, rather than treating one living group as an unchanged model for the ancestor.[3]

The reconstructed shape informs movement because the surfaces of a joint constrain how bones can meet. It does not preserve the full range of actions that its owner performed. Muscles, surrounding tissues and the rest of the skeleton would also have mattered.

Together, these discoveries make the fork more informative than a simple diagram. A skull records an anatomical combination; teeth support branch assignments; a dated layer constrains their presence; a hip challenges assumptions about the ancestral body. Their contributions strengthen one another without answering identical questions.

The emerging ape branch still contained possibilities absent from the living world. To understand them, we must now look beyond one familiar body form—and beyond the best-sampled parts of the African continent.
