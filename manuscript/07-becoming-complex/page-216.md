# CHAPTER SEVEN — BECOMING COMPLEX
## Page 216 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### THE COMPLEX CELL IS ASSEMBLED

We began Chapter Seven with a partnership.

An archaeal lineage.

A bacterial endosymbiont.

By LECA, the result is no longer describable as either one.

The cell has been rebuilt.

A nucleus separates the genome from translation.

Endomembranes create internal reaction spaces.

A cytoskeleton moves cargo and chromosomes.

Membrane trafficking controls exchange between compartments.

Mitochondria convert energy and supply essential metabolic functions.

Genes from the endosymbiont have moved into the nuclear genome.

Linear chromosomes require specialised ends and segregation sites.

Histones organise chromatin.

Introns interrupt genes.

A spliceosome removes them.

Mitosis distributes chromosomes.

Meiosis reshuffles them.

Sex mixes lineages.

This is already extraordinary complexity.

Yet almost all of it can exist inside one microscopic cell.

That is important.

The history of complex life does not begin with multicellular organisms.

It begins with **complex unicellular eukaryotes**.

A single cell can hunt.

Swim.

Sense light.

Build an armour.

Generate electrical signals.

Change shape.

Capture prey.

Fuse with another cell.

Coordinate hundreds of internal compartments.

Some modern protists are behaviourally and structurally more elaborate than many multicellular organisms.

The transition from prokaryotic to eukaryotic organisation therefore created a vast evolutionary world before animals or plants existed.

When did this world appear?

The answer remains frustratingly broad.

Molecular clocks place eukaryogenesis across a wide Proterozoic interval.

The 2025 dated-gene-duplication study places much host cellular elaboration broadly between about 3.0 and 2.25 billion years ago and mitochondrial acquisition later in its reconstructed sequence.

Other molecular estimates place mitochondrial acquisition and LECA younger.

Fossils provide minimum ages, but the oldest candidates are debated.

Cell shape alone rarely proves eukaryotic identity.

Large size is not enough.

Bacteria can be enormous.

Internal structures are rarely preserved.

Sterane biomarkers once thought to push eukaryotes very deep into the Archean have faced contamination and source problems.

So the earliest eukaryotic history cannot be read from one fossil or one molecular clock.

The best approach is convergence.

Genes tell us what LECA possessed.

Asgard archaea constrain the host lineage.

Mitochondrial biology reveals endosymbiosis.

Fossils show when recognisably eukaryotic morphologies appear.

Molecular clocks bracket divergences.

Geochemistry reconstructs the environments in which those cells lived.

None is sufficient alone.

Together they reveal a new cellular world emerging during the Proterozoic.

The next transition is easier to imagine but no less important.

A cell does not have to live alone.

Bacteria and archaea had formed colonies and mats for billions of years.

The new question is different:

**when do cells in a group surrender enough independence to become one multicellular organism?**

Cells must adhere.

Communicate.

Coordinate division.

Control cheating.

Specialise.

Pass through a reproductive bottleneck.

Some cells may sacrifice their own reproduction for the body.

Complex multicellularity will evolve more than once.

Animals are not the first attempt.

Plants are not the only solution.

Fungi use another architecture.

Algae explore several.

The path from one complex cell to many cooperating cells is not one invention.

It is a repeated evolutionary experiment.

And it unfolds on a planet entering another period of extraordinary environmental instability.

Continents rearrange.

Ice expands.

At times, glaciers may reach tropical latitudes.

The world approaches **Snowball Earth**.

Before the great freezes, however, eukaryotic cells have to leave a fossil record we can recognise.

The final block of this chapter follows those cells outward:

from solitary eukaryotes,

to multicellular experiments,

through global glaciations,

and into the strange ecosystems of the Ediacaran world.

## Research / fact-check notes
- LECA reconstruction (PLOS Biology 2024 and system-specific literature) supports extensive nuclear, cytoskeletal, endomembrane, mitochondrial, mitotic, meiotic and spliceosomal complexity.
- Eukaryogenesis/LECA timing remains model-dependent and fossil evidence provides minimum constraints rather than an exact origin date.
- Complex unicellular eukaryotes precede complex multicellularity conceptually and historically.
- Multicellularity evolved independently in several eukaryotic lineages.

**Decision:** pages 209–216 close the cellular-complexity block and transition from complex unicellular eukaryotes to multicellularity/environmental change.

**Transition:** pages 217–224 will examine early eukaryotic fossils, independent origins of multicellularity, Snowball Earth episodes and the Ediacaran biota, ending at the threshold of Cambrian ecological diversification.
