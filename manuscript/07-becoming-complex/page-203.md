# CHAPTER SEVEN — BECOMING COMPLEX
## Page 203 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### MEETING A LIVING ASGARD ARCHAEON

Environmental genomes tell us what genes exist.

They do not tell us exactly how a cell lives.

The first cultured Asgard archaeon changed that.

Researchers collected sediment from the deep sea and maintained enrichment cultures for years.

The organisms grew painfully slowly.

Eventually, in work reported in 2020, they isolated an archaeon named **Candidatus Prometheoarchaeum syntrophicum**.

Its cells were small.

No nucleus.

No mitochondria.

No recognisable phagocytic machinery.

But the cell surface produced long, branching membrane protrusions.

The archaeon also depended on close metabolic partners.

It degraded amino acids and released products including hydrogen and formate.

Partner microorganisms consumed those products.

Their removal helped make the archaeon's own metabolism energetically favourable.

This is **syntrophy** again.

We met the principle in Archean microbial ecosystems.

Here it becomes relevant to the origin of complex cells.

A host and a future symbiont do not necessarily begin with one cell swallowing another in the modern sense.

They can begin by living extremely close together because their metabolisms depend on one another.

Membrane protrusions can increase contact.

Sticky extracellular material can hold partners nearby.

Over evolutionary time, physical association can become more intimate.

The cultured Asgard archaeon inspired the **entangle–engulf–endogenize** model.

In that scenario, an archaeal cell with protrusions becomes physically entangled with partner bacteria.

Membranes gradually surround a partner.

Eventually the bacterium becomes internalised.

This is one proposed route to endosymbiosis.

It is not a filmed replay of eukaryogenesis.

Prometheoarchaeum is alive today.

It is separated from the eukaryotic ancestor by billions of years of independent evolution.

Its specific lifestyle may be derived.

Other Asgard lineages differ metabolically and morphologically.

The original eukaryotic host is extinct.

Still, the cultured cell proves something important.

Archaea closely related to the eukaryotic branch can possess dynamic protrusions and intimate syntrophic lifestyles without already being eukaryotes.

The gap between a small archaeal cell and endosymbiosis is therefore not a choice between:

perfectly rigid sphere,

or fully developed phagocytosis.

Intermediate physical relationships are possible.

Cultivation since 2020 has revealed additional Asgard representatives and broader diversity.

The 2026 *Nature Reviews Microbiology* synthesis emphasises that Asgard archaea occupy varied environments and possess diverse metabolic repertoires.

Some characterised cells show unusual morphologies.

Their biology is more diverse than one cultured species suggested.

This strengthens one lesson while weakening another.

The lesson strengthened:

Asgard archaea provide real cellular systems in which to test ideas about eukaryotic origins.

The lesson weakened:

there is no reason to assume that one living Asgard morphology is a frozen portrait of the ancestral host.

Living Asgards are **cousins**, not ancestors.

That distinction matters throughout evolutionary reconstruction.

A chimpanzee is not an ancestral human.

A lungfish is not the ancestor that walked onto land.

Prometheoarchaeum is not the cell that became us.

It preserves combinations of traits that help constrain what archaeal cells can do.

The ancestral host also carried genes.

And those genes contain an even more surprising message.

For decades, eukaryotic genomes were described as a near-even merger:

archaeal genes for information,

bacterial genes for metabolism.

A 2026 analysis argues that this picture substantially underestimates the Asgard contribution.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Imachi et al. (2020), *Nature*: first cultured Asgard archaeon, *Candidatus Prometheoarchaeum syntrophicum*, is a slow-growing syntroph with long membrane protrusions.
- “Entangle–engulf–endogenize” is one proposed model inspired by its cell biology, not direct evidence of the historical endosymbiotic mechanism.
- Panagiotou et al. (2026), *Nature Reviews Microbiology*: cultured and genomic Asgard diversity shows varied ecology/cell biology and a central role in eukaryogenesis.
- No living Asgard lineage is treated as the direct ancestor of eukaryotes.

**Decision:** use cultured Asgard biology to constrain possible interactions, not to recreate the ancestral host by analogy alone.
