# CHAPTER SEVEN — BECOMING COMPLEX
## Page 218 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### MOLECULAR FOSSILS REVEAL A HIDDEN BIOTA

A body fossil preserves shape.

A **molecular fossil** preserves chemistry.

Some biological lipids are unusually resistant to complete destruction.

Burial, heat and pressure alter them into stable hydrocarbons.

If the original molecule was strongly associated with a particular biological pathway, its altered product can provide evidence even when the organism itself left no recognisable body.

Eukaryotes make **sterols**.

Cholesterol is one familiar example.

After burial, sterols can become **steranes**.

For years, the scarcity of conventional steranes in much of the mid-Proterozoic record created a paradox.

Fossils and molecular clocks indicated eukaryotes existed.

Yet the molecular biomarker record seemed almost entirely bacterial.

Had eukaryotes been extremely rare?

Were the fossils misleading?

Were the biomarkers missing something?

In 2023, Jochen Brocks and colleagues reported an answer hidden in the chemistry.

They searched not only for the modern-style steranes commonly used as eukaryotic biomarkers, but for **protosteroids** — molecular products predicted from early stages of sterol biosynthesis.

These compounds occur widely in rocks from roughly **1.64 billion to 800 million years ago**.

The researchers proposed a widespread **protosterol biota**.

Some organisms producing these early sterol intermediates may have been stem-group eukaryotes.

Some could have been bacteria with sterol-related pathways.

The exact biological producers are not individually identified by the molecule alone.

But the record reveals that older forms of steroid biosynthesis were ecologically significant long before modern eukaryotic steranes became abundant.

This changes how we imagine early eukaryote ecology.

Modern sterol pathways were not necessarily present in their final form from the beginning.

Biosynthetic pathways themselves evolved.

An intermediate that looks “unfinished” from the perspective of a modern cell may have been the normal end product of an ancient organism.

The biomarker problem had partly been created by searching the rocks only for modern chemistry.

There is a caution.

Molecular fossils are exceptionally vulnerable to contamination.

Oil can migrate.

Drilling introduces hydrocarbons.

Laboratory handling introduces modern organics.

Younger material can move through older rocks.

Several spectacular claims for Archean eukaryotic steranes were weakened when contamination and geological context were examined more carefully.

A biomarker is therefore only as old as its **syngeneity** can be demonstrated.

This repeats a lesson from the first-life record.

A molecule inside an ancient rock is not automatically an ancient molecule.

Good biomarker studies use:

careful sampling,

blank controls,

thermal maturity,

stratigraphic consistency,

and chemical distributions difficult to explain by contamination.

Even then, biological specificity matters.

Some sterols once considered uniquely animal have later been found in unicellular eukaryotes.

One molecule rarely identifies a whole organism with certainty.

The protosterol record is powerful because it fills an ecological gap.

It suggests ancient steroid-producing organisms were present and important during the long interval when body fossils show eukaryotes but conventional modern-type steranes are scarce.

Then, during the Tonian Period, roughly one billion to 720 million years ago, modern eukaryotic sterane signals become much more prominent.

This is part of a wider ecological expansion.

Algae diversify.

Planktonic ecosystems change.

Multicellular forms become more conspicuous.

The eukaryotic world is no longer confined mostly to restricted benthic niches.

And one cellular innovation appears again and again across unrelated lineages.

Cells stay together.

Multicellularity has begun many times.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Brocks et al. (2023), *Nature*: protosteroids reveal an ecologically prominent “protosterol biota” from at least ~1.64 to ~0.8 Ga, interpreted as including deep-branching stem eukaryotes and possibly sterol-producing bacteria.
- Conventional sterane absence does not equal eukaryote absence.
- Archean/very old biomarker claims require especially strict contamination/syngeneity controls.
- Biomarkers can diagnose pathways/ecology more securely than exact taxonomic identity.

**Decision:** molecular fossils reveal hidden biochemical/ecological history while retaining contamination and producer-specificity limits.
