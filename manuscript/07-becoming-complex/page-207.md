# CHAPTER SEVEN — BECOMING COMPLEX
## Page 207 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### FECA IS NOT LECA

Evolutionary biologists use two useful landmarks when discussing eukaryogenesis.

**FECA**:

the First Eukaryotic Common Ancestor.

**LECA**:

the Last Eukaryotic Common Ancestor.

The names can mislead if imagined as two fossil species standing at opposite ends of a straight line.

They are conceptual points on a branching evolutionary history.

FECA marks the beginning of the lineage that would eventually lead to all living eukaryotes after it diverged from its closest archaeal relatives.

LECA is the most recent ancestral population from which all living eukaryotic lineages descend.

Between them lies the evolutionary interval where the characteristic eukaryotic cell was assembled.

That interval may have lasted hundreds of millions of years.

We have no complete fossil sequence through it.

Most intermediate lineages became extinct.

Living eukaryotes only preserve features that survived into LECA or evolved later.

Living Asgard archaea preserve related archaeal biology but not the exact FECA state.

Gene trees therefore become essential.

If a cellular system is distributed across the deepest eukaryotic branches and its genes trace back before those branches split, the simplest explanation is often that LECA already possessed it.

That reconstruction makes LECA remarkably complex.

It had mitochondria.

A nucleus or nuclear system.

An endomembrane network.

A cytoskeleton.

Membrane trafficking.

Complex chromosome replication and segregation.

Ubiquitin-based protein regulation.

Spliceosomal introns and RNA-processing machinery.

A sophisticated translation system.

Many analyses also infer that key components of sexual or meiotic reproduction were already present or emerging very early.

LECA was not a halfway cell.

It was recognisably eukaryotic.

This creates the great missing interval.

Where are the intermediate forms?

Mostly gone.

The fossil record rarely preserves subcellular machinery.

A round fossil cell does not reveal whether it possessed a nucleus, mitochondria or an actin cytoskeleton.

Genes cannot fossilise directly.

And because FECA's descendants that did not reach LECA are extinct, we cannot sample their genomes.

This is why eukaryogenesis feels abrupt in reconstruction.

Not necessarily because the biology changed instantly.

Because our evidence jumps from archaeal relatives to a LECA already carrying a large toolkit.

The 2024 *Nature* review describes this as a long stem lineage between the first and last common ancestors of eukaryotes.

Its estimated timing spans a broad interval, roughly **2.7 to 1.8 billion years ago**, depending on fossils, molecular clocks and evolutionary assumptions.

That range is not the lifespan of one organism.

It brackets a historical transition whose internal chronology is poorly resolved.

The 2026 Asgard gene-origin study tries to order some of the events inside that gap.

Its results support an Asgard-derived FECA that already possessed significant cytoskeletal and endomembrane complexity before mitochondrial acquisition.

Under that model, mitochondria arrive comparatively late.

Other models place endosymbiosis earlier and argue that mitochondrial energetics enabled later cellular elaboration.

Neither sequence has a direct fossil test.

Both have genomic predictions.

The difference matters because it changes the causal story.

Did cellular complexity make endosymbiosis possible?

Or did endosymbiosis make extreme cellular complexity possible?

The answer remains unsettled.

What LECA tells us is the endpoint.

Whatever route evolution took, by the time living eukaryotic lineages began diverging, the major pieces were already integrated.

And one environmental factor cannot tell us their order.

Oxygen.

The GOE happened before or during the broad eukaryogenesis window.

But the deepest steps of eukaryotic cell evolution do not line up neatly with one oxygen threshold.

## Research / fact-check notes
- FECA and LECA bracket the stem-eukaryotic interval rather than representing two necessarily fossil-identifiable species.
- Comparative genomics reconstructs LECA as already possessing extensive eukaryotic cellular complexity and mitochondria.
- 2024 *Nature* review gives a broad ~2.7–1.8 Ga eukaryogenesis interval and stresses the long unsampled stem lineage.
- Tobiasson et al. (2026) favours substantial pre-mitochondrial Asgard-derived complexity; mitochondria-early models remain active.

**Decision:** separate the well-supported complexity of LECA from the unresolved ordering of innovations between FECA and LECA.
