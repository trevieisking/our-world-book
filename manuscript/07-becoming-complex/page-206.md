# CHAPTER SEVEN — BECOMING COMPLEX
## Page 206 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### THE GENOME MOVES TO THE HOST

A modern human mitochondrion contains only a tiny genome.

Thirteen protein-coding genes.

A handful of RNA genes.

The ancestral bacterium must have carried far more.

Where did the missing genes go?

Some were lost.

Many moved into the host nuclear genome.

This is **endosymbiotic gene transfer**.

It is not only an ancient idea inferred from modern gene trees.

Gene transfer from organelles to nuclei still occurs.

Fragments of mitochondrial DNA can integrate into nuclear chromosomes.

Over evolutionary time, if a transferred gene becomes expressed correctly and its protein can be sent back into the mitochondrion, the original mitochondrial copy can become dispensable.

Repeat the process across hundreds of millions of years and the endosymbiont genome shrinks.

The organelle loses autonomy.

Most mitochondrial proteins in modern eukaryotes are now encoded in the nucleus.

They are synthesised outside the mitochondrion and imported through specialised protein-translocation machinery.

This creates a remarkable inversion.

The descendant of the host now manufactures much of the machinery required to maintain the descendant of the bacterium.

The endosymbiont has become an organelle under cellular control.

Yet mitochondria never became ordinary internal compartments.

They retain membranes inherited from their bacterial ancestry.

They divide from pre-existing mitochondria.

They retain a small genome in most lineages.

Their central energy-conversion machinery sits in a membrane homologous to the bacterial cytoplasmic membrane.

Even eukaryotes that appear to have “lost” mitochondria tell the same story.

For years, some anaerobic protists were classified as primitively amitochondriate.

Perhaps, researchers thought, they split from the eukaryotic tree before mitochondrial endosymbiosis.

That would have provided living examples of pre-mitochondrial eukaryotes.

The idea collapsed.

Hydrogenosomes and mitosomes — highly modified mitochondrial-related organelles — were found across supposedly amitochondriate groups.

In 2016, researchers described *Monocercomonoides*, a eukaryote that appears to have lost mitochondrial organelles completely.

But its evolutionary history indicates **secondary loss**.

Its ancestors had mitochondria.

No known living eukaryotic lineage provides a clear example of a cell whose ancestry never included mitochondria.

This strongly constrains eukaryogenesis.

By the time all living eukaryotic lineages shared their last common ancestor, the mitochondrial symbiosis had already happened.

The organelle also solved more than one problem.

Modern mitochondria participate in:

ATP production.

Iron–sulfur-cluster assembly.

Lipid metabolism.

Programmed cell death.

Metabolite exchange.

Redox control.

Many of these functions evolved or expanded after endosymbiosis.

The earliest symbiont should not be imagined as a modern mitochondrion dropped intact into an archaeal cell.

Integration itself created new biology.

Host proteins evolved targeting signals.

Import channels evolved.

Gene regulation had to coordinate nuclear and organellar genomes.

Cell division had to ensure daughters inherited mitochondria.

Conflict had to be controlled.

The transition from bacterium to organelle was therefore a long evolutionary process.

And it happened during another long process:

the transition from the **first eukaryotic common ancestor** to the **last eukaryotic common ancestor**.

Those two names sound similar.

They mark very different points.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Endosymbiotic gene transfer from mitochondria to nuclear genomes is established and continues in modern eukaryotic evolution.
- Most mitochondrial proteins are nuclear encoded and imported.
- No accepted living lineage is ancestrally pre-mitochondrial; highly reduced/absent mitochondrial organelles are secondary states.
- *Monocercomonoides* demonstrates complete secondary mitochondrial-organelle loss rather than a surviving pre-mitochondrial eukaryote.

**Decision:** LECA's mitochondrial ancestry is treated as strongly established; modern mitochondrial complexity is not projected intact onto the original bacterium.
