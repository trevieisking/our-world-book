# CHAPTER SEVEN — BECOMING COMPLEX
## Page 220 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### WHEN COOPERATION BECOMES AN ORGANISM

Microbes had cooperated for billions of years.

A biofilm is cooperative.

A microbial mat is organised.

Syntrophic partners depend on one another.

Yet the cells usually remain separate evolutionary individuals.

Multicellularity crosses a different boundary.

The group itself begins to reproduce as a unit.

Development becomes predictable.

Cell fates become coordinated.

Selection can act on the architecture of the whole body.

There are two broad routes.

**Clonal multicellularity** begins when descendants remain attached after cell division.

Cells in the group are close genetic relatives.

This reduces internal genetic conflict.

Animals, plants and many algae develop clonally.

**Aggregative multicellularity** begins when originally independent cells come together.

Social amoebae provide a famous modern example.

Under starvation, separate cells aggregate into a multicellular structure.

Some become reproductive spores.

Others build a stalk and die.

Relatedness and cheating become central evolutionary issues.

A 2026 *Nature* study discovered an especially interesting link near animal origins.

A unicellular relative of animals was shown to form aggregative multicellular structures with cell differentiation.

The organism is not an animal ancestor frozen in time.

But it shows that developmental behaviours relevant to multicellularity existed among close unicellular relatives and that the path toward animal multicellularity may have drawn on more than one mode of cellular cooperation.

This reinforces a broader lesson.

The genetic ingredients for multicellularity often predate the multicellular body.

Cell adhesion proteins have unicellular functions.

Signalling pathways regulate interactions with the environment.

Transcription factors already control cell states.

Cytoskeletal proteins already change shape.

Extracellular matrices already attach cells to surfaces.

Evolution can repurpose those systems so cells regulate **one another**.

Once that happens, developmental information emerges.

A cell does not simply respond to temperature or nutrients.

It responds to its position in a body.

Neighbouring cells send signals.

Genes turn on differently in different places.

Division orientation changes shape.

Programmed cell death can sculpt structure.

Some cells become reproductive.

Others become somatic.

The evolutionary transition is therefore not from:

one cell -> many cells.

It is from:

many cells -> **coordinated developmental system**.

Complex eukaryotic cells make this transition easier in some ways.

A cytoskeleton provides shape and adhesion control.

Endomembranes support secretion.

Sex and large genomes generate regulatory variation.

Mitochondria supply energy.

But none guarantees multicellularity.

Most eukaryotic diversity remains unicellular.

Multicellularity evolves when ecology rewards bodies and evolution solves internal cooperation.

Environmental change can alter those rewards.

Predators favour larger size.

Nutrient gradients favour division of labour.

Light competition favours elevated structures.

Stable surfaces favour attachment.

Snowball Earth changes all of these conditions at once.

During the Cryogenian Period, ice repeatedly spreads to low latitudes.

Oceans are reorganised.

Light, nutrients, temperature and circulation change dramatically.

Eukaryotic life survives.

Some lineages diversify afterwards.

It is tempting to say the ice created complex life.

That would repeat the oxygen mistake.

Climate changes the opportunity landscape.

Evolution supplies the innovations.

The next two glaciations are among the most extreme climate events recorded in Earth history.

Their name sounds absolute.

**Snowball Earth.**

The evidence for extreme low-latitude glaciation is strong.

How completely the oceans froze — and exactly how life survived — remains a more complicated question.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Clonal and aggregative multicellularity provide distinct evolutionary routes and different conflict/relatedness problems.
- Multicellularity repeatedly co-opts older adhesion, signalling, cytoskeletal and regulatory systems.
- Li et al. (2026), *Nature*: a unicellular animal relative exhibits aggregative multicellularity and differentiated states, constraining possible precursors without representing the animal ancestor.
- Multicellularity is not attributed to one environmental trigger.

**Decision:** distinguish the origin of multicellularity from the later ecological expansion of multicellular organisms.
