# CHAPTER SEVEN — BECOMING COMPLEX
## Page 210 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### THE CELL LEARNS TO MOVE ITS INSIDE

A large cell faces a transport problem.

Diffusion is slow across long distances.

Membranes have to change shape.

Organelles have to move.

Chromosomes have to separate.

Food particles may have to be engulfed.

A eukaryotic cell solves these problems with a **cytoskeleton**.

Actin forms dynamic filaments.

Tubulin forms microtubules.

Motor proteins walk along those tracks.

Other proteins nucleate, cut, branch and stabilise polymers.

Together they turn the interior of the cell into an active mechanical system.

This machinery did not appear without precursors.

Bacteria and archaea already possess cytoskeletal proteins.

FtsZ, a tubulin relative, helps many bacteria divide.

Actin-like proteins occur widely in prokaryotes.

Archaea possess several division and shape-control systems.

Asgard archaea make the connection especially striking.

Their genomes encode actin homologues.

Profilin-like actin regulators.

Tubulin-related proteins.

ESCRT membrane-remodelling machinery.

Ubiquitin-related systems.

Small GTPases and trafficking-associated proteins.

Biochemical studies show that some Asgard proteins behave in recognisably eukaryote-like ways.

That does not mean Asgard cells already possessed a eukaryotic cytoskeleton.

A toolkit is not an architecture.

What changed during eukaryogenesis was the scale, regulation and integration of these systems.

Gene duplication is central.

Duplicate a protein and the two copies can diverge.

One retains the original function.

Another becomes specialised.

Repeat that process and a small ancestral family becomes a network.

A 2026 study of Asgard evolution found an unusual combination of extensive horizontal gene transfer and substantial gene duplication, reinforcing the idea that Asgard lineages possessed evolutionary mechanisms capable of expanding cellular toolkits before LECA.

Even more strikingly, a 2025 *Nature* molecular-dating study analysed duplications of eukaryotic gene families.

Its reconstruction placed elaborated cytoskeletal, membrane-trafficking, endomembrane, phagocytosis-related and nuclear systems **before** mitochondrial endosymbiosis, broadly between about 3.0 and 2.25 billion years ago under its model.

The authors called this a **complexified archaeon, late-mitochondrion** scenario.

The result is important.

It is also model-dependent.

Ancient gene duplications are difficult to date.

Molecular clocks require calibrations.

Extinct lineages create hidden branches.

A later endosymbiosis can transfer genes whose subsequent duplications complicate ordering.

Other models still place mitochondrial acquisition earlier.

So the dates should not be read as a fossil stopwatch.

The broader lesson is stronger.

Substantial eukaryotic-like cellular complexity can plausibly evolve from archaeal molecular systems before the cell reaches the final LECA state.

The cytoskeleton also links several puzzles that are often discussed separately.

Membrane trafficking needs force.

Nuclear division needs chromosome movement.

Cell shape needs internal support.

Engulfment needs actin-driven membrane deformation.

Sexual cell fusion requires membrane and cytoskeletal control.

Once these systems become integrated, cellular innovations stop being independent modules.

A better actin network can make new forms of feeding possible.

New feeding can favour larger cells.

Larger cells increase the value of internal transport.

Internal transport supports more compartments.

More compartments create new regulatory possibilities.

Complexity can become self-reinforcing.

But one particular cytoskeletal ability is central to the mitochondrial-origin debate.

Can the host engulf another cell?

Could phagocytosis have existed before mitochondria?

The answer determines whether endosymbiosis required an already highly dynamic host.

And current evidence is beginning to narrow the possibilities.

## Research / fact-check notes
- LECA had complex actin/tubulin cytoskeleton, motors and membrane-trafficking systems.
- Asgard archaea encode cytoskeletal and membrane-remodelling homologues but are not full eukaryotic cytoskeletons.
- Manzano-Morales & Gabaldón (2026), *Nature Communications*: Asgard evolution combines substantial HGT with eukaryotic-like gene duplication patterns.
- Kay et al. (2025), *Nature*: molecular dating of pre-LECA duplications supports substantial host cytoskeletal/endomembrane complexity before mitochondrial acquisition; model-dependent.

**Decision:** host-complexity-first evidence is included as a strong recent model, not universal consensus.
