# CHAPTER SEVEN — BECOMING COMPLEX
## Page 213 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### GENES IN PIECES

A bacterial protein-coding gene is often close to continuous.

Read the DNA.

Make RNA.

Translate the RNA.

Eukaryotic genes are frequently interrupted.

Coding regions called **exons** are separated by **introns**.

The cell first copies the whole region into precursor messenger RNA.

Then it removes the introns.

Exons are joined.

Only then is the mature message exported and translated.

The machine that performs most of this editing is the **spliceosome**.

It is enormous.

Built from small nuclear RNAs and many proteins.

Highly dynamic.

Assembled and remodelled in stages.

At first glance, such a machine seems like a late luxury.

Comparative genomics says otherwise.

LECA already possessed a sophisticated spliceosomal system.

Shared intron positions across distantly related eukaryotes indicate that many introns were already present before the deepest living eukaryotic lineages diverged.

Reconstructions suggest LECA's genes may have been surprisingly intron-rich.

The spliceosome therefore belongs inside eukaryogenesis itself.

Where did the introns come from?

One influential model begins with **group II introns**.

These are mobile genetic elements found in bacteria and organelles.

They can remove themselves from RNA through a chemical reaction that resembles the chemistry of spliceosomal splicing.

Structural similarities also link parts of group II introns to spliceosomal small RNAs.

Because the mitochondrial ancestor was bacterial, one hypothesis proposes that group II introns entered the host genome during or after mitochondrial endosymbiosis.

They proliferated.

Their self-splicing capacity degraded.

Separate RNA and protein factors increasingly performed the chemistry in trans.

A primitive spliceosome emerged.

This is a strong mechanistic hypothesis.

It is not a filmed history.

Group II intron ancestry of spliceosomal introns is widely supported.

Exactly when the invasion happened, how many times, and whether the mitochondrion was the sole relevant source remain debated.

The 2025 gene-duplication chronology also complicates simplistic timing.

Its reconstructed host had substantial cellular complexity before mitochondrial acquisition, while some later bacterial-derived innovations accumulated afterwards.

The spliceosomal system likely evolved across that transitional interval rather than appearing in one event.

Introns create costs.

Every intron must be recognised and removed correctly.

Splicing takes time.

Mistakes can destroy a protein message.

Why tolerate them?

Once present, introns also create possibilities.

Exons can be combined differently.

RNA processing becomes regulated.

Introns can contain regulatory sequences.

Recombination can shuffle gene segments.

New transcripts can arise without changing the underlying coding sequence in a simple one-to-one way.

But we should not reverse-engineer their origin from their modern usefulness.

Introns may have begun as genomic parasites and only later became integrated into regulation.

Evolution frequently domesticates problems.

A selfish element becomes machinery.

A foreign gene becomes essential.

A symbiont becomes a mitochondrion.

A genome invaded by introns evolves a spliceosome.

The nucleus may have made that burden manageable by separating transcription from translation.

Or growing intron burden may have increased the advantage of that separation.

Cause and consequence remain entangled.

What is clear by LECA is that the eukaryotic genome had become structurally different from prokaryotic genomes.

Linear chromosomes.

Chromatin.

Introns.

Splicing.

Complex regulation.

That complexity creates mutation.

It creates recombination opportunities.

And it creates a problem shared by every large genome:

how do you preserve useful combinations while still generating variation?

Eukaryotes evolved an extraordinary answer.

They mix genomes deliberately.

Sex is older than animals.

Older than plants.

Older than fungi.

It was probably already present in LECA.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Comparative genomics reconstructs LECA as intron-rich with a sophisticated spliceosome.
- Structural/chemical evidence strongly supports evolutionary relationships between group II self-splicing introns and spliceosomal introns.
- Mitochondrial delivery of group II introns is a leading hypothesis, not a uniquely demonstrated historical sequence.
- Modern regulatory/alternative-splicing benefits are not assumed to be the original selective cause of intron proliferation.

**Decision:** distinguish established LECA spliceosomal complexity from unresolved intron invasion and nucleus–spliceosome causality.
