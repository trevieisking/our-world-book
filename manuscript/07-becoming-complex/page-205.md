# CHAPTER SEVEN — BECOMING COMPLEX
## Page 205 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### THE BACTERIUM THAT STAYED

Mitochondria are often introduced as the “powerhouses of the cell.”

That is true in a narrow sense.

It misses the evolutionary point.

A mitochondrion is not merely a compartment built by a eukaryotic cell.

It is the descendant of a **bacterium** that entered an ancestral host and became permanently integrated.

This is endosymbiosis.

The evidence is overwhelming.

Mitochondria contain their own DNA.

Their genomes are greatly reduced today, but their genes and translation machinery reveal bacterial ancestry.

Mitochondrial ribosomes retain bacterial relationships.

Mitochondria divide by growth and fission rather than being manufactured from scratch by the cell.

They possess a double membrane.

Their inner membrane contains respiratory machinery with deep bacterial homology.

Phylogenetic analyses place mitochondrial ancestry within or very close to the **Alphaproteobacteria**, although the exact relationship to living alphaproteobacterial groups remains debated.

The ancestral bacterium was not one of today's mitochondria.

It was a free-living or facultatively associated microorganism with a complete genome of its own.

The host was archaeal.

At some point, the relationship crossed from ecological association into cellular integration.

How?

No fossil preserves the moment.

Several models remain viable.

One family begins with **syntrophy**.

An archaeal host consumes compounds produced by a bacterial partner, or the reverse.

Close metabolic dependence favours close physical contact.

Asgard biology makes such relationships plausible.

Another family gives phagocytosis or membrane engulfment a major role.

If the host could already surround particles or cells, a bacterium might survive ingestion rather than being digested.

But whether true phagocytosis existed before mitochondrial acquisition is disputed.

Still other models involve external membrane protrusions gradually wrapping a partner.

The geometry differs.

The evolutionary requirement is the same.

The bacterium must stop being merely a neighbour.

It must become inherited with the host lineage.

At first, the partnership may have been optional.

Then mutually beneficial.

Then irreversible.

The bacterial partner supplied metabolic functions.

The host supplied substrates and a protected chemical environment.

Genes began moving.

Control shifted.

Replication became coordinated.

Protein import systems evolved.

The host's descendants became dependent on the symbiont.

The symbiont's descendants lost functions that the host could provide.

Natural selection no longer acted on two completely independent organisms.

It acted increasingly on the combined system.

This is one of the most profound evolutionary transitions known.

Two lineages entered.

One cellular lineage emerged.

The exact metabolism that first stabilised the partnership is not settled.

Some models emphasise hydrogen exchange under anoxic conditions.

Others emphasise oxygen respiration.

The ancestral mitochondrion may have been metabolically flexible.

Modern mitochondria themselves are not uniformly aerobic.

Some eukaryotes use mitochondrial-related organelles in low-oxygen or anaerobic environments.

That diversity warns against assuming the first partnership required an oxygen-rich habitat.

The symbiosis also changed the meaning of heredity.

The new cell contained two genomes.

Host genes.

Endosymbiont genes.

Over time, most mitochondrial genes did not remain inside the organelle.

They moved.

And once they moved, the bacterial partner ceased to control its own future.

The next stage of endosymbiosis was not simply living together.

It was **genomic takeover**.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Mitochondrial bacterial ancestry is established by genome, ribosome, membrane, respiratory and phylogenetic evidence.
- Current phylogenomic work places the mitochondrial ancestor within or sister to known Alphaproteobacteria; exact placement remains debated.
- 2024 *Nature* review: multiple endosymbiotic scenarios remain, including syntrophic, engulfment and membrane-protrusion models.
- Oxygen-rich conditions are not assumed to be required for initial symbiosis; mitochondrial-related organelles retain diverse anaerobic metabolisms.

**Decision:** mitochondrial endosymbiosis is treated as established; the physical mechanism and initial metabolism of the partnership remain unresolved.
