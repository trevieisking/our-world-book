# CHAPTER SEVEN — BECOMING COMPLEX
## Page 222 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### LIFE UNDER — AND ON — THE ICE

Snowball Earth did not sterilise the planet.

We know that because eukaryotic lineages existing before the Cryogenian have descendants and fossil records after it.

The survival problem is physical.

Photosynthetic organisms need light.

Heterotrophs need food.

Metabolism needs liquid water.

A completely dark ocean sealed beneath thick ice for millions of years would make some ecosystems extremely difficult to sustain.

Possible refugia have therefore become a major part of Snowball research.

Thin tropical sea ice could transmit enough light for photosynthesis.

Cracks and **polynyas** could expose open water.

Geothermal heat could maintain local liquid environments.

Hydrothermal systems beneath ice would remain active.

Sea-ice brine channels contain liquid water even below the nominal freezing point.

Meltwater can collect on top of ice.

Continental margins and volcanic regions can create local thermal exceptions.

None of these refugia has been identified as *the* place Cryogenian eukaryotes survived.

But some can be tested with modern analogues.

In 2025, researchers studied supraglacial meltwater ponds on Antarctica's McMurdo Ice Shelf.

The ponds look unpromising.

They are cold.

Seasonal.

Surrounded by ice.

Yet molecular surveys and sterol analyses revealed diverse eukaryotic communities.

Microalgae.

Protists.

Small multicellular organisms.

Photosynthetically active microbial mats.

The researchers then experimentally altered the sterols into sterane products to ask what such communities might leave in an ancient biomarker record.

Their conclusion was deliberately framed as an analogue.

Supraglacial meltwater ponds are physically credible candidates for Cryogenian refugia.

They are not proof that Sturtian or Marinoan organisms actually lived in identical ponds.

The distinction matters.

Modern Antarctic organisms possess hundreds of millions of years of later adaptations.

The geometry and salinity of Cryogenian ice need not match McMurdo.

Atmospheric composition differed.

Continents occupied different positions.

But the experiment defeats one overly restrictive assumption:

**an ice-dominated world need not be biologically uniform or sterile.**

Small liquid habitats can sustain surprisingly diverse eukaryotic communities.

The aftermath is equally important.

A common story says Snowball Earth ended, weathering flooded the ocean with nutrients, oxygen rose, algae expanded and animals followed.

Every link has some physical plausibility.

The complete chain is not established.

In 2025, a multi-proxy study of Mongolian rocks examined the ocean immediately after the Sturtian deglaciation.

Instead of simple oxygenation, it found an episode of **deoxygenation**, followed by less reducing but still largely anoxic conditions.

The authors reconstructed nutrient and sulfate limitation and a large reservoir of difficult-to-degrade dissolved organic carbon.

Transient oxidation pulses occurred.

A persistently oxygen-rich ocean did not simply appear when the ice disappeared.

That result is a useful warning.

Deglaciation can increase weathering.

Weathering can deliver nutrients.

Nutrients can increase productivity.

Higher productivity can also consume oxygen when organic matter is respired.

The sign of the oxygen response depends on where carbon and nutrients go.

Climate forcing does not map one-to-one onto biology.

Eukaryotic ecosystems nevertheless changed strongly through the Cryogenian and Tonian–Ediacaran transition.

Algal biomarkers become more prominent.

Multicellular lineages diversify.

Food webs become increasingly eukaryote-rich.

Whether glaciation accelerated those trends, reset ecosystems, altered nutrient regimes, or simply coincided with deeper evolutionary changes remains debated.

The safest conclusion is powerful enough.

Complex eukaryotic life survived both Snowball episodes.

The ice reorganised habitats and biogeochemical cycles.

It did not create multicellularity from nothing.

And when the Marinoan ice finally retreated around 635 million years ago, it opened the Ediacaran Period.

The world that emerged from the freeze began preserving organisms unlike anything that had appeared before.

Not microscopic cells.

Bodies visible to the naked eye.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Husain et al. (2025), *Nature Communications*: modern McMurdo supraglacial meltwater ponds contain diverse eukaryotic communities and are plausible Cryogenian-refugia analogues; this is not direct evidence of the actual ancient refuge.
- Zhang et al. (2025), *Nature Communications*: post-Sturtian Taishir record indicates initial ocean deoxygenation followed by less reducing but still largely anoxic, nutrient- and sulfate-limited conditions.
- Cryogenian survival may have involved multiple refugia including thin ice, melt ponds, polynyas, brine channels, hydrothermal or marginal settings; no unique refuge is established.

**Decision:** Snowball Earth is linked to ecological and biogeochemical restructuring without asserting a simple deglaciation -> oxygenation -> animals causal chain.
