# CHAPTER SEVEN — BECOMING COMPLEX
## Page 215 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### WHY KEEP SEX?

Sex is expensive.

A clonal cell can copy a successful genome directly.

A sexual lineage mixes that genome with another.

It spends time finding a partner.

It risks combining good genes with bad ones.

In organisms with separate sexes, some individuals may contribute little direct offspring production.

Meiosis itself requires complex machinery.

Why did sex persist?

There is no single accepted answer.

Several evolutionary advantages can operate at the same time.

**DNA repair** is one.

A homologous chromosome provides a template for repairing damage.

Recombination machinery is deeply connected to double-strand-break repair.

Early eukaryotic genomes may have experienced substantial stress from reactive oxygen species, mobile elements and endosymbiotic gene transfer.

Pairing chromosomes could turn repair into heredity.

**Removing harmful mutations** is another.

In a purely clonal lineage, deleterious mutations can accumulate together.

Recombination can produce some descendants carrying fewer harmful mutations and allow selection to remove bad combinations more efficiently.

**Combining beneficial mutations** provides the opposite advantage.

If two lineages independently acquire useful changes, sex can place both changes in one descendant without waiting for each mutation to arise again in the same clone.

**Escaping parasites** can also matter.

Hosts and parasites continually adapt to one another.

Recombination creates rare new genetic combinations.

That can make offspring harder for rapidly evolving parasites to exploit.

Environmental change creates a similar advantage.

A clone well suited to yesterday may fail tomorrow.

Sex continually samples new combinations.

None of these explanations is universal.

The benefit depends on population size.

Mutation rate.

Ecology.

Ploidy.

Parasite pressure.

Recombination rate.

The cost of mating.

The same mechanism can be advantageous in one population and costly in another.

This is why evolutionary biology has not reduced sex to one purpose.

It may persist for different reasons in different lineages.

Its origin may also differ from the reasons it persists now.

A repair mechanism can later become a diversity-generating mechanism.

A rare stress response can become a regular life-cycle stage.

An accidental cell fusion can become regulated mating.

Sex also has risks unique to eukaryotes.

Mitochondria introduce another genome into the cell.

If organelles from both parents are inherited equally, competing mitochondrial genomes can spread selfishly inside descendants.

Many modern sexual systems therefore restrict organelle inheritance to one parent.

That rule is common but not universal.

Its repeated evolution shows that sex requires control not just of nuclear chromosomes but of cytoplasmic heredity.

The key evolutionary effect is simple.

Sex **decouples ancestry from whole genomes**.

A lineage no longer inherits one intact parental genome.

Chromosomes recombine.

Alleles acquire new neighbours.

Selection can act on combinations.

Large genomes become more evolutionarily flexible.

This does not mean sex is required for complexity.

Asexual eukaryotes exist.

Some remain successful for long periods.

Many organisms alternate sexual and asexual reproduction.

But sex gives eukaryotic evolution a powerful way to explore genetic space.

And once multicellular bodies evolve, sex can generate variation among individuals built from enormous numbers of genetically identical somatic cells.

Before we reach bodies, however, we should notice what Chapter Seven has already done.

The cell itself has become a community of systems.

Archaeal inheritance.

Bacterial endosymbiosis.

Internal membranes.

Cytoskeletal mechanics.

Linear chromosomes.

Introns.

Splicing.

Mitosis.

Meiosis.

None is complexity by size alone.

They are complexity by **coordination**.

That coordination makes a new evolutionary step possible.

Cells can begin cooperating not only transiently in mats or colonies, but as parts of one reproductive individual.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Major explanations for sex include homologous repair, purging deleterious mutations, combining beneficial mutations, parasite/environmental adaptation and interactions among these mechanisms.
- No single universal selective explanation for sex is accepted.
- Uniparental organelle inheritance is common and can reduce conflict among cytoplasmic genomes, but is not universal.
- Present-day benefits need not equal the original cause of meiosis/sex.

**Decision:** explain multiple evolutionary mechanisms rather than assigning sex one adaptive “purpose.”
