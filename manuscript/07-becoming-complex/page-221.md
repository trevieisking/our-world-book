# CHAPTER SEVEN — BECOMING COMPLEX
## Page 221 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### WHEN ICE REACHED THE TROPICS

About 717 million years ago, Earth entered one of the most extraordinary climate states preserved in the rock record.

Glaciers spread to low latitudes.

Ice-rafted debris appears in ancient marine sediments deposited close to the equator.

Glacial deposits occur on multiple continents.

Carbonate rocks that normally form in warm settings sit directly above glacial strata.

Iron formations reappear in association with some glacial successions.

The event is called the **Sturtian glaciation**.

It lasted extraordinarily long.

Current geochronology places it from roughly **717 to 659 million years ago**.

A nonglacial interval followed.

Then Earth froze again.

The **Marinoan glaciation** began around 645 million years ago in many current reconstructions and ended abruptly around **635 million years ago**.

Together, the two events define much of the Cryogenian Period.

They inspired the phrase **Snowball Earth**.

The strongest form of the hypothesis imagines the oceans almost completely sealed beneath thick ice, even at the equator.

That picture captures the severity of the climate.

It is not the only model compatible with the evidence.

Alternative states include thinner equatorial sea ice, open-water regions, seasonal melt zones, cracks, polynyas and partially ice-covered oceans.

Recent modelling and sedimentological arguments continue to debate how complete the freeze actually became.

What is much harder to dispute is the extraordinary geographic extent of glaciation.

Palaeomagnetic measurements place some glacial deposits at low latitude.

Dropstones and diamictites record moving ice.

Glacial deposits are found on continents that were widely separated.

The global carbon cycle underwent large disturbances.

And the ends of the glaciations are marked in many regions by unusual **cap carbonates** deposited rapidly above glacial sediments.

The Sturtian is especially striking because of its duration.

More than fifty million years of severe glacial conditions is not one long winter.

Continents move.

Volcanoes erupt.

Weathering slows.

The carbon cycle adjusts.

Life continues.

How can a frozen planet escape?

The classic Snowball mechanism uses carbon dioxide.

On a temperate Earth, silicate weathering removes CO₂ over geological time.

During widespread glaciation, much continental weathering is suppressed beneath ice.

Volcanoes continue releasing CO₂.

If atmospheric CO₂ accumulates faster than it is removed, greenhouse warming strengthens.

Eventually the climate crosses a threshold where tropical ice begins to melt.

Dark water and rock absorb more sunlight than bright ice.

The albedo feedback reverses.

Deglaciation can become rapid.

That broad mechanism remains influential.

The details are more complicated.

Subglacial weathering does not stop completely.

The ocean stores carbon.

Sea ice exchanges gases differently depending on thickness and fractures.

Volcanic CO₂ fluxes vary.

A 2024 model of the Marinoan aftermath showed that ocean carbon chemistry can produce a range of postglacial greenhouse trajectories rather than one inevitable prolonged supergreenhouse.

Another 2024 model showed how cap carbonates could form in stages from seafloor weathering, intense postglacial continental weathering and later ocean mixing.

In 2025, high-resolution chronology from South China constrained Marinoan deglaciation there to begin around 636 million years ago and cap-carbonate deposition to continue through a longer recovery interval.

The result reinforces a key lesson.

Snowball Earth was not just ice.

It was an Earth-system cycle involving ice, volcanism, weathering, ocean chemistry and the carbon cycle.

And throughout both glaciations, eukaryotic life survived.

That survival itself constrains the climate.

Whatever “Snowball” means physically, it cannot mean a planet with nowhere for the ancestors of later eukaryotes to persist.

The fossil and molecular record on both sides of the ice proves that life crossed the frozen interval.

The question is where.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Cryogenian Sturtian glaciation: ~717–659 Ma; Marinoan: approximately ~645–635 Ma in current syntheses.
- Low-latitude glacial deposits, cap carbonates and globally distributed Cryogenian glacial successions strongly support exceptionally severe glaciation.
- “Hard Snowball,” thin-ice, slushball and open-water/refugia variants remain debated; complete ocean sealing is not assumed.
- Thomas & Catling (2024), *Nature Communications*: three-stage cap-carbonate model.
- 2025 astrochronology constrains South China Marinoan deglaciation onset near ~636 Ma and cap-carbonate deposition into ~635 Ma.

**Decision:** treat globally extensive low-latitude glaciation as established while leaving ice thickness, open-water extent and detailed climate state as active questions.
