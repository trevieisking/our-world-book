# CHAPTER SEVEN — BECOMING COMPLEX
## Page 204 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### A MOSAIC GENOME WITH AN ASGARD SCAFFOLD

Eukaryotic genes do not tell one ancestral story.

Some group with archaeal genes.

Some group with bacterial genes.

Some have no clear prokaryotic counterpart.

Some were duplicated repeatedly during eukaryogenesis and then specialised.

Some moved horizontally between lineages later.

For years, a popular summary divided the genome neatly.

Archaea supplied the information systems:

DNA replication.

Transcription.

Translation.

Bacteria supplied much of metabolism.

The mitochondrial endosymbiont was often assumed to account for a large fraction of those bacterial genes.

The real genome is messier.

And in January 2026, a major *Nature* study argued for a substantial revision.

Victor Tobiasson and colleagues analysed deeply conserved eukaryotic gene families using expanded Asgard and bacterial genome datasets and explicit evolutionary hypothesis tests.

They reported that the strongest ancestry signal for **most core functional systems traced toward the last eukaryotic common ancestor** was Asgard archaeal.

Not only information processing.

Cytoskeletal systems.

Membrane-remodelling machinery.

Multiple metabolic and cellular pathways.

The alphaproteobacterial contribution remained unmistakable but was especially concentrated in functions associated with mitochondria:

electron transport.

Mitochondrial translation.

Iron–sulfur-cluster biology.

Other bacterial genes were distributed more diffusely and may reflect many horizontal transfers before and during eukaryogenesis rather than additional whole-cell symbioses.

The study therefore favours an image in which an already increasingly elaborate Asgard-derived host supplied much of the cellular scaffold.

Mitochondrial acquisition then added a smaller number of genes by count but an enormously important biological system.

This is an important new result.

It is not the final word.

Ancient gene ancestry is difficult to reconstruct.

Many Asgard genomes remain incomplete metagenome assemblies.

Bacterial genome sampling is uneven.

Horizontal gene transfer can obscure the original donor.

Fast evolutionary rates stretch branches.

Ancient gene duplication confuses one-to-one comparisons.

The authors themselves emphasise these caveats.

Other analyses can recover larger bacterial contributions depending on how ancestral gene families are defined and how later horizontal transfers are filtered.

So the chapter will not replace one oversimplified slogan with another.

Not:

“half archaeal, half bacterial.”

And not:

“the eukaryotic cell is basically Asgard plus one mitochondrion.”

A more accurate statement is:

**the eukaryotic cell has a strongly archaeal/Asgard cellular ancestry, a crucial alphaproteobacterial mitochondrial ancestry, and additional bacterial contributions accumulated through a complicated history of gene transfer.**

The mitochondrial contribution can be small in gene count and enormous in consequence.

A handful of functions can transform a cell if they create a new energy-generating organelle.

This also complicates the order of events.

If the Asgard host already possessed substantial cytoskeletal and membrane-remodelling complexity, mitochondria may have arrived relatively late.

If mitochondrial energy enabled the expansion of eukaryotic complexity, endosymbiosis may have occurred earlier and driven later elaboration.

Both families of models remain active.

The 2026 Asgard-dominance study favours a relatively late mitochondrial capture.

Other researchers disagree.

Fortunately, one point does not depend on which sequence wins.

By the time of the **last eukaryotic common ancestor**, mitochondria were already present.

Every living eukaryotic lineage either has mitochondria or contains highly modified descendants of them.

The cell that gave rise to all living eukaryotes had already completed the symbiosis.

So we now turn to the bacterial partner itself.

One bacterium crossed a biological boundary few cells ever cross.

It entered another cell.

It stayed.

And eventually, it ceased to be an independent organism.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Eukaryotic genomes have mixed archaeal/bacterial ancestry plus eukaryote-specific innovations and extensive duplication/HGT.
- Tobiasson et al. (2026), *Nature*: dominant Asgard association across most analysed conserved eukaryotic functional systems; alphaproteobacterial signal concentrated largely in mitochondrial functions.
- Study favours an Asgard-complexity-first / relatively mitochondria-late scenario but carries genome-sampling and phylogenetic caveats.
- LECA already possessed mitochondria; no primary living eukaryote lineage is known to have ancestrally lacked mitochondria.

**Decision:** include the 2026 Asgard-dominance result as a major current finding while retaining competing models and methodological caveats.
