### WHERE DID THE NUCLEUS COME FROM?

The nucleus gives eukaryotes their name.

A membrane-bound compartment surrounds the chromosomes.

Nuclear pores control traffic between the genome and cytoplasm.

RNA is transcribed inside.

Many RNAs are processed before they are exported.

Translation happens outside on ribosomes.

This physical separation is fundamental to modern eukaryotic gene regulation.

Where did it come from?

We do not know.

The answer is more complicated than “the cell wrapped membrane around its DNA.”

The **nuclear envelope** consists of two membranes and is continuous with the endoplasmic reticulum.

Its membranes use the bacterial-type lipid chemistry characteristic of modern eukaryotes.

The nuclear pore complex is an enormous assembly of proteins with repeated structural motifs whose deep ancestry is difficult to reconstruct.

The proteins handling DNA replication, transcription and much RNA processing show strong archaeal ancestry.

So the nucleus combines:

an archaeal information system,

a eukaryotic membrane architecture,

and molecular machinery extensively remodelled during eukaryogenesis.

Several origin models have been proposed.

In **inside-out** models, protrusions from an archaeal cell expand around external symbionts and eventually fuse, leaving the original cell body as the nuclear region and creating the cytoplasm around it.

In other models, internal membrane invaginations surround the genome.

Some hypotheses connect nuclear origins to the spread of introns and the need to separate slow RNA processing from rapid translation.

Others emphasise membrane trafficking and cytoskeletal reorganisation.

The models solve different problems.

None has been directly observed historically.

A major 2026 phylogenomic study strengthens one side of the puzzle.

It finds that the core information-processing systems that now operate in the nucleus derive overwhelmingly from Asgard archaeal ancestry.

Yet the same study explicitly states that the **origin of the nucleus as a membrane-bounded compartment remains unresolved**.

The membrane problem is especially important.

Archaeal membranes and bacterial/eukaryotic membranes are chemically different.

Modern eukaryotes use ester-linked bacterial-type phospholipids.

Most archaea use ether-linked isoprenoid membranes.

If the host was archaeal, eukaryogenesis involved a profound transition in membrane composition.

Mixed archaeal–bacterial membranes are chemically possible.

Researchers have engineered cells containing both lipid types.

But possible is not historical proof.

The order remains uncertain.

Did membrane replacement begin before mitochondrial endosymbiosis?

Did bacterial genes arriving from the mitochondrial partner accelerate it?

Did internal compartments emerge during the transition?

The nucleus cannot be reconstructed independently from those questions.

What comparative biology does establish is the endpoint.

LECA already possessed a nucleus.

A nuclear pore system.

Complex chromatin.

RNA-processing machinery.

And an endomembrane system physically connected to the nuclear envelope.

This means nuclear architecture was not a late embellishment added after the major eukaryotic lineages had split.

It arose during the dark FECA-to-LECA interval.

The nucleus therefore illustrates a recurring theme.

Evolution does not invent every part from nothing.

Archaeal systems are retained.

Old proteins are duplicated.

Membranes are repurposed.

New structures emerge from interactions among systems with different ancestries.

The organelle looks like one invention.

Its components preserve a coalition of histories.

To understand how a cell could build and reshape such compartments, we need the machinery that physically moves membranes.

The cytoskeleton.

## Research / fact-check notes
- LECA is reconstructed with a nucleus, nuclear pores and extensive RNA-processing/chromatin machinery.
- Tobiasson et al. (2026), *Nature*: nuclear information-processing systems have strong Asgard ancestry; membrane-bounded nuclear origin remains unresolved.
- Nuclear-envelope continuity with the ER and bacterial-type eukaryotic membranes tie nuclear-origin models to the unresolved archaeal-to-eukaryotic membrane transition.
- Inside-out, invagination and intron-linked models remain hypotheses.

**Decision:** the nucleus is established in LECA; its membrane-bounded origin mechanism and event ordering remain unresolved.
