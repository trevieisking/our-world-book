# CHAPTER SEVEN — BECOMING COMPLEX
## Page 223 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### THE EDIACARAN EXPERIMENT

The Marinoan ice retreated around 635 million years ago.

The period that followed lasts to about **538.8 million years ago**.

We call it the **Ediacaran**.

For tens of millions of years after deglaciation, the fossil record contains diverse microscopic eukaryotes, algae and unusual multicellular forms.

Then, by about **574 million years ago**, macroscopic communities become dramatically more visible.

The classic sites lie in Newfoundland.

Soft-bodied organisms are preserved across ancient deep-marine surfaces.

Many are frond-shaped.

Some grow in repeated, self-similar branching patterns.

These are the **rangeomorphs**.

They look unlike modern animals.

No mouths are obvious.

No guts.

No limbs.

No shells.

Their bodies consist of branching units repeated at several scales.

For decades, their biological affinity was one of palaeontology's hardest problems.

Were they giant protists?

Fungi?

Lichens?

An extinct kingdom with no living counterpart?

Or animals on deep branches outside modern animal groups?

Recent developmental and ecological work increasingly supports an **animal** interpretation for many rangeomorphs.

A 2025 study reconstructed growth in *Fractofusus* and related forms.

The repeated body architecture followed regulated developmental rules.

The authors interpreted rangeomorphs as stem-group eumetazoans — animals lying outside the living crown groups but within the wider animal lineage.

That interpretation is influential.

Some details of rangeomorph relationships and functional biology remain debated.

The fossils should not be drawn as feather-like corals merely because the shapes look familiar.

By 2026, spatial analyses of the earliest widespread Ediacaran communities indicate another surprising feature.

Competition appears weak.

The Avalon assemblages from roughly **574–560 million years ago** contain sessile organisms distributed across the seafloor in patterns more consistent with limited resource competition than with the crowded competitive communities familiar from modern reefs.

Their reproductive strategies and dispersal helped shape community structure.

The ecological world was complex.

It was not yet modern.

Later Ediacaran assemblages become more diverse.

Body plans change.

Evidence for mobility becomes stronger in several forms.

Trace fossils show animals moving through or across sediment.

**Kimberella**, around 555 million years old, has been interpreted as a bilaterian-grade animal that scraped microbial surfaces.

**Dickinsonia**, known from rocks around 558 million years old and younger, has produced strong animal evidence from body form, growth and preserved lipid chemistry, although details of its exact position within animals remain debated.

Not every large Ediacaran fossil is an animal.

That lesson matters.

**Beltanelliformis**, a circular macrofossil once compared with animals, fungi and algae, preserves molecular evidence supporting a cyanobacterial interpretation.

Large size is not animal identity.

Body shape is not enough.

The Ediacaran record therefore resembles the earliest-life record in one surprising way.

Confidence comes from convergence.

Morphology.

Growth.

Movement.

Development.

Biomarkers.

Trace fossils.

Community ecology.

Sedimentary context.

The first animal ecosystems are not one fossil suddenly appearing.

They are a pattern becoming harder and harder to explain without animals.

And they appear before the Cambrian.

That fact changes the meaning of the phrase **Cambrian Explosion**.

Animals did not materialise from nothing at the beginning of the Cambrian Period.

Their evolutionary roots already occupied Ediacaran seafloors.

The Cambrian transition is about what happens when those animals begin transforming ecology far more aggressively.

Before we reach that threshold, one final distinction matters.

The Ediacaran biota are not a failed rehearsal for “real” animals.

They were real ecosystems with their own evolutionary history.

Some lineages probably continue across the boundary.

Others disappear.

Some body plans have no known descendants.

The world about to enter the Cambrian carries both inheritance and loss.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Ediacaran Period: 635–538.8 Ma in current chronostratigraphy.
- Earliest widespread macroscopic animal communities occur in Avalonia around ~574–560 Ma.
- Dunn et al. (2025), *Nature Communications*: *Fractofusus* morphogenesis supports rangeomorph interpretation as early total-group/stem eumetazoans, while internal relationships remain contentious.
- Mitchell & Manica (2026), *Nature Ecology & Evolution*: early Avalonian animal communities show weak resource competition and a later diversification “second wave.”
- Dickinsonia/Kimberella are strong later-Ediacaran animal candidates; exact phylogenetic positions are less certain.
- Beltanelliformis molecular-fossil work supports cyanobacterial rather than animal affinity.

**Decision:** the chapter recognises increasingly strong animal interpretations while preserving taxon-specific uncertainty and avoiding “all Ediacaran macrofossils = animals.”
