# CHAPTER SEVEN — BECOMING COMPLEX
## Page 211 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### COULD THE HOST SWALLOW THE MITOCHONDRION?

Modern eukaryotic cells can perform a remarkable act.

They can wrap membrane around something outside the cell and bring it inside.

When the target is a large particle or another cell, the process is called **phagocytosis**.

Actin pushes and pulls the membrane.

Receptors attach to the target.

The membrane closes.

An internal compartment forms around the captured material.

For decades, one mitochondrial-origin model seemed almost obvious:

first evolve phagocytosis,

then accidentally engulf the bacterial ancestor of mitochondria,

then fail to digest it.

The logic is neat.

The evidence is not decisive.

No living Asgard archaeon is known to perform true eukaryotic phagocytosis.

The cultured *Prometheoarchaeum* is small and produces protrusions, but its biology does not demonstrate cell engulfment.

Genomic predictions likewise have not turned Asgard archaea into hidden phagocytes.

That favours models in which internalisation occurs without modern phagocytosis.

Entangling protrusions.

Progressive membrane wrapping.

Long-term syntrophy.

Membrane fusion.

Each offers a route from external partnership to endosymbiosis.

But recent gene-dating work shifts the balance again.

The 2025 *Nature* study of pre-LECA gene duplications reconstructed much of the machinery associated with membrane trafficking, endocytosis and phagocytosis as already developing before or around mitochondrial acquisition.

Its preferred model contains a **complex host capable of endocytosis**.

Crucially, the authors could not resolve whether full phagocytosis came immediately before or just after the mitochondrial event.

The relevant systems overlap.

The endolysosomal machinery appears close in timing to the earliest alphaproteobacterial gene signal.

This prevents an easy verdict.

A host does not need the complete phagocytic machinery of a modern amoeba to internalise a bacterial partner.

Partial engulfment capability may be enough.

A protrusion-based system can evolve into deeper membrane enclosure.

Endocytosis and endosymbiosis may reinforce one another.

The bacterium itself can manipulate host membranes.

Modern intracellular bacteria demonstrate that microbes can enter or remodel host cells through many mechanisms.

So asking whether “phagocytosis existed first” may impose a modern category on a transitional cell.

The deeper question is physical.

Could the host deform its membrane around large external material?

Could it keep an internalised bacterium alive?

Could membrane trafficking prevent the partner from simply being digested or expelled?

Could the combined cell divide without losing the symbiont?

Current evidence increasingly supports an archaeal host that was more mechanically elaborate than a typical textbook prokaryote.

It does not establish one engulfment mechanism.

This matters because phagocytosis itself would transform ecology.

A cell able to engulf other cells gains access to concentrated packets of nutrients.

Predation becomes possible.

Food webs change.

Cell size can increase.

Internal digestive compartments become useful.

A cytoskeleton becomes even more valuable.

If phagocytosis arose during eukaryogenesis, complex cellular architecture immediately became an ecological innovation as well as an internal one.

The missing FECA-to-LECA line may therefore contain the beginnings of microbial predation.

But swallowing another cell is only useful if a large cell can control its own chromosomes reliably.

As genomes expand and chromosomes multiply, cell division becomes an engineering problem.

The next major eukaryotic system is the machinery of mitosis.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Modern phagocytosis is actin/endomembrane dependent; no cultured Asgard archaeon is demonstrated to perform true eukaryotic phagocytosis.
- 2024 *Nature* review retains multiple uptake models and notes unresolved phagocytosis–mitochondria ordering.
- Kay et al. (2025), *Nature*: dated duplications support a complex host with endocytic/phagocytosis-related machinery before or around mitochondrial acquisition but do not resolve exact order.
- Endosymbiotic internalisation need not require a modern amoeba-like phagocytic apparatus.

**Decision:** phagocytosis is treated as a developing capability around eukaryogenesis, not a proven prerequisite or consequence of mitochondrial capture.
