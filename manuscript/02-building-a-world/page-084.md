### THE PLANET STOPS BEING ONLY A CONSTRUCTION SITE

By the end of the Hadean, Earth had endured almost everything required to destroy a world.

Accretion.

Global or near-global melting.

Core formation.

The Moon-forming collision.

Atmospheric loss.

Volcanic outgassing.

Repeated impacts.

Crustal recycling.

And yet the planet kept cooling.

Water condensed again after heating events.

Crust formed again after melting.

Atmosphere returned after loss.

Weathering began.

Rain interacted with exposed rock.

The surface became a system rather than merely debris.

This does not mean the violence ended at the Hadean–Archean boundary 4.0 billion years ago.

Nature does not obey chapter headings.

Large impacts continued.

Volcanism remained intense.

The atmosphere was still unlike our own.

The continents were not modern continents.

Plate tectonics may not yet have operated in exactly its present form.

But something fundamental had changed.

Earth had become a **water-bearing rocky planet with long-lived surface environments**.

The 2026 synthesis of the early atmosphere and hydrosphere allows a hydrosphere as early as roughly 4.4 billion years ago, probably interrupted by impact-driven vaporisation.

By about 3.7 billion years ago, the rock record itself gives robust direct evidence of subaqueous environments.

At that point we no longer have to infer every ocean from a mineral grain.

We begin to encounter rocks that formed in water.

That is where our investigation now goes.

We have explained how the Universe made the elements.

How a star and disk assembled them.

How gravity built Earth.

How heat separated its interior.

How a collision produced the Moon.

How cooling, outgassing and volatile delivery produced the ingredients of a hydrosphere.

Now we can study the hydrosphere itself.

Not simply **where the water came from**.

But what a planet covered in water actually does.

Oceans alter climate.

They weather rock.

They move heat.

They dissolve gases.

They carry minerals.

They reshape crust.

They create chemical gradients.

And, eventually, they become the setting for the most difficult transition in this entire history.

Chemistry becomes biology.

But before we can ask how life began, we need to understand the world in which that chemistry occurred.
