### WHEN STEAM BECAME RAIN

At some point, Earth cooled enough for water vapour to condense.

That transition may have been one of the most important in planetary history.

A steam-rich atmosphere over molten rock can keep a planet hot. Once the surface cools sufficiently, liquid water becomes stable. Water vapour condenses. Clouds form. Rain can fall. Surface reservoirs begin to accumulate.

Carbon dioxide can dissolve into water.

Water reacts with rock.

Chemical weathering begins to couple atmosphere, crust and hydrosphere.

But the first hydrosphere may not have been permanent.

Large impacts can heat the surface and vaporise substantial amounts of water. The 2026 synthesis of Earth's early atmosphere and hydrosphere concludes that an initial hydrosphere could have developed as early as about **4.4 billion years ago**, while later accretion probably caused repeated episodes of vaporisation.

So the oceans may not have "appeared" once.

Early Earth could have moved through cycles of cooling, condensation, impact heating, vaporisation and renewed condensation.

How early could liquid water really have existed?

The best evidence does not come from an ancient beach.

The oldest surviving rocks are too young to show us a 4.4-billion-year-old shoreline.

Instead, the evidence comes from minerals whose chemistry indicates that liquid water had already interacted with crustal material.

That does not prove a global blue ocean.

It does establish that the old image of an uninterrupted Hadean fireball is too simple.

The young Earth could be violent and wet at the same time.
