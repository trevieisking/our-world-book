# CHAPTER TWO — BUILDING A WORLD
## Page 083 — WHAT SURVIVED?

If Hadean impacts were so destructive, why do we have any evidence from the Hadean at all?

Because destruction was incomplete.

Zircons survived.

Some are older than 4.3 billion years.

Their uranium–lead systems still preserve ancient crystallisation ages.

Their oxygen isotopes still preserve information about vanished source rocks and water–rock interaction.

That survival matters.

It tells us that the early Earth was not thermally reset everywhere, every time.

Impacts could melt or metamorphose crust locally and regionally.

Some giant events affected vast areas.

But the continued survival of ancient zircons means pieces of older crustal history escaped complete reprocessing.

Researchers have even searched individual Hadean zircon grains for thermal signatures that might record giant impacts.

The results are difficult to interpret, because a zircon can be heated, partially reset, recrystallised or survive unchanged depending on its location and thermal history.

So zircons are not a simple impact counter.

They are survivors carrying selective memories.

The direct terrestrial impact record becomes clearer later.

By about **3.47 billion years ago**, sedimentary rocks preserve impact-spherule layers.

These tiny rounded particles formed when enormous impacts melted or vaporised rock, lofted material into the atmosphere, and allowed droplets or condensates to fall back to Earth.

Their parent craters are gone.

The ejecta survived.

This is one of geology's recurring tricks.

The event disappears.

A side effect remains.

For still older Hadean time, the archive is thinner.

We therefore reconstruct bombardment by combining lunar craters, lunar sample ages, meteorites, dynamical models and surviving terrestrial minerals.

Different evidence.

Different limitations.

No single source gets to overrule all the others.

That is exactly why uncertainty over the late bombardment remains scientifically legitimate.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Abbott et al. (2012), *PNAS*, Hadean zircon thermal profiles.
- Simonson & Glass (2004), *Annual Review of Earth and Planetary Sciences*, impact spherule layers.
- Koeberl, Schulz & Huber (2024), Precambrian impact review.

**Decision:** zircon survival constrains thermal history but does not provide a complete impact chronology; the oldest widely recognised direct terrestrial impact-spherule record is about 3.47 Ga.
