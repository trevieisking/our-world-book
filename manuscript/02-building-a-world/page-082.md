# CHAPTER TWO — BUILDING A WORLD

### WHAT COULD AN IMPACT ACTUALLY DO?

The phrase **giant impact** hides an enormous range of events.

A crater-forming collision can melt rock near the impact site.

A larger one can launch molten and vaporised material high above the atmosphere.

Ejecta returning to Earth can heat the surface over vast regions.

Ocean water can be boiled or vaporised.

Atmosphere can be eroded.

New gases can also be added by the impactor or released from newly melted rock.

And impact-heated crust can remain hydrothermally active for long periods afterward.

So were the Hadean impacts capable of repeatedly turning the entire planet sterile?

The evidence does not support such a simple conclusion.

Thermal modelling of heavy bombardment shows severe disruption without requiring the whole crust to remain molten at once.

A 2013 review estimated that a representative late-bombardment history could melt roughly **1.5–2.5 percent of the upper 20 kilometres of crust in total**, while only a smaller fraction would be molten at any one time.

That is geologically enormous.

It is not a global magma ocean.

A 2009 study asked an even sharper question: could heavy bombardment eliminate a hypothetical microbial biosphere?

Its simulations found that even intense bombardment would not necessarily sterilise the entire planet.

Surface environments could be devastated.

Shallow oceans could be heated dramatically.

But subsurface habitats — pores, fractures, deeper crust and hydrothermal systems — could remain or reappear.

This distinction becomes crucial later when we ask when life began.

If life already existed during part of the bombardment, an impact might destroy ecosystems without erasing the biosphere.

If life had not yet begun, the same event might create new chemical gradients, hot-water systems and reactive mineral surfaces.

Impacts are therefore not simply villains or benefactors.

They are energetic geological events.

Their biological consequences depend on size, location, environment and timing.

A kilometre-scale object and a Moon-forming collision belong in the same family only in the way that a match and a forest fire both belong under the word **burning**.

Scale changes everything.
