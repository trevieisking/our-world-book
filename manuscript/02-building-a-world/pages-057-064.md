## PAGE 057 — EARTH DID NOT APPEAR ALL AT ONCE

By the end of Chapter One, the Solar System had become a construction site.

Dust had become pebbles.

Pebbles had become planetesimals.

Planetesimals had become planetary embryos.

Now one of those growing bodies begins to deserve a new name:

**proto-Earth.**

That name can mislead us if we imagine a small but otherwise finished Earth waiting patiently to become larger.

It was not.

The body changed as it grew.

Every collision altered its mass, temperature, chemistry and rotation.

Some impacts added material.

Some removed it.

Some mixed metal and rock.

Some arrived from regions of the Solar System with different chemical compositions.

Some were large enough to melt enormous volumes of the growing planet.

Accretion was therefore not simply "more rocks landing on Earth."

It was the process that made Earth into a planet.

The planet's gravity became stronger as its mass increased. That let it attract and retain more material. Growing bodies disturbed one another's orbits, increasing collision speeds and scattering material across the inner Solar System.

The physics became increasingly violent.

A dust grain arriving at a centimetre per second can stick.

A planetary embryo arriving at kilometres per second can excavate a hemisphere.

The same word — **collision** — covers a spectacular range of consequences.

Computer simulations of terrestrial-planet formation produce repeated impacts among large embryos over tens of millions of years. The exact sequence cannot be reconstructed uniquely because chaotic orbital dynamics erase much of the detailed history.

We can recover the broad process.

We cannot write a trustworthy diary saying:

"At 09:14 on cosmic Tuesday, object number 7 struck proto-Earth from the southwest."

Nature did not preserve that level of paperwork.

But it preserved enough to establish that Earth's growth was prolonged, energetic and chemically transformative.

Meteorites tell us what early Solar-System materials were like.

Isotope systems record when certain reservoirs separated.

Planet-formation models show how embryos interact gravitationally.

And the present Earth retains the result: a planet whose interior is layered rather than chemically uniform.

That layering is our next clue.

Because if Earth had simply remained a cold pile of mixed rock and metal, it would not have an iron-rich core beneath a silicate mantle.

Something had to sort the planet from the inside.

First, it had to get very hot.

## PAGE 058 — BUILDING A PLANET GENERATES HEAT

A growing planet does not need a furnace installed inside it.

Gravity provides one.

Imagine dropping a stone.

As it falls, gravitational potential energy becomes motion.

Now scale that idea from a stone to planetary embryos.

Material falling into a growing proto-Earth can arrive at tremendous speed. When that motion is stopped in a collision, kinetic energy is converted into heat, deformation, melting, excavation and shock.

The larger Earth became, the more gravitational energy was available during accretion.

There were other heat sources too.

Compression raised temperatures in the interior.

The separation of dense metal toward the centre released additional gravitational energy.

Radioactive isotopes contributed heat.

In the earliest planetesimals, the short-lived isotope **aluminium-26** was especially important. It decayed quickly enough, and released enough energy, to melt many small bodies that formed early. This is one reason some asteroids differentiated into metallic cores and rocky mantles very early in Solar-System history.

By the time Earth grew large, the dominant heating story involved a combination of accretion, large impacts, metal segregation and radioactive decay rather than one single source.

That distinction matters because diagrams often show a convenient arrow labelled:

**HEAT**

as though early Earth had one thermostat.

It did not.

It had a budget.

And the entries in that budget changed with time.

An impact could heat part of the planet almost instantly.

Radioactive decay operated continuously.

Core formation converted gravitational potential energy into heat while metal sank.

Surface radiation attempted to cool the planet at the same time.

The result was competition between heating and heat loss.

During energetic stages of accretion, heating could win dramatically.

Large portions of the silicate Earth melted.

Some models produce a nearly or fully molten mantle after giant impacts.

That state is called a **magma ocean**.

The phrase sounds pleasantly tropical.

It was not.

## PAGE 059 — THE MAGMA OCEAN

A magma ocean is exactly what the name suggests in one sense and dangerously misleading in another.

It is a large region of molten silicate.

It is not an ocean of water.

And it may not have been one uninterrupted, perfectly global layer persisting unchanged through all of Earth's accretion.

Modern models strongly support extensive melting during terrestrial-planet growth, especially after major impacts and during metal segregation. The depth, lifetime and repeated nature of those molten states remain active research problems.

A giant impact can deliver enough energy to melt a substantial fraction of a planet.

If the surface and mantle become extensively molten, dense and light components can move much more efficiently than they can through solid rock.

This matters for differentiation.

It also matters for atmosphere formation, because volatile compounds dissolved in magma can be released as pressure and temperature change.

But those later consequences belong to later pages.

For now, picture a young planet whose outer silicate shell is glowing hot enough to behave as liquid rock.

Convection would be vigorous.

Hot material would rise.

Cooler material would sink.

Crystals would begin to form as the magma ocean lost heat.

The order in which minerals crystallised would depend on pressure, temperature and composition.

And that crystallisation did not necessarily proceed in one simple direction.

A 2025 Nature study showed that, under some physically plausible models, crystallisation of a deep terrestrial magma ocean naturally produces dense late-stage melts that can accumulate above the core, creating a long-lived **basal magma ocean**.

That is an active modern result, not something this book should quietly promote to universal certainty.

The early mantle's exact crystallisation history is still being worked out.

What is much more secure is the broader conclusion:

**early Earth experienced substantial melting, and that melting helped its interior separate into distinct chemical reservoirs.**

The planet was becoming layered.

Metal behaved differently from silicate.

Some elements followed the metal.

Others stayed in rock.

Gravity did the sorting.

## PAGE 060 — METAL SINKS, ROCK FLOATS

Iron is heavy.

That alone does not explain Earth's core.

The crucial point is that molten metal and molten silicate do not mix indefinitely as one uniform liquid.

Under the right pressures and temperatures, they separate into distinct phases.

Dense iron-rich metal tends to sink.

Silicate material remains above it.

This process is called **planetary differentiation**.

The same basic phenomenon happened in many smaller bodies before Earth had finished forming.

NASA's meteorite collections include iron meteorites interpreted as fragments of metallic asteroid cores. Those parent asteroids melted early, separated internally, and were later shattered by collisions.

An iron meteorite is therefore not just a strange space rock.

It is a sample from the inside of a small differentiated world.

Earth's differentiation occurred on a vastly larger scale.

As metallic liquid moved downward, it carried with it elements that prefer metal.

Geochemists call these **siderophile** elements — literally "iron-loving."

Other elements prefer silicate phases and are described as **lithophile**, or rock-loving.

This chemical preference is one of our strongest clues that core formation really happened.

If Earth's bulk material began with roughly chondritic proportions of many elements, but the mantle is now strongly depleted in certain metal-loving elements, a reasonable place to look for the missing inventory is the metallic core.

We cannot drill to the core and check a shelf labelled "missing tungsten".

But geochemistry lets us infer the separation.

The core is mostly iron, with nickel and smaller amounts of lighter elements.

The mantle above it is dominated by silicate minerals.

That division was produced during Earth's early differentiation.

The exact physical route taken by metal is more complicated.

Molten metal may have moved through a partially molten mantle.

Large impactors may have delivered already differentiated metallic cores that plunged downward.

Some metal may have emulsified into droplets and chemically equilibrated with surrounding silicate before continuing deeper.

Some may have descended more directly.

Different models predict different degrees of chemical mixing.

That uncertainty becomes important when we try to use isotope clocks to time core formation.

Because the clock only tells the truth if we understand what was being separated.

## PAGE 061 — AN ISOTOPE CLOCK FOR A VANISHING ELEMENT

One of the most elegant clocks in planetary science uses **hafnium** and **tungsten**.

The radioactive isotope hafnium-182 decays into tungsten-182.

In the early Solar System, hafnium-182 was present in measurable abundance.

Today it is extinct because its half-life is short compared with the age of the Solar System.

But its daughter isotope remains.

Now add chemistry.

Hafnium is strongly lithophile.

It prefers silicate.

Tungsten is much more siderophile under core-forming conditions.

It prefers metal.

So when metal separated from silicate during core formation, tungsten was preferentially removed into the metallic phase while hafnium remained in the silicate mantle.

Any hafnium-182 left behind could then decay into tungsten-182.

That creates an isotopic memory of when metal and silicate separated.

This is the **hafnium–tungsten chronometer**.

The basic principle is beautifully simple.

The interpretation is not.

Early studies produced different estimates for how quickly Earth's core formed. Later work showed why the answer depends on assumptions about how rapidly Earth accreted and how thoroughly incoming metal equilibrated with the mantle.

If an impactor's metal core plunges into Earth without fully mixing chemically with surrounding silicate, the isotope record differs from a model in which every drop of metal equilibrates perfectly.

Modern work therefore treats core formation as a process coupled to accretion, not one instantaneous date.

The main phase happened early in Solar-System history.

But there is no single universally agreed timestamp at which somebody could have written:

**CORE COMPLETE — 4.5 billion years ago.**

This is a recurring lesson in deep history.

Nature often gives us clocks for processes rather than birthdays for objects.

And the clocks become more powerful when independent evidence agrees.

Meteorites show early differentiation in small bodies.

Hafnium–tungsten systematics constrain metal–silicate separation.

Siderophile-element patterns constrain how metal and mantle interacted.

High-pressure experiments show how those elements partition at core-forming conditions.

And seismology tells us that a dense core exists today.

Several different languages of evidence converge on the same broad story.

Earth differentiated early.

But even after the metallic core existed, the interior did not yet look exactly like today's Earth.

## PAGE 062 — THE CORE THEN IS NOT THE CORE NOW

Earth's core today has two major parts.

A **liquid outer core** surrounds a **solid inner core**.

That does not mean the solid inner core formed when the planet first differentiated.

The original separation of metallic core from silicate mantle happened very early.

The solid inner core crystallised much later as Earth cooled.

The exact age of inner-core nucleation remains debated.

This distinction matters because diagrams can make modern Earth look timeless.

It is not.

The interior evolved.

How do we know the outer core is liquid?

Earthquakes.

Earthquake waves travel through the planet and change speed and direction depending on the material they cross.

Compressional **P waves** can travel through solids and liquids.

Shear **S waves** require a material that can support shear stress and therefore do not propagate through liquids.

Seismologists found that S waves do not pass through Earth's outer core.

P waves do, but their velocities and paths change strongly at the core boundary.

The pattern revealed a liquid outer core.

The inner core was identified later from subtler seismic behaviour indicating a solid region at Earth's centre.

This is a remarkable fact.

Nobody has visited the core.

The deepest boreholes barely scratch Earth's crust.

Yet earthquakes let us map boundaries thousands of kilometres below our feet.

The planet rings, and we listen.

Seismology also tells us something about composition.

Earth's core is too dense to be made of ordinary mantle rock.

But it is also less dense than pure iron would be under the relevant pressures and temperatures.

That density deficit implies that lighter elements are mixed into the predominantly iron-nickel core.

Candidates include sulfur, silicon, oxygen, carbon and hydrogen.

The exact proportions remain uncertain.

So even the object beneath our feet that contains roughly a third of Earth's mass still has a recipe we have not fully solved.

We know its broad structure extremely well.

We are still refining the ingredients.

## PAGE 063 — HOW DO YOU TEST A PLACE YOU CANNOT REACH?

If scientists cannot collect a bucket of outer-core liquid, how can they test theories about core formation?

They recreate pieces of the problem.

In laboratories, researchers place tiny samples of metal and silicate under enormous pressures.

Diamond-anvil cells compress material between the tips of gem-quality diamonds.

Lasers can heat those microscopic samples to thousands of degrees.

Shock experiments drive pressure and temperature upward over extremely short times.

Large presses reproduce conditions deeper in the mantle.

X-rays probe crystal structures and melting behaviour.

Computer calculations test how atoms should behave under conditions impossible to maintain in ordinary laboratory glassware.

No single experiment reproduces an entire planet.

That is not the goal.

The goal is to measure specific physical properties:

How dense is molten iron alloy at a given pressure?

Which elements prefer metal over silicate?

How does sulfur alter melting?

How does oxygen partition?

At what temperature does a mantle mineral melt?

How fast might metal droplets sink through silicate melt?

Those measurements can then be compared with seismology, meteorites and geochemistry.

A model of Earth's core has to satisfy several constraints at once.

It has to produce the correct density.

It has to agree with seismic-wave speeds.

It has to be chemically compatible with what is left in the mantle.

It has to make sense under high-pressure physics.

And it has to fit the isotope record of early differentiation.

This is how deep-Earth science avoids becoming informed guesswork.

A theory that fits one clue but breaks four others is not a successful reconstruction.

The method is similar to the rest of this book:

**use different witnesses whose weaknesses are different.**

Meteorites cannot tell us Earth's present seismic structure.

Earthquakes cannot tell us the isotopic composition of a meteorite.

A high-pressure experiment cannot tell us which impactor struck Earth.

But together they restrict the possibilities much more tightly than any one method could.

The inaccessible Earth is surprisingly testable.

## PAGE 064 — A PLANET, BUT NOT YET OUR PLANET

By now, the proto-Earth is recognisably planetary.

It has accumulated most of its eventual mass.

It has been heated repeatedly.

Large regions have melted.

Dense metallic material has segregated toward the centre.

A silicate mantle surrounds the core.

The planet is chemically differentiated.

But it is not the Earth we would recognise.

The surface is unstable.

The atmosphere is evolving.

Large impacts are not finished.

The Moon does not yet exist in its present form.

Water has not yet settled into the long-lived oceans that will dominate later chapters.

And the final stages of accretion may still change the planet profoundly.

One collision in particular becomes central to the next part of the story.

The leading explanation for the Moon's origin involves a giant impact between the young Earth and another large planetary body.

That hypothesis is strongly supported in broad form.

Its details are not settled.

How massive was the impactor?

How fast did it arrive?

At what angle?

How thoroughly did the two bodies mix?

Was there one dominant collision or a more complicated sequence?

How did the debris become the Moon while preserving the chemical similarities and differences measured in lunar samples?

Those questions have generated decades of models and experiments.

We will not answer them by choosing the prettiest animation.

We will follow the evidence.

Lunar rocks.

Isotopes.

Angular momentum.

Computer simulations.

Meteorite chronology.

The chemistry of Earth and Moon.

For the moment, the important achievement is already enormous.

The Universe began with almost no elements capable of making rock.

Stars manufactured them.

The Galaxy recycled them.

A disk collected them.

Gravity assembled them.

Heat melted them.

Chemistry separated them.

And a layered rocky planet emerged.

Earth now exists.

Next, something hits it hard enough to remake the world.

- Earth accretion is described as a prolonged, collision-dominated process rather than one formation event.
- Heating is explicitly multi-source: impacts, gravitational energy, differentiation and radioactive decay.
- Aluminium-26 is emphasised as an important early heat source especially for small planetesimals; it is not presented as the sole or dominant heat source for the full-grown Earth.
- Magma oceans are treated as strongly supported consequences of energetic accretion/giant impacts, while their exact depth, lifetime and crystallisation history remain model-dependent.
- Planetary differentiation is described as metal–silicate separation; iron meteorites are used as direct evidence that smaller planetary bodies differentiated early.
- Hf–W chronometry is presented as a process clock whose interpretation depends on accretion and metal–silicate equilibration, not a single exact "core birthday".

- The liquid outer core is supported by seismic-wave behaviour, particularly the absence of transmitted S waves.
- Core composition is described as mainly Fe–Ni plus lighter elements, while the exact light-element mixture remains uncertain.
- High-pressure experiments, seismology, meteorites and geochemistry are presented as independent constraints that must agree.
