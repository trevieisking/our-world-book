## PAGE 065 — THE IMPACT THAT MADE A MOON

The Moon did not need to exist.

Most rocky planets do not have anything like it.

Mercury has no moon.

Venus has no moon.

Mars has two tiny satellites that are nothing like ours in relative size.

Earth has a companion more than one quarter of its own diameter.

That demands an explanation.

Before the Apollo missions, several ideas competed seriously.

Perhaps the Moon formed beside Earth from the same part of the solar nebula.

Perhaps Earth captured a wandering body.

Perhaps the young Earth spun so rapidly that material separated from it and later became the Moon.

Each idea could explain part of the problem.

None explained everything very well.

Then astronauts brought the Moon home in boxes.

The six Apollo landing missions returned **382 kilograms** of lunar material. Those rocks transformed the argument because a theory of lunar origin now had to explain not just an orbit in the sky, but actual chemistry.

The modern leading explanation is the **giant-impact hypothesis**.

Near the end of Earth's accretion, another planetary embryo collided with the proto-Earth.

That hypothetical impactor is conventionally called **Theia**, after the mother of Selene in Greek mythology.

The name is useful.

It can also make the object sound more certain than it is.

We have not found a surviving planet called Theia.

We infer that a large impactor existed because a giant collision can explain several otherwise awkward properties of the Earth–Moon system.

In the classic version, Theia was roughly Mars-sized and struck Earth off-centre.

The collision expelled enormous quantities of hot silicate-rich material into orbit around Earth.

Some debris escaped.

Some fell back.

Some remained gravitationally bound in a circumterrestrial disk.

From that orbiting material, the Moon formed.

The broad giant-impact picture is now strongly supported.

The exact collision is not.

This difference will matter for the next several pages.

There is no single universally accepted set of values for Theia's mass, impact speed, impact angle, composition or spin.

Different simulations reproduce different parts of the evidence.

Some fit the present angular momentum well but struggle with isotopes.

Some mix Earth and impactor material more thoroughly but begin with too much angular momentum.

Some invoke hotter post-impact structures.

Some change the mass ratio of the colliding bodies.

Some even replace one dominant impact with several smaller ones.

So the first secure statement is not:

**"A Mars-sized planet called Theia hit Earth at exactly this angle."**

It is:

**"A giant collision late in Earth's accretion is the leading explanation for the Moon, while the detailed impact history remains under active investigation."**

The Moon's rocks tell us why.

## PAGE 066 — APOLLO TURNED THE MOON INTO EVIDENCE

Apollo did something no telescope could do.

It let scientists put pieces of the Moon into laboratory instruments.

The results were strange.

The Moon is made largely of silicate rock, like Earth's mantle, but it is comparatively poor in metallic iron.

Earth's iron-rich core contains roughly a third of the planet's mass.

The Moon's metallic core accounts for only a small fraction of its mass.

A Moon built mainly from the outer silicate layers of colliding differentiated bodies fits that observation naturally: the dense metallic cores of the impactors would tend to remain with the larger Earth while silicate-rich mantle material was much easier to place into orbit.

That is one clue.

Another is heat.

Lunar samples and orbital observations indicate that the young Moon was once extensively molten.

The idea of a **lunar magma ocean** grew directly from Apollo geology.

One especially important rock type is **anorthosite**, rich in the mineral plagioclase.

As a lunar magma ocean cooled, low-density plagioclase crystals could float upward while denser minerals sank.

The floating material helped build the Moon's ancient bright highland crust.

That is why widespread anorthositic crust is evidence not merely for volcanism, but for large-scale early melting and differentiation.

A giant impact supplies exactly the sort of energy capable of producing such an intensely heated Moon.

Then there are the volatiles.

Many lunar rocks are depleted in elements and compounds that are relatively easy to vaporise compared with terrestrial rocks.

That pattern is consistent with high-temperature processing.

But the Moon is not literally bone-dry.

Later analyses of volcanic glasses, minerals and other lunar materials found water and additional volatile components preserved inside lunar samples.

So the correct statement is:

**the Moon is depleted in many volatile elements relative to Earth, not devoid of volatiles.**

This matters because older descriptions sometimes overshot the evidence.

The Apollo samples also showed something even more puzzling.

In many isotopic systems, lunar and terrestrial mantle rocks are extraordinarily similar.

That similarity is one reason a giant collision with Earth remains attractive.

It is also one of the hardest problems the simplest giant-impact model has to solve.

The samples did not hand scientists a finished theory.

They handed them a list of constraints.

A successful origin model has to explain, at minimum:

the Moon's large relative size,

its small metallic core,

its highly heated early state,

its volatile depletion,

its orbit and angular momentum,

and its remarkable isotopic relationship with Earth.

The Moon is not allowing theorists to choose only the convenient clues.

It insists on all of them.

## PAGE 067 — THE ISOTOPE PROBLEM

Suppose the classic giant-impact model is correct.

A Mars-sized Theia strikes Earth.

Most of the debris that forms the Moon comes from Theia's outer layers.

Then a simple prediction follows:

if Theia formed from a chemically and isotopically different part of the Solar System, the Moon should carry a noticeable isotopic fingerprint of Theia.

For many years, that prediction seemed to fail spectacularly.

Earth and Moon samples are extremely similar in oxygen isotopes.

They are also very similar in isotopic systems involving elements such as titanium and tungsten.

Other Solar-System bodies do not all look identical in these systems.

Meteorites from different parent bodies often occupy distinct isotopic families.

So why should Theia, an independently formed planetary embryo, look almost exactly like Earth?

This became the **isotopic similarity problem**.

There are several possible solutions.

One is that Earth and Theia formed from the same local isotopic reservoir in the inner Solar System and were similar before they collided.

Dynamical models show that neighbouring planetary embryos can sometimes have similar source material, although the probability depends on how accretion is modelled.

Another solution is stronger mixing.

Perhaps the impact was energetic enough that silicate vapour from Earth and Theia mixed extensively before the Moon condensed.

High-energy models, including the **synestia** concept, explore this possibility.

A third family of solutions changes the impact geometry so that more of the Moon comes directly from proto-Earth rather than from Theia.

And then the samples became even more interesting.

Higher-precision measurements have revealed small Earth–Moon differences in some isotope systems.

A 2020 study reported measurable oxygen-isotope differences among lunar materials.

A 2021 study found the Moon's vanadium isotopic composition offset from bulk silicate Earth and argued that the result could actually be compatible with a more canonical impact if Earth and Theia formed from a broadly similar reservoir.

These results do not make the Moon "isotopically different from Earth" in an ordinary sense.

The overall similarity remains extraordinary.

They show that **near-identical is not the same as perfectly identical**.

And those tiny differences are useful.

If Earth and Moon were absolutely homogenised after impact, certain pre-impact differences should have been erased.

If they preserve subtle differences, complete equilibration becomes harder to require.

That is why isotope measurements can change impact models even when the numerical differences look microscopic.

A few parts per million can decide whether two planets were thoroughly mixed.

Deep history sometimes turns on decimals.

## PAGE 068 — ANGULAR MOMENTUM GETS A VOTE

Chemistry is not the only judge.

The Earth–Moon system moves.

Earth spins.

The Moon orbits.

Together those motions contain **angular momentum**.

Any proposed Moon-forming impact has to produce a system whose dynamical evolution can reach the one we observe today.

This was one of the great successes of the classic giant-impact model.

An oblique collision with a roughly Mars-sized body can eject enough iron-poor silicate debris to make a Moon-sized satellite while also producing angular momentum of roughly the required scale.

But "roughly the required scale" hides decades of work.

The Moon formed much closer to Earth than it is today.

Tidal interactions then transferred angular momentum between Earth's rotation and the Moon's orbit.

Earth's rotation slowed.

The Moon moved outward.

Laser ranging experiments now measure that recession directly: the Moon is still moving away from Earth by a few centimetres per year.

The present orbit is therefore not the orbit the newborn Moon occupied.

Likewise, the present length of Earth's day is not the day length immediately after the impact.

Researchers must run the clock backward using tidal physics.

The total angular momentum of the Earth–Moon system has been approximately conserved over most of its history, but not perfectly isolated from the rest of the Solar System.

Solar torques and possible orbital resonances complicate the early evolution.

That matters because some high-energy Moon-forming impacts produce more angular momentum than the Earth–Moon system has today.

Those models therefore require a later mechanism capable of removing the excess.

Proposed resonance interactions with the Sun may do some of that work under particular conditions.

Whether they work efficiently enough across the necessary parameter range remains model-dependent.

This gives us an important methodological rule.

A spectacular impact simulation is not successful merely because it produces something round that looks like Earth and a blob that looks like the Moon.

It must also reproduce:

mass,

composition,

iron fraction,

isotopes,

orbit,

and angular momentum,

or provide a physically credible evolutionary route from its initial result to the modern system.

The movie is not the evidence.

The numbers underneath it are.

## PAGE 069 — THERE IS NO SINGLE "GIANT IMPACT MODEL"

When people say **the giant-impact theory**, it sounds like one calculation.

It is better to think of it as a family.

### The canonical impact

The classic version uses an impactor roughly comparable to Mars in mass striking a nearly formed Earth at relatively low speed and at an oblique angle.

It naturally produces an iron-poor debris disk and can reproduce the broad angular-momentum budget.

Its major historical difficulty is that many simulations make the disk mostly from impactor material.

That is awkward if the impactor should have been isotopically different from Earth.

### High-angular-momentum impacts

Other models begin with a rapidly spinning Earth or use a more energetic collision.

These can put much more terrestrial material into orbit and increase mixing.

The price is often too much angular momentum.

A later mechanism then has to remove the excess.

### The synestia

At sufficiently high energy and angular momentum, the post-impact body may not resemble a planet surrounded by a neat disk.

Rock can become partly vaporised, and the structure can extend outward as a hot, rotating mass in which the usual distinction between planet and disk breaks down.

This proposed structure is called a **synestia**.

In synestia models, lunar material can interact extensively with silicate vapour, potentially helping explain Earth–Moon isotopic similarity and volatile patterns.

It is physically motivated.

It is not directly observed in the ancient Earth–Moon system.

### Similar impactor, less mixing

Another approach keeps a more conventional collision but assumes Theia itself formed from material isotopically very similar to Earth.

This is supported by dynamical and isotopic arguments showing that Earth's late accretion came from a restricted inner-Solar-System reservoir.

### Multiple impacts

A less conventional proposal replaces one giant Moon-forming collision with a sequence of smaller impacts.

Each produces a disk and a moonlet.

The moonlets later merge.

This can ease some fine-tuning problems, but it introduces others and is not the leading interpretation.

The important conclusion is not that all of these models are equally good.

They are not.

It is that **the giant-collision origin is more secure than the exact giant-collision geometry**.

Modern systematic simulations continue to find that no single simple scenario reproduces every geochemical and dynamical constraint without additional assumptions.

That is not failure.

It is the scientific problem becoming more precise.

## PAGE 070 — FROM IMPACT DEBRIS TO A MOON

After the collision, there was no familiar Moon hanging above a familiar Earth.

There was heat.

Debris.

Melt.

Vapour.

And orbiting silicate material.

Some of the ejecta had enough speed to escape Earth entirely.

Some returned to the planet.

Some remained in orbit.

Material inside the region where Earth's tides prevented stable clumping stayed dispersed or repeatedly collided.

Farther out, orbiting debris could accrete.

Early dynamical models showed that an impact-generated disk can assemble a large satellite rapidly on astronomical timescales.

Modern simulations vary in detail, but the broad route remains plausible:

**impact -> bound debris -> orbiting disk or extended post-impact structure -> lunar accretion.**

The newborn Moon was hot.

Very hot.

A giant impact followed by rapid accretion releases enormous energy.

Much or perhaps nearly all of the Moon's silicate portion melted.

As that **lunar magma ocean** cooled, minerals crystallised.

Dense early minerals tended to sink.

Later, plagioclase became stable and, because it was comparatively buoyant in the melt, accumulated toward the surface.

That helped build the primordial anorthositic crust preserved in the lunar highlands.

The Moon also differentiated internally.

Metal moved inward to form its small core.

Silicates formed mantle and crust.

Heat-producing radioactive elements became concentrated unevenly.

The result was not a dead grey sphere created instantly in final form.

It was a differentiated world undergoing its own geological evolution.

And it still contained volatiles.

Apollo-era descriptions sometimes portrayed the Moon as essentially waterless.

Modern measurements have shown a more complicated picture.

Lunar volcanic glass beads, apatite and other materials contain indigenous water or hydroxyl.

The Moon is still strongly volatile-depleted compared with Earth in many respects, and high-temperature loss is real.

But complete desiccation is not.

That distinction is important because the giant impact must explain selective loss and retention, not simply "everything volatile boiled away."

The Moon's birth was hot.

Its chemistry was not erased.

## PAGE 071 — WHEN DID THE MOON FORM?

A frustrating question has several possible answers.

**When did the Moon form?**

Do we mean the instant of the giant impact?

The time the orbiting debris assembled into a Moon-sized body?

The time the lunar magma ocean finished crystallising?

The age of the oldest surviving lunar zircon?

The age when a later heating event last reset an isotope clock?

Those are different events.

And the lunar record has been heated, melted and bombarded enough to blur them.

For years, many estimates placed Moon formation tens of millions of years after the first Solar-System solids.

Other chronometers and lunar samples have suggested later ages.

Published estimates for key lunar formation or differentiation events have spanned roughly **4.35 to 4.51 billion years ago**.

That is not a small disagreement.

It is more than one hundred million years.

A 2024 Nature study proposed an important reconciliation.

The authors argued that many lunar samples recording ages near **4.35 billion years** may have been reset by intense tidal heating as the early Moon's orbit evolved through a dynamical transition.

Under that interpretation, the Moon itself could be older — formed within the first few tens of millions of years of Solar-System history — while later remelting reset many of the clocks that had been used to date its birth.

This is a current hypothesis, not the final word.

Other chronology models remain in the literature.

The lesson for our book is straightforward:

**do not give the Moon a falsely precise birthday.**

We can say it formed very early in Solar-System history and almost certainly during the late stages of Earth's accretion.

We can say a giant impact is the leading explanation.

We can date particular lunar minerals and rocks.

We can model magma-ocean crystallisation.

But the exact impact date remains actively debated because the clocks may record different physical events.

A date is only useful when we know what the clock was actually timing.

That sentence applies to geology so often that it could probably be printed on the laboratory door.

## PAGE 072 — EARTH AFTER THE COLLISION

The Moon-forming event did not merely create a satellite.

It changed Earth.

A sufficiently energetic giant impact would have melted enormous portions of the planet.

Silicate vapour and molten rock would have surrounded the post-impact Earth.

The planet's rotation would have been altered.

Its mantle would have been mixed to some degree.

Metal from the impactor could have joined Earth's core.

Some volatile material could have escaped.

Other material would have remained bound or later been reaccreted.

Exactly how much mixing, loss and survival occurred depends on the impact model.

That uncertainty reaches surprisingly deep into modern Earth science.

A 2023 study proposed that two enormous dense structures now detected seismically near the base of Earth's mantle — the large low-velocity provinces — could contain relic material from Theia.

The simulations are intriguing.

They do **not** prove that pieces of Theia are sitting above the core.

The structures have other possible origins.

So for now, that idea belongs in the category:

**testable and interesting, not established.**

What is established is enough.

By the end of the Moon-forming era, Earth was a differentiated rocky planet with a metallic core, a silicate mantle and a large satellite.

Its surface had been heated catastrophically.

It needed to cool again.

That cooling would determine what came next.

A crust had to form.

An atmosphere had to evolve.

Volcanic gases had to escape from the interior.

Water had to be delivered, retained, released or recycled in sufficient quantities for liquid oceans eventually to exist.

And impacts had not stopped.

The Solar System still contained leftover debris.

The young Earth was about to become a place with a surface history.

For the first time in our journey, we can begin asking not simply:

**How was a planet assembled?**

but:

**What was it like to stand on that planet — if standing had been possible?**

The answer begins with fire, gas and cooling rock.

### Fact-check decisions
- The giant-impact origin is described as the leading and strongly supported explanation; **Theia's exact mass, orbit, composition and impact geometry are not treated as established facts**.
- Apollo returned **382 kg** of lunar material; sample chemistry, the Moon's small core, volatile depletion, and evidence for a lunar magma ocean are used as constraints rather than as a single-proof argument.
- The Moon is described as volatile-depleted, **not volatile-free**.
- Earth–Moon isotopic similarity is preserved as a major constraint, while high-precision evidence for small differences in oxygen and vanadium is also included.
- Angular momentum is treated as a dynamical constraint and is distinguished from the modern Earth–Moon orbital configuration after billions of years of tidal evolution.
- Canonical, high-energy/synestia, Earth-dominated, similar-impactor and multiple-impact models are described as model families; they are **not** presented as equally supported.
- A giant collision is more secure than the exact collision geometry.
- The age of the Moon is not assigned one false exact value. Published formation/differentiation interpretations span roughly **4.35–4.51 Ga**, and the 2024 tidal-remelting model is labelled as a current hypothesis that may reconcile older formation with younger reset ages.
- The proposal that Earth's present-day large low-velocity provinces contain Theia remnants is labelled **hypothesis/model result, not established evidence**.
