# CHAPTER TWO — BUILDING A WORLD

### ATMOSPHERES CAN LEAK

A planet does not simply receive an atmosphere and keep it forever.

Gas escapes.

The lightest gases escape most easily.

Hydrogen is particularly vulnerable.

Ultraviolet radiation can split water and other hydrogen-bearing molecules high in an atmosphere. Hydrogen can then escape to space. Under sufficiently intense early stellar radiation, escape can become much more powerful than the slow leakage occurring today.

Large impacts can also alter an atmosphere.

Some impact energy can remove gas.

The impactor can simultaneously deliver new volatile material.

Magma generated by the collision can outgas additional gases.

One event can therefore both reduce an atmosphere and help rebuild it.

This is why the phrase **"the early atmosphere"** can be misleading.

There was not necessarily one stable gas mixture sitting above Earth throughout the Hadean.

There was an evolving exchange system among the mantle, crust, atmosphere, hydrosphere, incoming material and space.

The balance mattered for water.

Lose too much hydrogen and the planet dries.

Retain enough hydrogen and oxygen in bound forms, and water can survive within minerals, melts, atmosphere and eventually surface reservoirs.

A 2023 model proposed that interactions between hydrogen-rich primordial atmospheres and magma oceans in Earth's building blocks could have helped produce some of Earth's water while also affecting mantle oxidation and core composition.

That is an intriguing route.

It is not a demonstration that all terrestrial water formed that way.

Other evidence shows that volatile-bearing asteroidal material was also added during accretion.

The important conclusion is broader:

Earth's water inventory did not have to arrive in one delivery.

It could be accumulated, produced, lost, recycled and replenished across several stages of planet formation.

Water has a history.

And that history is more complicated than a comet hitting a dry rock.
