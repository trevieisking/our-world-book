### THE FIRST ATMOSPHERES

Earth probably had more than one early atmosphere.

Growing planetary embryos could capture hydrogen-rich gas from the solar nebula while that nebula still existed. A young proto-Earth may therefore have possessed a temporary primary atmosphere richer in hydrogen than the atmosphere we know today.

That atmosphere did not survive intact.

Young stars emit strong ultraviolet and X-ray radiation. Smaller planetary embryos also have weaker gravity than the finished Earth, and large impacts can remove or transform large amounts of gas. Much of any primordial hydrogen-rich envelope could therefore be lost, chemically changed or incorporated into the growing planet.

Then came atmospheres produced from Earth itself.

Volatiles dissolved in magma were released as the mantle melted, convected and crystallised. This is **outgassing**.

The exact gas mixture depended on pressure, temperature and especially the oxidation state of the mantle. More reduced conditions favour species such as hydrogen and carbon monoxide, while more oxidised conditions favour water vapour, carbon dioxide and sulfur-bearing gases.

Hadean zircon chemistry provides an indirect clue. Measurements of cerium in ancient zircons suggest that by roughly 4.35 billion years ago, at least some Hadean magmas had oxidation states broadly comparable with later mantle-derived magmas.

But this does not give us one exact Hadean air sample.

The atmosphere evolved.

Impacts changed it.

Escape changed it.

Oceans, once present, changed it.

One point is secure: an oxygen-rich modern atmosphere belongs much later in Earth's story.

The early atmosphere was chemically alien even when liquid water may already have existed.
