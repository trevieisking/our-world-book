# CHAPTER FIVE — A PLANET OF MICROBES
## Page 170 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### WHEN DOES A MICROSTRUCTURE BECOME A MICROFOSSIL?

A sphere is easy for nature to make.

A filament is easy too.

Minerals grow as fibres.

Fluid bubbles become round.

Organic coatings wrap grains.

Crystals dissolve and leave tubes.

Carbon moves through fractures.

At microscopic scale, geology produces shapes that look disturbingly biological.

This is why an ancient microfossil must be more than a familiar silhouette.

The Strelley Pool Formation provides a useful example.

Researchers have reported large populations of carbonaceous microscopic structures from its roughly 3.4-billion-year-old cherts.

They include spheroids.

Lenticular, lens-shaped forms.

Filaments.

Films.

Some occur in colonies.

Some lenticular forms possess flange-like walls or other repeated structural features.

In 2011, David Wacey and colleagues described microstructures in ancient beach sandstones that were associated with microscopic pyrite crystals.

Carbon and sulfur analyses supported the interpretation that they were fossil microorganisms involved in sulfur metabolism.

Later work on Strelley Pool material used Raman spectroscopy, electron microscopy, NanoSIMS and chemical isolation to test the structures in greater detail.

Why does this count for more than “it looks like a cell”?

First, **context**.

The structures occur within primary sedimentary chert rather than only in later fractures.

Second, **composition**.

They contain carbonaceous material with thermal characteristics consistent with the age and alteration history of the host rock.

Third, **population behaviour**.

Hundreds of structures occur with repeatable size ranges and shapes.

Random mineral artefacts do not necessarily form populations with biologically plausible variation.

Fourth, **wall structure**.

Some specimens preserve continuous outer boundaries and repeated internal/external organisation that are harder to reproduce through simple crystal growth.

Fifth, **elemental associations**.

Nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur and carbon distributions can sometimes preserve remnants of biological chemistry.

None of those criteria alone is infallible.

Together they can make a strong case.

A 2022 study tackled one of the most difficult problems directly: simple spheroids.

The researchers compared Strelley Pool carbonaceous spheres with abiotic lookalikes produced by mineral growth and organic-matter migration.

At nanoscale resolution, genuine candidate cells differed in wall organisation and chemical distribution from the pseudocellular mineral structures.

This kind of experiment improves palaeontology because it does not merely ask whether a fossil interpretation is attractive.

It asks whether the strongest **false positive** can be distinguished from it.

Even then, taxonomic ambition has to remain modest.

A 3.4-billion-year-old lenticular cell cannot usually be assigned to a modern bacterial genus.

A filament does not automatically mean cyanobacterium.

A sulfur-rich microfossil does not uniquely identify one sulfur metabolism.

Billions of years of evolution separate those organisms from living groups.

What the fossils can establish is enough.

They were cellular.

They occurred in populations.

Different morphologies existed.

Some were associated with sulfur-rich microenvironments.

Some may have lived planktonically or on sediment.

And early life had already diversified into more than one cellular form.

That is far more direct evidence than an isotope ratio alone.

But the history of early-life research contains a warning.

One of the most famous sets of “oldest cells” ever announced came from rocks slightly older than Strelley Pool.

They looked extraordinarily biological.

For years, they appeared in textbooks.

Then geologists looked more closely at the rock around them.

The Apex Chert became a case study in how difficult the oldest fossil record can be.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Wacey et al. (2011), *Nature Geoscience*: 3.4 Ga Strelley Pool microstructures with carbon/sulfur evidence interpreted as sulfur-metabolising cells.
- Strelley Pool assemblages include spheroidal, lenticular, filamentous and film-like carbonaceous structures; later nanoscale studies examine wall chemistry and preservation.
- 2022 work explicitly compares cellular-looking Strelley Pool spheroids with abiotic pseudocellular structures.
- Morphology alone is insufficient for Archean microfossil identification.

**Decision:** microfossil confidence is built from syngenetic context, composition, population structure, cell-wall-scale organisation and tests against abiotic biomorphs.
