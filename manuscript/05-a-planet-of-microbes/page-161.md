# CHAPTER FIVE — A PLANET OF MICROBES
## Page 161 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### SOMEONE HAS TO MAKE THE FOOD

Every ecosystem needs a way to turn inorganic carbon into organic matter.

Modern surface ecosystems are dominated by plants, algae and cyanobacteria.

They use light.

The early Earth had no plants and no algae.

Its primary producers were microbes.

Some may have used light.

Others used chemical energy.

The shared task was **carbon fixation**:

taking carbon dioxide or bicarbonate and converting it into carbon compounds that cells can build into biomass.

This is energetically difficult chemistry.

Carbon dioxide is stable.

Reducing it requires electrons.

Modern organisms solve the problem with several different carbon-fixation pathways.

Some of those pathways may be extremely ancient.

The acetyl-CoA pathway is often singled out because it is used by anaerobic archaea and bacteria, depends heavily on metal-containing enzymes and can couple carbon fixation directly to energy metabolism.

Reverse-TCA chemistry provides another route.

Chapter Four showed why origin researchers pay attention to them: metals can reproduce parts of both systems without enzymes.

But the existence of a non-enzymatic analogue does not tell us which pathway an organism in the Dresser Formation actually used.

Ancient carbon isotopes help at a broader level.

When cells fix carbon dioxide, enzymes generally discriminate between the heavier carbon isotope, carbon-13, and the lighter carbon-12.

Organic matter can therefore become depleted in carbon-13 relative to its inorganic carbon source.

This fractionation survives in ancient carbonaceous material.

It is one of the strongest tools for recognising biological carbon processing.

The difficulty is specificity.

Different carbon-fixation pathways produce overlapping isotope effects.

Temperature and carbon availability alter fractionation.

Methane-related metabolisms can generate especially light carbon.

Abiotic hydrothermal reactions can also produce fractionated organics.

So a negative carbon-isotope value can support biology without identifying one pathway.

The ecological conclusion is stronger than the biochemical one.

By 3.48 billion years ago, microbes were producing enough organic carbon to leave repeated traces in sediment and hydrothermal settings.

That organic matter became the foundation for other metabolisms.

A primary producer does not merely build itself.

When it leaks compounds, sheds material or dies, its carbon becomes food.

Other organisms can ferment that material.

Others can respire it using sulfur, iron or other electron acceptors.

Still others can exploit the hydrogen, acetate or methane produced downstream.

An ecosystem therefore begins with carbon fixation and immediately branches.

This is why ancient mats can become chemically complex even when every organism is microscopic.

At an illuminated surface, phototrophs may generate organic matter.

Deeper in the mat, organisms consume it.

In dark rock, chemoautotrophs may make new biomass from CO₂ using hydrogen or other geochemical fuels.

In sediment, buried organic material drives anaerobic respiration.

Carbon moves from inorganic reservoir to biomass to waste to another organism and back toward inorganic carbon.

No oxygen is required for that cycle to exist.

The early carbon cycle was not the modern carbon cycle.

But life had already inserted itself into planetary carbon flow.

And one reduced carbon compound was especially important.

It can be made biologically from carbon dioxide.

It carries both carbon and chemical energy.

It can escape from sediments into the ocean and atmosphere.

And on an oxygen-poor early Earth, it may have accumulated far more readily than it does today.

Methane.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Biological carbon fractionation is a major early-life proxy but generally does not uniquely identify a carbon-fixation pathway.
- Acetyl-CoA and reverse-TCA pathways are central candidates for ancient carbon fixation, supported by comparative biology and partial non-enzymatic analogues; pathway presence at a specific 3.5 Ga site is not assumed.
- Dresser carbonaceous matter establishes substantial biological carbon processing at ecosystem scale.

**Decision:** reconstruct primary production functionally. Exact ancient enzymes/pathways are not assigned where isotope evidence cannot distinguish them.
