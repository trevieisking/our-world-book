# CHAPTER FIVE — A PLANET OF MICROBES
## Page 176 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### THE MICROBIAL PLANET

For most readers, life's history begins too late.

Animals attract attention because they leave bones.

Plants reshape landscapes because we can see them.

But long before either existed, microbes had already performed some of the most important evolutionary experiments in Earth's history.

By at least about 3.48 billion years ago, microbial communities were building mats and stromatolitic structures.

They occupied volcanic, hydrothermal and shallow sedimentary settings.

By roughly 3.4 billion years ago, the record preserves diverse stromatolites and increasingly convincing cellular microfossils.

Possible subsurface communities lived in hydrothermal fractures.

By 3.22 billion years ago, microbial mats occupied river-dominated terrestrial environments.

Carbon moved through biological systems.

Sulfur cycled.

Iron became part of microbial redox ecology.

Methane was probably being produced biologically by very early in the Archean, although the oldest site-specific evidence remains debated.

Biological nitrogen fixation is securely recorded by about 3.2 billion years ago.

Communities divided chemical labour.

Mats organised metabolism across microscopic gradients.

Life did not wait for complexity before becoming ecological.

It did not wait for animals before becoming planetary.

Microbes changed sediments.

They changed mineral formation.

They altered weathering.

They influenced greenhouse gases.

They redirected carbon, sulfur, iron and nitrogen.

Then one lineage — or ancestral population of lineages — evolved the ability to extract electrons from water using light.

The date remains uncertain.

Molecular evolution may push the innovation deep into the Archean.

Geochemical evidence shows local oxygen production well before the atmosphere became oxygenated.

The 2025 cerium chronology places oxygenic photosynthesis by at least about 2.87 billion years ago.

By the late Archean, multiple proxies record local oxygen oases.

Still the global atmosphere remained overwhelmingly anoxic.

That is where Chapter Five must stop.

The story of microbes is not over.

It never will be.

Microbes still dominate enormous parts of Earth's biogeochemical machinery today.

But the next transition is larger than an ecosystem.

It changes the chemistry of oceans.

It changes minerals exposed on land.

It changes the atmosphere.

It creates energetic possibilities for later complex life.

And it also creates a planetary toxin for organisms adapted to an anaerobic world.

Oxygen is both opportunity and catastrophe.

The critical question is no longer whether microbes can make it.

They can.

The question becomes:

**What happens when the planet can no longer consume it as fast as biology produces it?**

For hundreds of millions of years, oxygen was local.

Then the balance changed.

The geological record captures that change in sulfur isotopes, iron minerals, red beds, trace metals and the disappearance of chemical signatures that can exist only under an oxygen-poor atmosphere.

Earth crossed another threshold.

Not from non-life to life.

From an anaerobic biosphere to a planet whose surface chemistry would increasingly be controlled by oxygen.
