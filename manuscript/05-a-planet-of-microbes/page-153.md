# CHAPTER FIVE — A PLANET OF MICROBES
## Page 153 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### A WORLD TOO SMALL TO SEE

For most of Earth's history, there were no forests.

No grass.

No insects.

No fish.

No animals walking across land.

Life was microbial.

That does not mean the planet was biologically simple.

A single modern drop of water can contain organisms using radically different ways of obtaining carbon, electrons and energy.

Microbes cooperate.

They compete.

They exchange metabolites.

They build films.

They alter minerals.

They recycle waste from one metabolism into fuel for another.

The earliest preserved biosphere was almost certainly invisible at human scale as individual organisms.

Its **effects** were not.

Microbial communities can change a landscape.

Grow enough cells together and they trap sediment.

They bind grains with sticky extracellular material.

They precipitate minerals.

They consume gases and release others.

They alter the oxidation state of iron and sulfur.

They discriminate between isotopes as enzymes process molecules.

They leave carbon behind.

Over time, microscopic metabolism becomes geology.

This changes the evidence problem.

A dinosaur can leave a skeleton.

A tree can leave a trunk.

The oldest microorganisms usually leave something less direct.

A layer.

A wrinkle.

A filament.

A carbon-rich patch.

A sulfur isotope pattern.

A mineral arrangement.

A repeated structure in sediment.

No single category is automatically biological.

Minerals can imitate cells.

Physical sediment processes can build laminations.

Hydrothermal chemistry can create organic compounds and unusual isotope ratios.

Later fluids can carry younger carbon into older rocks.

Metamorphism can stretch a genuine microfossil until it no longer resembles its original form.

That is why Chapter Four began with the distinction between **biogenicity** and **syngeneity**.

We now use it in practice.

Was a feature produced by life?

And is it genuinely the same age as the rock in which it occurs?

The strongest early-life sites survive because several lines of evidence converge.

Sedimentary structures fit microbial growth.

Carbon occurs in the right geological context.

Isotopes are compatible with biological processing.

Textures repeat across an environment rather than appearing as one curious shape.

The rock's depositional history is reconstructed independently.

Abiotic alternatives are tested.

By about **3.48 billion years ago**, that convergence becomes remarkably strong in the Dresser Formation of Western Australia.

The importance of Dresser is not that it contains one object we can point to and say:

“That was the first organism.”

It does not.

Its importance is that it preserves an **ecosystem**.

Microbial mats.

Stromatolitic structures.

Carbonaceous material.

Sulfur chemistry.

Hydrothermal settings.

Shallow illuminated environments.

Different habitats existing within the same volcanic landscape.

This is already far beyond the first successful replicator imagined in Chapter Four.

Whatever crossed the chemistry-to-biology boundary did so earlier.

By the time the Dresser rocks formed, descendants had spread into environmental niches and begun modifying sediments on a scale large enough to survive for almost three and a half billion years.

The origin of life may be hidden.

The first biosphere is less hidden.

But we have to learn how to read what it wrote.

The language is sediment.

## Research / fact-check notes
- 2025 *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* review: the Palaeoarchaean record by ~3.48 Ga contains widespread manifestations of life and evidence for ecological/metabolic diversification.
- Dresser Formation evidence includes multiple sedimentary, morphological, organic and isotopic biosignatures.
- No Dresser structure is treated as the first organism or the site of life's origin.

**Decision:** Chapter 5 shifts from origin mechanisms to ecosystem reconstruction. Early-life claims will be strongest where multiple independent biosignatures agree with geological context.
