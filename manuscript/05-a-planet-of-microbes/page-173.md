# CHAPTER FIVE — A PLANET OF MICROBES
## Page 173 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### WHEN WATER BECAME AN ELECTRON DONOR

Anoxygenic phototrophy already solved a major problem.

Use sunlight for energy.

Take electrons from compounds such as hydrogen sulfide, hydrogen or ferrous iron.

Fix carbon.

But every one of those electron donors is limited by geology.

Hydrogen sulfide occurs where sulfur chemistry supplies it.

Hydrogen depends on particular geochemical reactions.

Ferrous iron is abundant only under the right redox conditions.

Water is different.

It is everywhere life lives.

The evolutionary step that changed Earth was learning to use **water itself** as an electron donor.

Split H₂O.

Extract electrons and protons.

Release molecular oxygen, O₂, as a by-product.

This is **oxygenic photosynthesis**.

It is chemically difficult.

Pulling electrons from water requires an exceptionally strong oxidant and a sophisticated photosynthetic reaction centre.

Modern cyanobacteria solve the problem with Photosystem II and a manganese–calcium cluster that accumulates enough oxidising power to split water.

The invention opened an enormous energy resource.

Phototrophs no longer depended on a limited external supply of reduced sulfur, hydrogen or iron.

Sunlight and water could support primary production across vast surface environments.

The by-product was initially a problem for the organisms making it.

Oxygen is reactive.

It damages molecules that evolved in an anoxic world.

Cells need pigments, antioxidants, repair systems and enzymes to survive it.

But oxygen's reactivity is precisely why its later accumulation transformed the planet.

When did water splitting begin?

For decades, the answer has remained one of the most contested dates in early evolution.

Some molecular-clock studies place the origins of Photosystem II or oxygenic photosynthesis deep in the Archean, possibly before 3.0 billion years ago and in some models considerably earlier.

Those estimates depend on evolutionary models, calibration choices and how ancient gene duplications are interpreted.

The fossil record of identifiable cyanobacteria is younger and ambiguous in its oldest portions.

A cell shaped like a cyanobacterium is not proof that it produced oxygen.

Stromatolites can be built by anoxygenic phototrophs.

Even ancient pigments rarely survive in a form that uniquely identifies oxygenic photosynthesis.

Geochemistry therefore becomes critical.

In 2025, Laureline Patry and colleagues reported an important new constraint from three Archean carbonate platforms in Canada.

The deposits are about **2.87, 2.85 and 2.78 billion years old**.

They contain negative cerium anomalies.

Cerium behaves differently from neighbouring rare-earth elements when Ce³⁺ is oxidised to Ce⁴⁺ and removed from seawater.

Such depletion can therefore record the presence of oxygen.

The difficult question is when the oxidation happened.

Could younger oxygenated fluids have altered an older rock?

The researchers used the radioactive decay of lanthanum-138 to cerium-138 to date the rare-earth-element fractionation itself.

Their results indicated that the cerium oxidation occurred during deposition.

That makes these the oldest directly dated cerium anomalies currently reported.

The implication is significant:

**oxygenic photosynthesis existed by at least about 2.87 billion years ago.**

The origin must be older than the evidence.

How much older remains unresolved.

Claims for oxygen production around 3.0 billion years ago or earlier exist from other geochemical proxies and molecular clocks.

Some are persuasive.

Some have been challenged.

None yet supplies an uncontested exact birthday.

That is the same rule we used for life itself.

Evidence can establish:

**oxygenic photosynthesis existed by this date.**

It cannot automatically establish:

**oxygenic photosynthesis originated at this date.**

By 2.87 billion years ago, however, the microbial world had acquired the metabolism that would eventually change the atmosphere.

The change did not happen immediately.

For a long time, oxygen production and an oxygen-rich planet were two different things.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Patry et al. (2025), *Nature*: directly dated negative Ce anomalies in 2.87, 2.85 and 2.78 Ga carbonate platforms indicate oxygen production at deposition and place oxygenic photosynthesis in the Mesoarchaean or earlier.
- Molecular-clock and older geochemical estimates can place oxygenic photosynthesis earlier, but model/proxy disagreements remain substantial.
- Stromatolite morphology and generic cyanobacterium-like cell shape do not uniquely demonstrate water-splitting photosynthesis.

**Decision:** use ~2.87 Ga as a strong directly dated minimum constraint from the 2025 Ce study, not as the origin date of oxygenic photosynthesis.
