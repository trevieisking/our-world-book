# CHAPTER FIVE — A PLANET OF MICROBES
## Page 158 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### LIFE BENEATH THE SEAFLOOR

The surface gets sunlight.

The subsurface does not.

Yet the modern Earth contains an enormous biosphere beneath sediments and inside fractured rock.

Microbes there live on chemistry.

Hydrogen.

Methane.

Sulfur compounds.

Iron.

Carbon dioxide.

Organic material carried down from above.

Was this hidden habitat already occupied more than three billion years ago?

Rocks from the Barberton Greenstone Belt in South Africa provide a striking possibility.

In 2021, Barbara Cavalazzi and colleagues described filamentous microscopic structures from a roughly **3.42-billion-year-old subseafloor hydrothermal vein system**.

The veins cut ultramafic volcanic rock beneath an ancient marine setting.

Low-temperature hydrothermal fluids had moved through fractures.

Along the walls of those conduits, the researchers found branching and non-branching filaments preserved within mineralised material.

The structures were not interpreted from shape alone.

Their three-dimensional morphology was examined.

Their carbonaceous composition was mapped.

Nickel was associated with organic material in some structures.

The geological relationship between the filaments and the hydrothermal vein was reconstructed.

The authors concluded that the filaments were putative microfossils of indigenous subsurface microorganisms.

They went further.

Based on the setting and chemical characteristics, they proposed that the organisms participated in **methane cycling** — potentially methanogens, which produce methane, or methanotrophs, which consume it.

That is a fascinating interpretation.

It should not silently become a fact stronger than the evidence.

The word **putative** matters.

Mineral systems can create filamentous forms.

Hydrothermal fluids can move carbon and metals.

Metamorphism can modify original textures.

And a chemical environment compatible with methane metabolism does not identify the organism that occupied it.

The Barberton filaments are therefore best treated as a strong and unusually detailed candidate for ancient subsurface life, with a methane-linked ecological interpretation that remains inferential.

If the biological interpretation is correct, the implications are important.

By 3.42 billion years ago, life was not confined to illuminated microbial mats.

Microorganisms inhabited fractures below the seafloor.

They exploited chemical energy delivered by water–rock reactions and circulating fluids.

The early biosphere therefore occupied both **surface** and **subsurface** habitats.

This creates an ecological connection back to Chapter Four.

Hydrothermal systems may or may not have been the birthplace of life.

But they certainly became habitats for life very early.

That distinction prevents a common error.

Finding ancient organisms at a hydrothermal vent does not prove that organisms originated at vents.

Penguins live on Antarctic ice.

That does not mean birds originated in Antarctica.

Habitat and birthplace are different questions.

The Barberton evidence tells us about adaptation.

By the Palaeoarchaean, microbial evolution had produced systems capable of living without sunlight in chemically powered rock environments.

If methane cycling was involved, then carbon was already moving between reduced and oxidised forms through biological processes.

And if similar metabolisms existed alongside phototrophic mats and sulfur-processing communities, the early biosphere was beginning to look ecologically partitioned.

Different organisms.

Different energy sources.

Different habitats.

Connected by the chemistry of one planet.

Older rocks may preserve an even deeper version of that network.

But they have been damaged much more severely.

For those, shapes mostly fail us.

We have to read carbon and sulfur.

We return to Greenland.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Cavalazzi et al. (2021), *Science Advances*: ~3.42 Ga putative filamentous microfossils in a palaeo-subseafloor hydrothermal vein system in the Barberton Greenstone Belt.
- Authors interpret morphology/chemistry as consistent with methane-cycling microorganisms and describe them as the oldest evidence for indigenous subsurface microorganisms.
- Methanogen/methanotroph assignment remains inferential and is not treated as taxonomically confirmed.

**Decision:** Barberton demonstrates a strong candidate for early subsurface life and habitat diversification; it does not establish a hydrothermal origin of life.
