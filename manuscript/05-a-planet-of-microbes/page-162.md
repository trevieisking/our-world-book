# CHAPTER FIVE — A PLANET OF MICROBES
## Page 162 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### THE METHANE WORLD

Methane is one carbon atom surrounded by four hydrogens.

CH₄.

Simple formula.

Enormous planetary consequences.

Modern methanogens are archaea that make methane in oxygen-free environments.

Some reduce carbon dioxide with hydrogen.

Others use acetate or small methylated compounds.

The reactions provide energy while disposing of electrons.

In sediments, wetlands, animal guts and hydrothermal systems, methane production often occurs where stronger electron acceptors have been exhausted.

On the early Earth, such an anaerobic metabolism could have been ecologically important.

The atmosphere contained almost no free oxygen to destroy methane rapidly near the surface.

Methane is also a greenhouse gas.

Enough biological methane could have contributed to keeping parts of the young Earth warm under the faint young Sun.

How much methane existed is uncertain.

Atmospheric chemistry limits how strongly methane can accumulate.

Haze formation may change climate.

Volcanoes and water–rock reactions can produce abiotic methane too.

So the climate argument cannot be used as proof that methanogens existed.

The rocks provide a more direct test.

In 2006, researchers analysed methane trapped in fluid inclusions within roughly 3.5-billion-year-old hydrothermal precipitates of the Dresser Formation.

Some methane was extremely depleted in carbon-13, with values below roughly minus 56 per mil.

The authors interpreted this as microbial methane and argued for methanogenesis older than 3.46 billion years.

That remains an important result.

It is not uncontested.

Hydrothermal systems can generate methane abiotically.

Later work on the Dresser environmental setting complicated assumptions used to exclude other biological carbon sources.

Reviews therefore treat the Dresser methane evidence as plausible early methanogenesis rather than an uncontested taxonomic fossil.

By around 3.0 billion years ago, very light carbon in sedimentary organic matter provides stronger evidence that methane-producing ecosystems were widespread.

Evidence for organisms **consuming** methane appears later and is generally more difficult to identify.

Anaerobic methane oxidation can couple methane consumption to sulfate or other electron acceptors.

Carbon produced by methanotrophs can become extremely depleted in carbon-13 because the methane they consume is already isotopically light.

Some Archean rocks preserve such signals.

But assigning a 3.4-billion-year-old filament or carbon grain specifically to a methane oxidiser remains inferential.

This is why the Barberton filaments on page 158 were described as possible methane-cycling organisms rather than confirmed methanotrophs.

The ecological logic, however, is compelling.

Once biological methane production exists, a new energy-rich compound enters the environment.

Any organism capable of consuming that methane gains access to a resource made by another organism.

A new trophic link appears.

The producer's waste becomes the consumer's food.

Methane can therefore connect deep geochemical hydrogen, carbon dioxide, microbial metabolism, sediment chemistry and atmosphere.

A molecule produced in a pore can influence climate.

A gas produced by one metabolism can power another.

This is the scale jump that microbes repeatedly create.

Microscopic reactions become planetary processes.

But methane was only one branch of the anaerobic economy.

In a world rich in volcanic sulfur and poor in oxygen, sulfur offered an entire family of electron transfers.

And sulfur leaves some of the oldest metabolic fingerprints in the rock record.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Ueno et al. (2006), *Nature*: highly ^13C-depleted methane in Dresser fluid inclusions interpreted as microbial methanogenesis >3.46 Ga.
- Abiotic hydrothermal methane and revised environmental context mean the Dresser assignment is not treated as uniquely proven.
- 2020 Archean-life review: methanogenesis is strongly indicated by ~3.0 Ga and possibly as early as 3.48 Ga; evidence for methane oxidation is generally younger/more indirect.
- Modern methanogenesis is restricted to Archaea, but a geological methane signature is not used to identify a specific ancient archaeal lineage.

**Decision:** methane is reconstructed as an important probable early biosphere component while age-specific methanogen/methanotroph assignments retain uncertainty labels.
