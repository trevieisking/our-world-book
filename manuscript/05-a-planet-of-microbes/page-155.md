# CHAPTER FIVE — A PLANET OF MICROBES
## Page 155 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### THE DRESSER LANDSCAPE

The Dresser Formation lies in the Pilbara Craton of Western Australia.

Its rocks are about **3.48 billion years old**.

For decades, researchers described parts of the environment in different ways.

Deep marine.

Shallow marine.

Coastal.

Hydrothermal.

Subaerial hot spring.

The reason is not simply disagreement.

The landscape itself was complicated.

Modern field mapping and geochemistry increasingly describe the fossil-bearing sequence as part of a dynamic **volcanic–hydrothermal caldera system**.

Water from several sources moved through it.

Marine influence.

Hydrothermal fluids.

Subaerial or meteoric water.

Shallow pools and sedimentary surfaces.

Hot-spring deposits.

This is important because “the Dresser environment” was not one habitat.

It contained gradients.

The lowermost chert–barite succession records repeated interactions between volcanism, water, hydrothermal circulation and sedimentation.

Some beds formed under water.

Other deposits show evidence of exposure or very shallow conditions.

Geyserite — silica deposited around terrestrial hot springs and geysers — occurs in the sequence.

Stromatolites and microbial textures occur within this heterogeneous setting.

The result is a picture of life occupying a physically active landscape.

At shallow sedimentary surfaces, microbial mats influenced grains and built structures.

In hydrothermal settings, carbonaceous material and isotope patterns record different kinds of chemistry.

Hot-spring deposits preserve possible microbial fabrics in places exposed to sunlight.

This is not what we would expect from one fragile experiment in life barely hanging on in a single protected niche.

By 3.48 billion years ago, the evidence is more consistent with a biosphere able to occupy several related environments.

That does not mean every metre of the caldera was alive.

It does not establish how long life had existed.

It does not tell us whether life originated in a similar setting.

But it changes the minimum picture.

The earliest convincing biosphere was not necessarily restricted to a deep hydrothermal refuge.

Microbial communities could live at or near illuminated surfaces.

They could interact with sediments.

They could tolerate hydrothermal influence.

They could colonise a landscape where salinity, temperature and water source changed over space and time.

Trace-element studies published in 2023 strengthened this environmental reconstruction.

Rare-earth elements and yttrium in the Dresser succession record varying marine, hydrothermal and subaerial fluid influences through time.

That helps place biological structures into an evolving habitat rather than treating them as isolated fossils.

It also illustrates why ancient-life interpretation can change even when the fossils themselves do not.

A carbon isotope measurement means something different in a deep hydrothermal vein than in an illuminated shallow-water mat.

A structure formed around a hot spring has different ecological possibilities from one formed below the reach of sunlight.

Context determines which metabolisms are plausible.

And Dresser's context has become richer.

This gives us the next question.

Some of these microbial communities lived where light was available.

Were they already photosynthetic?

Probably some were phototrophic.

But that word requires care.

Using light does not automatically mean making oxygen.

The world at 3.48 billion years ago was still overwhelmingly anoxic.

So if sunlight powered these communities, the first photosynthetic ecosystems may have worked very differently from the green world we know.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Djokic et al. and subsequent field/geochemical work: Dresser fossil-bearing units formed within a dynamic volcanic–hydrothermal caldera influenced by marine, hydrothermal and subaerial fluids.
- 2023 trace-element (REE + Y) work refines the changing habitat reconstruction.
- 2025 early-biosphere review treats Dresser as preserving stromatolites, MISS and hot-spring-associated microbial evidence.

**Decision:** Dresser is reconstructed as a heterogeneous volcanic-hydrothermal landscape containing multiple habitats; no single depositional label is imposed on the entire formation.
