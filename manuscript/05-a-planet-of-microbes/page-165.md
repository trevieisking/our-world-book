# CHAPTER FIVE — A PLANET OF MICROBES
## Page 165 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### THE NITROGEN BOTTLENECK

Carbon builds the skeleton of a cell.

Nitrogen helps build the machinery.

Proteins require amino acids containing nitrogen.

RNA and DNA require nitrogen-rich bases.

Many cofactors contain it.

A biosphere cannot expand indefinitely without a supply of chemically usable nitrogen.

The atmosphere provides an enormous reservoir.

Most of it is N₂.

That creates a problem.

The two nitrogen atoms in N₂ are held together by one of the strongest common chemical bonds.

Most organisms cannot break it.

Modern ecosystems depend heavily on **nitrogen fixation**:

the conversion of atmospheric N₂ into ammonia, which can then be incorporated into biomass.

Biological nitrogen fixation is performed by nitrogenase enzymes.

They are complicated metal-containing molecular machines.

The best-known form contains molybdenum and iron.

Alternative nitrogenases use vanadium or iron.

All are energetically expensive.

Life spends large amounts of chemical energy to acquire nitrogen because nitrogen is indispensable.

Before biological fixation became widespread, lightning, ultraviolet chemistry and other abiotic processes could generate some fixed nitrogen.

Chapter Three showed why abundance and availability must be separated.

An atmosphere full of N₂ does not automatically feed a cell.

When did microbes evolve their own solution?

The oldest **strong** sedimentary isotope evidence currently reaches back to about **3.2 billion years ago**.

In 2015, Eva Stüeken and colleagues measured nitrogen isotope ratios in marine and fluvial sediments between roughly 3.2 and 2.75 billion years old.

The values were most readily explained by biological N₂ fixation.

The authors argued that the fractionation was consistent with a molybdenum-based nitrogenase.

That interpretation is important.

It is also still discussed.

Molecular evolutionary reconstructions of nitrogenase do not agree perfectly on when the modern molybdenum-specific machinery arose.

Molybdenum availability in ancient oceans creates another constraint.

An early nitrogenase may have handled metals differently from modern enzymes.

So the safest conclusion is:

**biological nitrogen fixation was operating by about 3.2 billion years ago.**

The exact architecture of the enzyme is less certain.

Can we push nitrogen biology back farther?

Palaeoarchaean rocks contain nitrogen associated with carbonaceous matter.

A 2026 review of Archean nitrogen cycling notes biological nitrogen burial back to at least about 3.7 billion years ago.

But metamorphism makes it difficult to reconstruct a specific nitrogen metabolism from those oldest rocks.

That is a crucial distinction.

Nitrogen inside ancient organic material shows that the biosphere contained nitrogen.

It does not automatically prove that the organisms fixed N₂ themselves.

Fixed nitrogen could have entered through abiotic chemistry and then been recycled.

If the early biosphere initially depended heavily on external fixed nitrogen, nitrogen availability may have limited productivity.

The evolution of biological nitrogen fixation would then have been a major ecological release.

Life could tap the atmosphere directly.

Primary producers no longer depended entirely on lightning, impacts or local geochemical sources to supply reduced nitrogen.

The biosphere could expand into environments previously starved of usable nitrogen.

And once nitrogen entered biomass, it could be recycled.

Cells die.

Proteins break down.

Ammonium is released.

Other microbes assimilate it.

Before the modern nitrification–denitrification cycle existed, an anaerobic nitrogen economy could still circulate nitrogen repeatedly through organic matter and reduced inorganic forms.

This introduces another principle that makes microbial ecosystems powerful.

One organism does not have to perform every difficult reaction.

A community can divide the labour.

One species fixes carbon.

Another fixes nitrogen.

Another consumes fermentation products.

Another removes hydrogen.

Another transforms sulfur.

Metabolism becomes ecological.

The waste of one cell becomes the thermodynamic opportunity of another.

That relationship has a name.

**Syntrophy.**

And in an oxygen-free world, it can make otherwise difficult chemistry possible.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Stüeken et al. (2015), *Nature*: nitrogen isotopes in ~3.2–2.75 Ga sediments most readily explained by biological N₂ fixation; authors proposed Mo-nitrogenase.
- Garcia et al. (2020), *Geobiology*: evolutionary history/metal specificity of ancient nitrogenases remains debated.
- 2026 *The Archean Earth* review: biological nitrogen burial is suggested from at least ~3.7 Ga, but Palaeoarchaean metamorphic overprint prevents confident assignment of specific N metabolisms.
- Abiotic fixed-N sources existed and may have supplied early ecosystems.

**Decision:** secure nitrogen fixation evidence is anchored at ~3.2 Ga; older nitrogen burial is not promoted into pathway-specific proof.
