# CHAPTER FIVE — A PLANET OF MICROBES
## Page 172 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### LIFE MOVES ONTO LAND

Land before plants did not look green.

There were no roots holding soil.

No moss.

No forests.

No insects crawling through leaf litter.

Rock met air directly.

Rain ran across mineral surfaces.

Rivers carried sand and gravel through barren landscapes.

Yet by about **3.22 billion years ago**, some of those surfaces were already biological.

The evidence comes from the **Moodies Group** in the Barberton Greenstone Belt of South Africa.

In 2018, Martin Homann and colleagues described fossil microbial mats draped across fluvial conglomerates and gravelly sandstones.

These were not marine rocks misidentified as land.

Field relationships, sedimentary structures and basin reconstruction showed river-dominated terrestrial deposition.

The carbonaceous layers formed with the sediment.

Raman spectroscopy, petrography and carbon-isotope measurements supported their biological origin.

The result provided direct fossil evidence for microbial communities living on land 3.22 billion years ago.

This was a different habitat from Dresser's shallow hydrothermal settings or Strelley Pool's coastal carbonate platform.

Fresh water flowed across exposed continental crust.

Microbial films colonised sand and gravel.

Some mats survived burial and silicification.

The terrestrial and nearby marine mats also differed isotopically.

That suggests their microbial communities or carbon/nitrogen cycling were not chemically identical.

The researchers interpreted nitrogen isotope differences as possible evidence for denitrification of an atmospheric nitrate source in the terrestrial setting.

That metabolic interpretation is more uncertain than the existence of the mats themselves.

But the ecological conclusion is strong.

By 3.22 billion years ago, life occupied **land as well as water**.

This expansion matters because land provides chemical opportunities unavailable in the open ocean.

Wet–dry cycling.

Freshwater chemistry.

Direct atmospheric exchange.

Intense ultraviolet light.

Unweathered mineral surfaces.

Volcanic ash.

Rapid temperature changes.

Rivers connect these environments to the sea.

Chapter Four discussed such settings as candidate reactors for prebiotic chemistry.

Chapter Five now shows that, whatever role land played in life's origin, microbes had colonised terrestrial environments early in the surviving record.

Living on land also changes weathering.

A microbial mat holds moisture against rock.

Organic acids and carbon dioxide alter mineral dissolution.

Sticky extracellular material traps fine grains.

Chemical gradients develop beneath the mat.

Even without roots, biology begins affecting the interface between atmosphere and crust.

We should not exaggerate the global effect.

The preserved Moodies mats prove local terrestrial ecosystems.

They do not prove that every Archean continent was carpeted in microbes.

Exposed land itself was distributed unevenly.

Ultraviolet radiation remained intense.

Fresh water could be episodic.

But once life can occupy rivers, tidal flats, hot springs, shallow seas and subsurface fractures, the concept of a single microbial habitat becomes obsolete.

The biosphere has geographic range.

It can move between environments.

It can evolve separately in different chemical settings.

And every new habitat creates new selective pressures.

By 3.2 billion years ago, life's expansion was no longer only a story told by chemistry.

It was visible in landscapes.

That expansion also changes the oxygen question.

A photosynthetic organism on a shallow platform or terrestrial mat places any oxygen it produces directly against water, sediment and atmosphere.

If oxygenic photosynthesis had already evolved, local oxygen-rich microenvironments could exist long before the atmosphere became globally oxygenated.

The challenge is proving when that began.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Homann et al. (2018), *Nature Geoscience*: direct fossil evidence for terrestrial microbial mats in fluvial rocks of the 3.22 Ga Moodies Group.
- Field, petrographic, Raman and isotopic evidence support synsedimentary biogenic mats and a terrestrial habitat.
- Nitrogen-isotope interpretation involving denitrification is retained as an interpretation rather than required for the terrestrial-life conclusion.
- Local terrestrial colonisation is not extrapolated to complete global land coverage.

**Decision:** by 3.22 Ga, direct evidence supports microbial life on land, expanding the documented biosphere beyond marine/hydrothermal settings.
