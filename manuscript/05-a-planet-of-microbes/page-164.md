# CHAPTER FIVE — A PLANET OF MICROBES
## Page 164 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### AN OCEAN FULL OF IRON

Pour dissolved iron into oxygenated seawater today and much of it will not remain dissolved for long.

Ferrous iron, Fe²⁺, is oxidised toward ferric iron, Fe³⁺.

Iron minerals precipitate.

The modern oxygen-rich surface ocean therefore carries very little dissolved ferrous iron.

The Archean ocean was different.

With little free oxygen, Fe²⁺ supplied by hydrothermal systems and weathering could persist at far higher concentrations.

That created a planetary reservoir of reduced iron.

Microbes can exploit the difference between Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺.

Some modern organisms reduce ferric iron while oxidising organic matter or hydrogen.

Others oxidise ferrous iron.

Some iron oxidation can be driven by light without producing oxygen.

This metabolism is called **photoferrotrophy**.

A phototrophic organism uses light for energy and Fe²⁺ as an electron donor.

Ferric iron is produced and can precipitate as minerals.

Because ancient oceans were ferruginous, photoferrotrophy has often been proposed as an important form of early primary production.

It could link sunlight directly to the massive iron reservoir in seawater.

Iron isotope patterns and ancient iron-rich sediments are compatible with biological iron cycling in the Archean.

Some studies have suggested Fe²⁺ oxidation by anoxygenic phototrophs as early as roughly 3.5 billion years ago.

But this claim requires caution.

Iron can be oxidised without biology.

Ultraviolet light can contribute.

Mineral reactions can fractionate iron isotopes.

Hydrothermal mixing changes iron chemistry.

And the evolutionary history of modern photoferrotrophy remains debated.

A 2025 review of the earliest biosphere specifically notes the possibility that photoferrotrophy could be a later adaptation in some phototrophic lineages rather than an ancestral universal state.

So we should not paint every early ocean green with iron-oxidising phototrophs.

The stronger statement is functional.

The Fe²⁺-rich ocean made **iron metabolism chemically available**.

By the early Archean, isotopic and sedimentological evidence is consistent with biological iron transformations in at least some settings.

The 2026 Isua study adds an intriguing older signal.

Carbon-isotope patterns varied with iron content in 3.7-billion-year-old sediments.

The authors interpreted the relationship as organic-matter respiration coupled to iron reduction.

That would make iron not merely a donor for surface phototrophs but an electron acceptor for organisms consuming buried organic carbon.

Again, this is an inference from geochemical patterns in metamorphosed rocks.

It is not a fossilised iron-reducing cell.

But the ecological possibility is important.

Iron can connect primary production and decomposition.

A surface organism fixes carbon.

Organic matter settles.

Another organism oxidises that organic matter while reducing ferric minerals.

Hydrothermal and sedimentary processes recycle iron.

Different redox zones emerge.

Later in Earth's history, iron chemistry will leave one of the most spectacular geological records on the planet:

banded iron formations.

Their origin involves interactions among seawater chemistry, iron sources, oxidation processes and biology.

We will return to them when oxygen becomes central.

For now, iron belongs in the pre-oxygen microbial economy as both resource and recorder.

It gave microorganisms another way to move electrons.

But no microbial economy can grow indefinitely on carbon, sulfur and iron alone.

Cells need nitrogen.

And the atmosphere held an enormous reservoir that was frustratingly difficult to use.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Archean surface oceans were widely ferruginous/anoxic relative to today, allowing much higher dissolved Fe²⁺.
- Biological iron oxidation/reduction are plausible ancient metabolisms; Fe-isotope and sedimentary evidence have been interpreted in terms of early biological iron cycling.
- Timing and ancestry of photoferrotrophy remain debated; it is not assigned universally to the earliest phototrophs.
- 2026 Isua study interprets coupled carbon-isotope/iron patterns as organic-matter respiration linked to iron reduction.

**Decision:** iron cycling is reconstructed as a plausible and increasingly supported component of early ecosystems, while specific photoferrotrophic timing remains an active question.
