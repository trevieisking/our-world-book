# CHAPTER FIVE — A PLANET OF MICROBES
## Page 163 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### SULFUR WAS AN ANAEROBIC CURRENCY

Sulfur is chemically versatile.

It can exist in several oxidation states.

That means electrons can move into and out of sulfur compounds.

Where electrons can move, organisms can often harvest energy.

Modern microbes demonstrate an entire sulfur economy.

Some oxidise hydrogen sulfide or elemental sulfur.

Some reduce sulfate to sulfide.

Some reduce elemental sulfur.

Some **disproportionate** intermediate sulfur compounds, splitting one sulfur compound into both more oxidised and more reduced products.

None of these metabolisms requires oxygen in the way animals do.

Some thrive only when oxygen is absent.

Early Earth offered abundant sulfur from volcanism and hydrothermal activity.

But the sulfur ocean differed greatly from today's.

Chapter Three showed that Archean seawater probably contained much less sulfate than the modern ocean.

Modern sulfate-reducing ecosystems therefore cannot simply be projected backward at modern rates and concentrations.

Atmospheric photochemistry and volcanic sulfur could still generate oxidised sulfur compounds locally.

Hydrothermal systems supplied reduced sulfur.

Interfaces between those reservoirs created opportunities.

The Dresser Formation preserves some of the oldest evidence that microorganisms exploited them.

Microscopic pyrite occurs with barite and other sulfur-bearing minerals.

Sulfur isotope ratios vary in ways that researchers have interpreted as biological fractionation.

Early studies argued for microbial sulfate reduction.

Later multiple-isotope work showed that elemental-sulfur cycling or disproportionation could also explain parts of the record.

A 2024 synthesis of the Dresser environment lists microbial cycling of elemental sulfur and/or microbial sulfate reduction among the viable interpretations.

This is a useful example of science becoming more precise without making the evidence disappear.

The broad conclusion survives:

**microorganisms were altering sulfur chemistry.**

The exact pathway is less certain.

At Isua, the 2026 study goes farther back.

Sulfide grains in roughly 3.7-billion-year-old marine sediments preserve isotope patterns interpreted as reduction of atmospherically produced elemental sulfur, with possible minor sulfate reduction during sedimentary pauses.

If the interpretation survives further testing, sulfur respiration was already part of an Eoarchaean ecosystem.

Why does sulfur matter ecologically?

Because it creates links.

A phototroph can oxidise sulfide while fixing carbon.

A decomposer can release reduced sulfur from organic matter.

A sulfur reducer can consume organic compounds or hydrogen.

A disproportionating organism can exploit intermediate sulfur species produced by atmospheric or volcanic chemistry.

Minerals precipitate when the products meet iron.

Each reaction changes the substrate available to the next organism.

This is the basic architecture of a microbial cycle.

Geology supplies sulfur in one form.

Biology transforms it.

Chemistry turns some products into minerals.

Burial preserves a fraction of the isotope pattern.

Billions of years later, we reconstruct the metabolism from the imbalance left behind.

The early sulfur cycle also reminds us that an anaerobic world is not an energy-poor world.

Oxygen is an exceptionally powerful electron acceptor.

It is not the only one.

Before oxygen became abundant, microorganisms could build ecosystems from smaller energetic differences.

Hydrogen.

Carbon dioxide.

Sulfur.

Organic molecules.

And iron.

Iron was everywhere in the anoxic ocean in a chemical form almost absent from oxygenated surface seawater today.

For early microbes, that made the ocean itself a reservoir of electrons.

## Research / fact-check notes
- Dresser multiple-sulfur-isotope studies have been interpreted as microbial sulfate reduction and/or elemental-sulfur cycling/disproportionation.
- Early Archean sulfate concentrations were much lower than modern seawater; local oxidised sulfur could still be supplied by atmospheric/volcanic processes.
- 2026 Isua work interprets sulfide isotope patterns as reduction of atmospherically produced elemental sulfur with possible minor sulfate reduction.

**Decision:** sulfur cycling is strongly supported as ancient; exact Dresser pathway labels remain open where isotope data permit multiple microbial interpretations.
