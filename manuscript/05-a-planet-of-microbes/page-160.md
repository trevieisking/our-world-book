# CHAPTER FIVE — A PLANET OF MICROBES
## Page 160 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### BY 3.5 BILLION YEARS AGO, THIS WAS ALREADY A BIOSPHERE

Stand back from the individual claims.

The pattern matters.

At Dresser, roughly 3.48 billion years ago, microbial communities influenced shallow sediments.

They built stromatolitic structures.

They formed microbial mats.

They lived in a landscape affected by volcanism, hydrothermal circulation, marine water and subaerial processes.

Some communities occupied illuminated environments.

Carbon and sulfur chemistry record additional biological activity.

In Barberton, by about 3.42 billion years ago, putative microbial filaments occur inside an ancient subseafloor hydrothermal system.

If the interpretation is correct, methane-linked microorganisms occupied fractures below the seafloor.

Other Palaeoarchaean rocks preserve mat structures and stromatolites across different sedimentary environments.

At Isua, more than 3.7 billion years ago, altered sedimentary graphite increasingly supports the presence of an older biosphere.

The latest isotope work is consistent with interacting anaerobic metabolisms in those sediments.

The confidence is not equal at every site.

It does not have to be.

A scientific picture can be built from evidence with different strengths as long as the labels remain attached.

What emerges by the Palaeoarchaean is not one organism.

It is **ecology**.

Life at the surface.

Life beneath it.

Communities structured by light.

Communities structured by chemical gradients.

Carbon fixation.

Organic-matter recycling.

Sulfur transformations.

Possible methane production and consumption.

Probable use of iron chemistry in some settings.

The beginnings — or already the continuation — of element cycles increasingly influenced by biology.

This is why the earliest convincing fossils cannot date the origin of life.

A biosphere diverse enough to occupy several habitats and exploit different energy sources has already undergone evolution.

Metabolisms have changed.

Lineages have diverged.

Communities have assembled.

Ecological interactions have appeared.

The first replicator, whatever it was, lies behind all of this.

Molecular-clock estimates that place the last universal common ancestor much earlier become less startling in that context, although the dates remain model-dependent.

LUCA itself, as Chapter Four stressed, was not the first life.

And the organisms recorded in Dresser were not necessarily direct portraits of LUCA.

They were members of a world that had already moved on.

There is another important consequence.

The early biosphere was **anaerobic**.

Free oxygen was not yet abundant in the atmosphere.

That means the metabolic economy of these communities cannot be understood by imagining modern surface ecosystems with the oxygen removed.

Many modern pathways that dominate our world depend on oxygen.

Early organisms had to use other electron donors and acceptors.

Hydrogen.

Carbon dioxide.

Sulfur compounds.

Ferrous iron.

Organic molecules.

Light without necessarily producing oxygen.

To reconstruct that world, we need to understand what microbes can do when oxygen is scarce or absent.

That is the next step in this chapter.

Not a catalogue of species.

We do not know their species.

Not a modern family tree projected backward.

The ancient record is too incomplete for that.

Instead we will reconstruct the **jobs** in the ecosystem.

Who made organic carbon?

Who consumed it?

Who made methane?

Who oxidised methane?

Who cycled sulfur?

Who used iron?

How did nitrogen become biologically available?

How did microbial mats organise these reactions across millimetres of sediment?

The first great ecosystems on Earth were chemical economies.

Every organism lived by moving electrons.

Every waste product was a potential resource.

Every gradient created a niche.

For billions of years, microbes would control Earth's living world.

And eventually, one microbial innovation would alter the chemistry of the entire planet.

Oxygenic photosynthesis.

We are not there yet.

First, we have to understand the world before oxygen.

## Research / fact-check notes
- 2025 early-biosphere review synthesises widespread Palaeoarchaean biosignatures and evidence for ecological/metabolic diversification by ~3.48 Ga.
- Dresser, Barberton and Isua carry different confidence levels; they are not collapsed into equal-certainty evidence.
- The early atmosphere/ocean surface system was broadly anoxic relative to the later oxygenated Earth.
- Specific taxonomic identities are not reconstructed where rocks preserve only ecological/metabolic proxies.

**Decision:** by ~3.5 Ga the evidence supports an established, habitat-diverse microbial biosphere. The next block will reconstruct anaerobic metabolic roles without over-assigning specific lineages.
