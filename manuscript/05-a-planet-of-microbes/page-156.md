# CHAPTER FIVE — A PLANET OF MICROBES
## Page 156 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### LIGHT BEFORE OXYGEN

Photosynthesis is easy to associate with oxygen.

Plants release it.

Algae release it.

Cyanobacteria release it.

But photosynthesis did not have to begin that way.

At its most general, **phototrophy** means using light as an energy source.

An organism can harvest photons without splitting water and releasing oxygen.

Modern anoxygenic phototrophs demonstrate several alternatives.

Some use hydrogen sulfide as an electron donor.

Some can use hydrogen.

Some oxidise ferrous iron.

Their pigments and reaction centres capture light while their chemistry remains compatible with an oxygen-poor world.

This matters when we look at the oldest microbial mats.

The 3.48-billion-year-old Dresser Formation preserves MISS and stromatolitic structures in shallow environments where light would have been available.

A 2025 review of the earliest biosphere interprets these, along with comparable Palaeoarchaean structures, as evidence for phototrophic microbial communities.

Similar mat structures occur in the roughly 3.47-billion-year-old Middle Marker Chert of the Barberton Greenstone Belt in South Africa.

By about 3.4 billion years ago, the Strelley Pool Chert in Western Australia preserves an especially diverse set of stromatolites across a carbonate platform.

These structures make ecological sense if microbial growth responded to light and sedimentary conditions.

But we must separate three claims.

**Claim one: microbes built mats in illuminated environments.**

Strongly supported.

**Claim two: at least some of those microbes used light metabolically.**

Strongly plausible and supported by the morphology and environmental distribution interpreted in the broader geological record.

**Claim three: they performed oxygenic photosynthesis like cyanobacteria.**

Not established by these structures alone.

This distinction matters because oxygenic photosynthesis transforms a planet.

Splitting water releases molecular oxygen.

Enough biological oxygen eventually changes atmospheric chemistry, ocean chemistry, mineral weathering and the possibilities available to life.

Those changes become central in Chapter Six.

The Dresser mats do not require us to move that revolution back to 3.48 billion years ago.

Anoxygenic organisms can build mats.

They can produce stromatolitic structures.

They can thrive in shallow water.

And an anoxic atmosphere does not prevent them from harvesting sunlight.

Even bubbles preserved in ancient hot-spring microbial fabrics require caution.

In modern photosynthetic mats, gas bubbles can contain oxygen.

In an ancient hydrothermal setting, gas can also be supplied geologically.

A bubble-shaped void is not an oxygen measurement.

So the earliest evidence for phototrophy is a story about **energy capture before atmospheric transformation**.

Light created a vast energy source at Earth's surface.

Once organisms evolved pigments and reaction centres capable of using it, biological productivity no longer had to depend only on relatively limited geochemical fuels such as hydrogen and reduced sulfur.

That transition could expand the biosphere dramatically.

Exactly when different photosynthetic mechanisms appeared remains debated.

The oldest rocks rarely preserve the pigments or genes we would most like to inspect.

Instead we infer from environments, community structures, isotope chemistry and the later evolutionary record.

Still, the broad implication is powerful.

By the Palaeoarchaean, microbial ecosystems were not merely surviving Earth's chemistry.

They were learning to tap one of the planet's largest continuous energy flows.

Sunlight.

The next revolution would come when one lineage found an electron donor available almost everywhere:

water.

But before oxygen filled the story, early ecosystems were already cycling other elements.

Carbon.

Methane.

Sulfur.

Iron.

Nitrogen.

The invisible planet was becoming a biochemical network.

## Research / fact-check notes
- 2025 *Philosophical Transactions B* review: phototrophic mats/MISS and stromatolites occur in the ca. 3.48 Ga Dresser Formation and ~3.47 Ga Barberton record.
- Anoxygenic phototrophy can use electron donors other than water and does not release O₂.
- Dresser mat/stromatolite evidence does not by itself establish oxygenic photosynthesis or cyanobacteria at 3.48 Ga.

**Decision:** phototrophy is distinguished strictly from oxygenic photosynthesis. Chapter 6 retains the oxygen revolution rather than back-projecting it from early stromatolite morphology.
