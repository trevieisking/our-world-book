# CHAPTER FIVE — A PLANET OF MICROBES
## Page 168 — Controlled manuscript draft v0.1

### MICROBES BEGIN TO RUN THE PLANET

A biosphere becomes planetary gradually.

One cell changes almost nothing.

A population changes its immediate surroundings.

A mat changes sediment and pore water.

Millions of mats change element fluxes through a basin.

A metabolism repeated across oceans can alter the atmosphere.

By the Archean, microbes were beginning that escalation.

Carbon fixation pulled inorganic carbon into biomass.

Decay and fermentation returned part of it.

Burial preserved another fraction in sediment.

Methanogens may have converted carbon dioxide, hydrogen and simple organics into methane.

Methane could escape into the atmosphere and affect climate.

Sulfur metabolisms moved sulfur between oxidation states.

Their products reacted with iron and became minerals.

Iron metabolisms may have helped redistribute the enormous ferrous-iron reservoir of anoxic oceans.

Nitrogen fixation, securely evident by about 3.2 billion years ago, eventually gave ecosystems direct access to atmospheric N₂.

Before then, fixed nitrogen was recycled through biomass and supplemented by abiotic sources.

None of these cycles was independent.

Carbon fixation needs nitrogen.

Sulfur reduction can consume organic carbon.

Methane production competes with or depends on other anaerobic reactions.

Iron reduction can consume buried organic matter.

Mineral precipitation removes substances from solution.

Burial changes what remains available.

Weathering returns new material.

The biosphere inserts feedback loops into geology.

This is where the word **ecosystem** becomes insufficient if we imagine only organisms interacting with organisms.

Early microbial life interacted with a planet.

Rock supplied electron donors.

Water transported nutrients.

Atmospheric photochemistry supplied reactive compounds.

Microbes transformed them.

Sediments buried the products.

Volcanism returned gases and metals.

The boundaries between biology and geology became chemically porous.

Yet one feature of modern Earth was still largely missing.

Free molecular oxygen.

Without abundant O₂, many high-energy metabolic pathways available to modern organisms were unavailable.

Iron stayed dissolved in seawater.

Reduced sulfur compounds persisted.

Methane survived longer.

Ultraviolet radiation reached the surface without an ozone shield.

The redox structure of the planet was profoundly different.

The early biosphere did not merely tolerate that world.

It evolved inside it.

For hundreds of millions of years, perhaps much longer, anaerobic metabolisms were normal metabolism.

Oxygen was not the universal solution.

It was eventually a disruptive biological innovation.

Before we reach that revolution, however, the fossil record lets us watch microbial ecosystems become more visibly established.

The Dresser Formation gives us one of the earliest strong snapshots.

Younger Palaeoarchaean terrains preserve increasingly diverse stromatolites.

Microfossils become better preserved in some cherts.

Carbonaceous mats appear in different habitats.

By the Mesoarchaean, nitrogen fixation leaves clearer isotope evidence.

Methane cycling becomes easier to recognise.

The geological record becomes less fragmentary, although never complete.

The final pages of this chapter will follow that strengthening record.

We will look at how stromatolites diversified.

How genuine microfossils are distinguished from mineral mimics.

How microbial communities spread through shallow seas, lakes and hydrothermal settings.

And how the metabolic innovations of an invisible biosphere prepared Earth for the most consequential waste product in planetary history.

Oxygen.

The microbes had already transformed sediments.

Soon, they would transform the sky.

## Research / fact-check notes
- This page synthesises documented Archean carbon, methane, sulfur, iron and nitrogen-cycle evidence while maintaining their different age/confidence levels.
- Biological nitrogen fixation is anchored securely at ~3.2 Ga rather than assumed for Dresser.
- Methanogenesis at ~3.48 Ga remains possible/strongly argued but not equivalent in confidence to all other Palaeoarchaean biosignatures.
- Global magnitudes of individual early-Archean biological fluxes remain uncertain.

**Decision:** microbial biogeochemical feedbacks are established qualitatively; global quantitative dominance is not claimed where flux constraints are weak.

**Transition:** pages 169–176 will strengthen the fossil record through younger Archean stromatolites/microfossils and close Chapter 5 at the threshold of oxygenic photosynthesis and planetary oxygenation.
