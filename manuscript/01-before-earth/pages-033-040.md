## PAGE 033 — HOW OLD IS "BEFORE"?

Before Earth existed, there was already history.

A great deal of it.

The Universe is about **13.8 billion years old**. Earth formed roughly **4.54 billion years ago**. That means more than nine billion years of cosmic history had already passed before our planet assembled.

No ocean.

No Moon.

No Sun as we know it yet.

No Earth.

But not nothing.

The phrase **Big Bang** is often imagined as an explosion happening at one point inside empty space. That picture is misleading. In modern cosmology, the early Universe was an extremely hot, dense state in which space itself was expanding. The model describes the evolution of the Universe from that early state; it does not give us a confirmed description of an earlier "outside" into which the Universe exploded.

That is the first boundary to keep clear.

Cosmology can reconstruct astonishingly early stages.

It cannot yet provide a tested account of an absolute beginning beyond every known physical description.

NASA's current summary places the age of the Universe at about **13.8 billion years**. That figure does not come from one ancient object carrying a cosmic date stamp. It is inferred from several linked observations and physical models: the expansion of the Universe, the cosmic microwave background, the abundances of light elements, the ages of the oldest stars, and the geometry and contents of the cosmos.

The age is therefore a model-constrained measurement.

Very good.

Not metaphysical omniscience.

In the earliest moments accessible to our physical models, the Universe was far hotter and denser than it is today. As it expanded, it cooled.

Within the first few minutes, protons and neutrons combined in a period called **Big Bang nucleosynthesis**. Most of the ordinary matter available for later stars was left as hydrogen and helium, with only small traces of a few light nuclei such as lithium.

There were no mountains.

There were no rocks.

There was not yet enough chemical variety to make a grain of sand.

Even atoms in the familiar sense could not immediately survive. The Universe was too hot for electrons to remain bound to nuclei for long.

Expansion continued.

Cooling continued.

And around **380,000 years** after the Big Bang, conditions changed enough for electrons and nuclei to combine into neutral atoms in large numbers.

Then something extraordinary happened.

The Universe became transparent.

For the first time, light could travel across enormous distances without being repeatedly scattered by free electrons.

That ancient light still reaches us.

And we can measure it.

## PAGE 034 — THE OLDEST LIGHT WE CAN SEE

If we could travel backward through the history of the Universe using light alone, eventually we would hit a wall.

Not a solid wall.

A glowing one.

Before neutral atoms formed, the early Universe contained so many free charged particles that photons were continually scattered. It was opaque, rather like trying to see through a dense luminous fog.

Around **380,000 years after the Big Bang**, expansion had cooled the Universe enough for electrons to remain bound to nuclei. Free electrons became much less abundant.

The fog cleared.

The light released from that era has been travelling through expanding space ever since. Expansion stretched its wavelengths enormously, so today we detect it primarily as microwaves.

This is the **cosmic microwave background**, or CMB.

It is the oldest light we can directly observe.

"Oldest light" does not mean the photons have remained untouched in a cosmic cupboard for 13.8 billion years. They have crossed an evolving Universe. Their wavelengths have been stretched. Their path has passed through large-scale structure. Modern instruments measure the faint radiation arriving from every direction and reconstruct tiny variations in its temperature.

Those variations are tiny.

They are also enormously important.

The early Universe was remarkably smooth, but not perfectly smooth. Slight differences in density existed. Over cosmic time, gravity amplified those differences. Regions containing a little more matter attracted more matter. Eventually, the seeds visible in the CMB contributed to the growth of the cosmic web: galaxies, groups and clusters separated by enormous voids.

This is one reason cosmology is more than a story invented backward from the modern sky.

The model makes connections between observations made at completely different epochs.

A pattern measured in ancient microwave radiation must be consistent with the large-scale distribution of matter seen billions of years later.

The CMB also helps constrain the age and composition of the Universe when interpreted through modern cosmological models.

It is not a photograph of the Big Bang itself.

It is a picture of the Universe when it was already about 380,000 years old.

That is extremely early by cosmic standards.

It is also much later than the first few minutes in which the lightest nuclei formed.

And after the CMB was released, the Universe entered a period with no stars shining in it.

Hydrogen and helium existed.

Dark matter was gravitating.

Structure was growing.

But the stars had not switched on.

Astronomers call this interval the **cosmic dark ages**.

The name is unusually literal.

For a while, the Universe really was waiting for its first stars.

## PAGE 035 — WHEN DARKNESS LEARNED TO SHINE

We do not yet know the exact moment when the first star ignited.

That sentence deserves to remain exactly as it is.

The first stars have not been individually observed and identified beyond reasonable doubt.

Physics nevertheless gives us boundaries.

They must have formed **after** the Universe cooled enough for neutral atoms to exist.

And they must have formed **before** the earliest galaxies we can observe, because galaxies already contain stars.

NASA describes the first-star era as occurring sometime during the few hundred million years between recombination and the appearance of the earliest known galaxies.

The first generation is usually called **Population III**, although the name is historical rather than chronological. These stars would have formed from gas made overwhelmingly of hydrogen and helium, with essentially none of the heavier elements that later generations inherited.

Computer models suggest that the chemistry of this nearly metal-free gas may have favoured stars unlike the typical stars forming around us today, perhaps including many very massive stars.

That is a model-dependent expectation.

The exact mass distribution of the first stellar generation remains uncertain because we have not yet observed a clean surviving Population III star.

But stars began to shine.

Their ultraviolet radiation changed surrounding gas.

Their gravity gathered matter.

Their deaths began enriching space with newly made chemical elements.

And structures grew.

James Webb has now observed galaxies astonishingly close to this era. As of 2026, the spectroscopically confirmed galaxy **MoM-z14** is seen as it existed only about **280 million years after the Big Bang**.

That is not "280 million light-years away".

It is a statement about **cosmic time**: the light we detect left that galaxy when the Universe itself was only about 280 million years old.

This is one of astronomy's strangest gifts.

Distance becomes history.

Look far enough away and you do not see an object as it is now.

You see it as it was when its light began travelling toward us.

The farther away the object, the farther back in time we are looking.

This does not allow us to visit the past.

It allows old light to visit us.

And that distinction lets telescopes become historical instruments.

Webb has shown that surprisingly substantial galaxies already existed within the first few hundred million years. Exactly how galaxies assembled so quickly is still an active area of research.

The early Universe was not a finished modern cosmos populated by neat spiral galaxies.

It was a construction site.

Gas collapsed.

Stars formed.

Small structures merged.

Black holes grew.

Galaxies changed shape and mass.

The first lights did not switch on all at once.

Cosmic dawn was a process.

And it lasted a very long time by human standards.

## PAGE 036 — THE COSMIC FAST-FORWARD

There is a wonderfully tempting way to describe astronomy.

We point telescopes at the sky and watch the Universe grow up.

That is almost right.

But not literally.

Human astronomy has not existed for billions of years, so we cannot sit beside one newborn star and record a four-million-year documentary from beginning to end.

Instead, we do something cleverer.

We observe **many different systems at different stages of development**.

Nearby molecular clouds contain dense clumps beginning to collapse.

Other regions contain protostars.

Some young stars are surrounded by thick disks of gas and dust.

Older young stars have thinner disks.

Some disks show rings, gaps and spirals.

Mature stars host finished planetary systems.

Dead stars preserve the final stages of stellar evolution.

Astronomers combine these snapshots with physics, chemistry, orbital mechanics, stellar models and statistical populations to reconstruct an evolutionary sequence.

It is a cosmic version of walking into a city and seeing babies, children, adults and elderly people all on the same day.

You have not watched one person age for ninety years.

But you can still learn a great deal about the human life cycle.

Astronomy adds another advantage.

Because light takes time to travel, distant objects are genuinely seen at earlier cosmic epochs.

A galaxy one billion light-years away is not being shown to us "live" in the everyday sense.

The light arriving now left long ago.

At the greatest distances, cosmic expansion makes the distance language more complicated than simply equating light-years with years, but the principle remains: **lookback time is real**.

This means telescopes provide two complementary histories.

For nearby star-forming regions, we compare many objects at different developmental stages.

For the deep Universe, we also observe genuinely different eras because the light has travelled for billions of years.

Together, those methods can look remarkably like a fast-forward film of cosmic evolution.

But the film has been assembled from many cameras pointed at many places at many times.

Astronomy does not own a 13.8-billion-year GoPro.

It has something better: independent objects obeying the same physical laws, observed across an enormous range of ages and distances.

That is why we can test star-formation models.

A theory has to explain protostars, disks, stellar clusters, mature stars and remnants.

It has to produce the right temperatures, luminosities, spectra, masses and timescales.

And when observations refuse to cooperate, the model has to change.

The sky is not illustrating our theories for us.

It is marking them.

## PAGE 037 — A GALAXY DOES NOT HAVE ONE BIRTHDAY

It is natural to ask:

**How old is the Milky Way?**

The answer is not quite like the age of Earth.

Earth assembled over time too, but geochemists can meaningfully estimate a formation age for the Solar System and planet.

A galaxy is even less cooperative.

Galaxies grow by forming stars from gas, accreting fresh material and merging with other galaxies. Their disks, halos, bulges and stellar populations can form at different times.

So a galaxy does not necessarily have one exact birthday.

It has a **formation history**.

ESA's Gaia mission has transformed our ability to reconstruct ours.

Gaia measures positions, motions and other properties for enormous numbers of stars. When those data are combined with stellar ages and chemical abundances, astronomers can identify populations that share a common history.

One major analysis found that part of the Milky Way's **thick disk began forming about 13 billion years ago**, only around 800 million years after the Big Bang.

Other ancient structures identified with Gaia contain stars roughly **12 to 13 billion years old**.

And the Galaxy kept changing.

The Milky Way grew partly by forming stars from its own gas and partly by merging with smaller systems.

The event known as the **Gaia–Sausage–Enceladus merger** contributed large numbers of stars to the halo roughly ten billion years ago.

New work continues to push parts of the merger history deeper into the Galaxy's past.

The thin disk containing the Sun developed later.

The spiral structure we see today is therefore not a frozen relic from one birth moment.

It is the latest visible state of a system that has been building itself for most of cosmic history.

This is a useful correction to the phrase "the age of a galaxy".

We can date ancient stars.

We can estimate when particular components formed.

We can reconstruct mergers.

We can model when star formation accelerated or declined.

We can observe distant galaxies at earlier developmental stages.

But saying "the Milky Way is exactly X years old" can hide the fact that the object did not appear fully assembled on one cosmic Tuesday morning.

Galaxies have biographies.

And ours began long before the Sun.

That matters for the history of Earth because the Sun formed from material that had already passed through previous generations of cosmic evolution.

By the time our Solar System appeared, the Milky Way was ancient.

The raw ingredients of Earth had history before Earth had geography.

## PAGE 038 — HOW A STAR IS BORN

Stars are still forming today.

That matters enormously.

It means star formation is not an event we infer only from unreachable cosmic antiquity. We can study it in nearby regions of our own Galaxy.

Stars begin inside cold **molecular clouds** containing gas and dust.

These clouds can contain thousands to millions of times the Sun's mass and extend across enormous regions of space.

They are not perfectly uniform.

Turbulence, gravity, magnetic fields, radiation and interactions between structures create dense regions.

If a sufficiently dense clump becomes gravitationally unstable, gravity begins to win.

Material collapses inward.

As the cloud contracts, gravitational energy is converted into heat.

A dense central object develops: a **protostar**.

But collapse does not usually send every particle directly into the centre.

The original cloud has angular momentum.

As material contracts, rotation becomes increasingly important. Gas and dust flatten into a disk around the growing protostar.

Some material falls inward through the disk.

Some can be expelled in narrow jets and broader outflows.

The system is messy, dynamic and often deeply embedded in dust.

That dust is precisely why different telescopes are useful.

Visible light may be blocked.

Infrared observations can penetrate much of the obscuring material and reveal warm young stars.

Radio and millimetre telescopes can map cold gas and dust and identify molecules moving within the cloud and disk.

Eventually, the centre of the protostar becomes hot and dense enough for sustained **hydrogen fusion**.

At that point, the star enters the long phase of its life during which nuclear fusion provides the pressure and energy needed to oppose gravitational collapse.

The exact route depends strongly on stellar mass.

A small red dwarf and a massive blue star do not live the same kind of life.

Mass sets the pace.

High-mass stars consume nuclear fuel much faster and can live only millions of years.

Low-mass stars burn more slowly and can survive for timescales far longer than the current age of the Universe.

Our Sun lies between those extremes.

So when astronomers look into stellar nurseries, they are not merely seeing "clouds near stars".

They are observing the physical stages predicted by gravitational collapse and star-formation theory:

dense cores,

protostars,

accretion disks,

jets,

young stellar objects,

and clusters of newly formed stars.

The Universe is still demonstrating the process.

No reconstruction of Earth's origin makes sense without it.

Because the Sun had to form before Earth could orbit it.

And the disk around a young star is where planets begin.

## PAGE 039 — PLANETS IN THE MAKING

Planet formation used to have one enormous observational disadvantage.

We had a sample size of one.

Our Solar System.

Any theory of planet formation had to explain the Sun, eight planets, moons, asteroids and comets without knowing whether our arrangement was typical, unusual or almost absurd.

That situation has changed.

Thousands of exoplanets are now known.

And even more importantly for this chapter, astronomers can observe **protoplanetary disks** — the gas-and-dust structures around young stars in which planets form.

One of the landmark examples is **HL Tauri**.

In 2014, the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array, or ALMA, imaged its young disk with remarkable detail. The disk contained a system of bright rings separated by dark gaps.

The image became famous because it looked uncannily like construction was already underway around a very young star.

But we must preserve an important scientific distinction.

A ring or gap is not automatically a planet.

Planets can carve structures into disks, but pressure variations, snow lines, magnetic effects and other disk processes can also create substructure.

That is why astronomy searches for multiple lines of evidence.

The case of **PDS 70** goes further.

In 2018, the European Southern Observatory reported the first robust image of a young planet, **PDS 70b**, caught forming inside a disk around its star.

Later observations identified a second planet in the system and studied material associated with their growth.

Hubble observations detected ultraviolet emission from hot gas falling onto PDS 70b, allowing researchers to estimate the rate at which the young giant planet was gaining mass.

That is not a computer animation of how somebody thinks planets probably form.

It is observation of a young planetary system in the act of construction.

Again, we are not watching PDS 70 continuously for five million years.

We combine its present state with observations of other young systems, older systems, disk chemistry, orbital dynamics and computer models.

The same population logic used for stars applies to planets.

Different systems provide different frames of the movie.

And the frames have delivered another surprise:

planetary systems are wildly diverse.

Hot Jupiters orbit astonishingly close to their stars.

Compact systems fit several planets inside the orbital distance of Mercury.

Other planets travel on elongated or inclined paths.

Some stars host small rocky worlds.

Others host massive giants.

Our Solar System is one outcome of planet formation.

It is not the definition of a planetary system.

That lesson will matter when we finally reach Earth.

We should explain why our planet formed the way it did without quietly assuming that every disk must produce another copy of us.

## PAGE 040 — WORLDS BEFORE OURS

The Universe had planets before it had Earth.

We can prove that much.

The star **Kepler-444** hosts five small planets and is roughly **11 billion years old**. NASA reported the system as having formed when the Milky Way was only about two billion years old.

Those worlds orbit extremely close to their star and are not Earth twins.

They are important for a different reason.

They demonstrate that small planets could form **billions of years before the Solar System existed**.

That changes the timeline of possibility.

By the time Earth formed, the Universe had already been making stars for billions of years.

Some of those stars had already died.

Their material had been recycled.

Galaxies had merged and grown.

Planetary systems had appeared.

Some planets had existed for longer than the entire current age of Earth.

Does that prove that another civilisation existed before humanity?

No.

It proves **time was available**.

That is not the same thing as proving life used it.

At present, Earth remains the only world on which life is confirmed.

No extraterrestrial civilisation has been verified.

No archaeological ruin has been found on an exoplanet.

No signal has been established as technology from another species.

The correct scientific statement is therefore narrower but still fascinating:

**planetary systems existed long before Earth, so environments in which life might potentially arise have existed for billions of years longer than our planet has.**

Whether life actually appeared on any of them is a separate question.

Whether complex life appeared is another.

Whether intelligence and technology appeared is another again.

Those probabilities cannot currently be measured from one confirmed biosphere.

And there is another wrinkle.

A civilisation elsewhere need not be our contemporary.

If technological species exist or have existed elsewhere, cosmic history allows them to arise before us, after us, or during the same broad epoch.

But "allows" is doing important work in that sentence.

Physics and chronology permit the possibility.

Evidence has not yet confirmed the occupants.

This is where astronomy can be humbling in two directions at once.

We are not cosmically early in the sense of living near the beginning of star and planet formation.

But we also cannot assume that an old planet is inhabited simply because it had time.

Age creates opportunity.

It does not manufacture biology.

Later, when this book reaches the origin and persistence of life, we will return to the distinction.

For now, ancient planetary systems tell us something secure:

Earth was not the Universe's first attempt at building a world.

Our planet arrived in a cosmos that had already been constructing stars and planets for billions of years.

The next question is what those earlier stars contributed to the material from which Earth would eventually be made.

Because the young Universe had plenty of hydrogen and helium.

It did not yet have enough carbon, oxygen, silicon, iron and the long list of heavier elements required to build a rocky planet like ours.

Those elements had to be made.

Mostly, the factories were stars.

### Fact-check decisions

- **13.8 Ga** is used as the current standard age of the Universe, with the explicit explanation that it is model-constrained rather than an exact metaphysical timestamp.
- The CMB is described as light released around **380,000 years after the Big Bang**, not light from time zero.
- The manuscript explicitly states that the **first individual Population III stars have not yet been securely observed**, so no exact first-star birthday is claimed.
- **MoM-z14** is used as a dated 2026 frontier example: a spectroscopically confirmed galaxy observed at roughly **280 million years after the Big Bang**. This record-holder must be rechecked before publication because the observational frontier changes rapidly.
- Telescope observations are described as **snapshots across populations plus genuine lookback time**, not a literal time-lapse of one star or galaxy over millions/billions of years.
- The Milky Way is described as having a **formation history rather than one exact birthday**. Gaia evidence for ancient components beginning around 13 Ga is distinguished from the later assembly of the modern disk and merger history.
- Star formation is presented as an observable ongoing process involving molecular clouds, dense cores, protostars, disks, jets/outflows and eventual sustained hydrogen fusion.
- HL Tauri's rings/gaps are not automatically labelled as confirmed planets; the manuscript retains alternative disk-physics explanations.
- PDS 70b is used as direct observational evidence of a young planet forming within a protoplanetary disk.
- Kepler-444 is used only to establish that small planets existed billions of years before the Solar System. The manuscript **does not infer life or civilisation from system age**.
- The possibility of extraterrestrial civilisations existing before/after humanity is labelled as chronologically permitted but **unconfirmed**.
