### A TEMPERATURE IS NOT A METABOLISM

A warm body can raise several different questions. How warm was it? Did its temperature remain steady? How much heat did it produce, and how much did it retain? The familiar division between warm-blooded and cold-blooded animals can conceal these distinctions rather than explain them.

In 2011, Robert Eagle and colleagues investigated fossil teeth from large Jurassic sauropods using **clumped-isotope thermometry**. The method examines how heavier forms of carbon and oxygen occur together within a mineral. Their arrangement depends on the temperature at which that mineral formed.[1]

The reconstructed temperatures lay around 36–38 degrees Celsius, comparable with those of many living mammals. They were lower than the temperatures predicted by a particular model linking dinosaur body size and heat retention. The result constrained that model, but did not independently reveal the animals' complete metabolism.[1]

Eggshell supplied another archive. Because its mineral forms within the mother, an intact chemical signal can record conditions during reproduction rather than the temperature of the nest after laying. A 2015 study applied this approach to eggshells attributed to titanosaurs and oviraptorids.[2]

The titanosaur estimates resembled temperatures in large living heat-producing animals. The oviraptorid estimates were lower, yet exceeded temperatures reconstructed from associated non-biological carbonates. The authors argued for variation in thermoregulation among the dinosaurs sampled. A reference carbonate, however, is an environmental proxy, not a thermometer held beside the living animal.[2]

Preservation requires its own test. A shell may retain its outline while some original mineral has been replaced or chemically altered. In 2020, Robin Dawson and colleagues examined shell microstructure and chemical indicators of alteration alongside isotope measurements, comparing dinosaur estimates with those from local fossil molluscs.[3]

Some dinosaur temperatures exceeded the environmental estimates, supporting internal heat production. The authors extended that interpretation across representatives of the major dinosaur branches. They also identified an important limitation: uncertainty over the producer of a Romanian eggshell weakened a proposed comparison between dwarf and giant sauropods.[3]

A 2023 investigation added a second clumped-isotope measurement. Comparing the two signals helped distinguish temperature effects from other consequences of mineral formation. Modern bird eggshells showed a particular departure from chemical equilibrium, whereas the troodontid eggshells examined did not.[4]

Those fossils, assigned in the study to *Troodon*, yielded both high and substantially lower reconstructed temperatures. The researchers interpreted the variation as evidence of **heterothermy**: an ability to vary body temperature rather than maintain one rigid setting. Separate shells do not follow one individual through its day, so the proposed control remains an interpretation rather than observed behaviour.[4]

The investigation therefore works through successive questions. First, does the original chemical signal survive? Then, what temperature does the calibration imply? How does that compare with the surroundings? Only after those tests can the result help distinguish ways of producing, conserving or shedding heat.

These records make a single physiological label for every dinosaur inadequate. They also show why uncertainty need not make the investigation directionless: a new measurement can test a specific assumption that an older method left unresolved.

Temperature brings us back to the body's surface. Bones and eggshells offer indirect clues, but occasionally the rock preserves something more immediate: the covering that lay outside the skeleton.
