### THE EVIDENCE INSIDE A MEAL

A tooth can suggest what an animal was equipped to eat. Food preserved after swallowing brings us closer to what it actually consumed. Even then, recognising a meal and identifying its eater are different achievements.

In 2024, Martin Qvarnström and colleagues compared five fossil assemblages from Poland spanning the Late Triassic and earliest Jurassic. Their material included hundreds of **bromalites**, fossil digestive products such as droppings and regurgitated remains, alongside other feeding evidence. Scanning and other methods revealed fish, beetles, plant fragments and pieces of vertebrate bone.[1]

Shapes, contents and associated fossils helped identify probable producers. Some connections were therefore more secure than others. The authors combined direct feeding evidence with anatomical comparisons to reconstruct food webs, rather than treating every connection as a preserved encounter.[1]

The regional sequence supported a stepwise expansion of dinosaur ecological roles. Important losses among other groups preceded the end-Triassic crisis. Broader diets, considered alongside changing vegetation, offered one explanation for the expansion of dinosaur herbivores. This was not evidence that one eruption instantly created every dinosaur-dominated ecosystem.[1]

A food web is consequently both a record and an argument. A swallowed fragment anchors one connection; extending that connection to a whole community requires further evidence. Its value increases when the reader can distinguish the preserved link from the reconstructed one.

Growth supplied a separate test. Another 2024 study, led by Kristina Curry Rogers, examined thigh-bone microstructure in early dinosaurs and their contemporaries from Argentina's Ischigualasto Formation. Tissue organisation, vascular spaces and interruptions in bone deposition helped investigators compare growth histories.[2]

The dinosaurs were among the faster-growing animals sampled, but they were not alone. Several other reptile lineages showed rapid growth too. The result supported an early origin for high dinosaur growth rates without making that capacity a unique advantage over every neighbour.[2]

This distinction is important in any explanation of success. A useful characteristic need not be exclusive. Conversely, possessing it does not guarantee survival when the availability of food or the conditions of a habitat change. The question is how a trait worked in a particular setting, not whether it deserves a permanent label of superiority.

An earlier comparison approached the problem through overall anatomy. In 2008, Stephen Brusatte and colleagues compared early dinosaurs with crurotarsan archosaurs, the comparison group they used for many crocodile-line relatives. They measured **morphological disparity**, the spread of anatomical forms, alongside rates of change in recorded skeletal characters.[3]

Dinosaurs occupied a narrower anatomical range in that analysis, while their rate of character evolution was indistinguishable from the comparison group's. The researchers favoured historical circumstances over a prolonged, general competitive advantage as the principal explanation for their expansion.[3]

Neither measure was a direct experiment in competition. A range of body forms is not the same as reproductive success, and a rate of anatomical change is not a universal measure of adaptation. The result challenges a sweeping explanation without showing that no particular dinosaur ever benefited from a particular trait.

Meals, growth records and anatomical comparisons thus test different parts of the story. Together they make an inevitable takeover difficult to assume. The next question concerns another visible outcome of dinosaur evolution: how could some bodies become so large, and what can their surviving bones establish about that transformation?
