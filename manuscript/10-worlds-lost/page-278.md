### ICE ABOVE, OXYGEN BELOW

A limestone can preserve two different records of a crisis. Fossils tell us which organisms were present. The chemistry of the rock can help reconstruct the conditions surrounding them. Near the end of the Ordovician, these records connect severe marine losses with a changing climate and ocean.[1–3]

The climate investigation begins with a difficulty. Changes in the oxygen isotopes of marine carbonate can reflect both water temperature and the growth of continental ice. Isotopes are versions of an element with different atomic masses. A changing ratio is evidence, but its interpretation requires us to separate overlapping effects.

In 2011, Seth Finnegan and colleagues used **clumped-isotope thermometry**, a method examining particular combinations of isotopes in carbonate, to estimate ocean temperatures independently enough to constrain the ice-volume problem. Their reconstruction identified a cooling episode during the final Ordovician stage, coinciding with a glacial maximum and the extinction interval.[1]

The study did not depict a uniformly frozen planet. Its reconstructed tropical seas remained warm even during the cooling. Ice growth and tropical warmth could coexist; a global climatic change need not produce the same temperature in every habitat.[1]

Oxygen supplied another part of the investigation. A 2018 study measured uranium isotopes in limestones from Anticosti Island, Canada. The authors interpreted a shift in their composition as evidence that oxygen-poor conditions had expanded across the ocean, rather than merely at the place where the samples accumulated.[2]

That larger interpretation depended partly on comparison with indicators of local conditions. The uranium signal did not simply track those local indicators. Using a model of uranium cycling, the investigators connected the pattern with more extensive **anoxia**: the absence of dissolved oxygen.[2]

Their reconstructed anoxic interval extended through changing glacial and sea-level conditions. It was not confined to a simple moment when the ice disappeared. Cooling, circulation, nutrient delivery and oxygen supply could interact; a cold climate did not guarantee a well-oxygenated ocean everywhere.[2]

How quickly did the biological and physical changes unfold? In 2025, Zhutong Zhang and colleagues dated zircon crystals from volcanic ash beds in South China. They combined those measurements with models relating rock depth to age and with previously assembled fossil and climate records.[3]

Their preferred chronology placed the extinction's onset near 442.8 million years ago, with uncertainties of several hundred thousand years. It supported a cooling-associated phase followed by a shorter, faster phase linked to warming. The authors argued that the rate of climatic change was important, not just the difference between starting and finishing temperatures.[3]

Those rates depend on the reconstructed durations and on how the records are matched. The study's numerical timeline is therefore a dated scientific proposal with reported uncertainty, not an exact stopwatch for every marine community. The stratigraphic cautions from the previous page still apply: apparent pulses must be tested against the way sediments accumulated.[3,4]

Together, these investigations establish a crisis larger than the disappearance of one local population. They also resist a single uncomplicated picture of its cause. Changes in temperature, habitat and oxygen availability need not affect every organism equally or operate on identical schedules.

The Ordovician seas did not become lifeless. But survival did not restore the lost species or prevent later crises. Farther forward in time, during the Devonian, another set of dark marine layers records losses that must not be compressed into one catastrophe.
