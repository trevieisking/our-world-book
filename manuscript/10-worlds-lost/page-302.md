### BRANCHES OLDER THAN THEIR FOSSILS

A lineage can exist before it becomes abundant, and before it leaves the oldest fossil we have found. That interval matters when we ask whether the ancestors of living placental mammals diversified before or after the catastrophe. A recovery curve and a family tree do not measure the same history.

First, the group must be defined. The placental **crown group** comprises the last common ancestor of all living placental mammals and all its descendants. Extinct relatives outside that crown can possess many similar features without establishing that its deepest branches had already separated. The identification of a fossil controls which evolutionary event its age can constrain.[1,4]

In 2013, Maureen O'Leary and colleagues assembled 4,541 observable biological characters for 86 living and fossil species, combining them with molecular sequences. Their fossil-calibrated reconstruction placed the origin of the placental crown and its major orders after the Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary.[1]

That was an inference from an extensive comparison, not a skeleton of the common ancestor recovered intact. It also made a claim that other evidence could challenge. Finding the earliest recognisable member of a branch is not necessarily equivalent to dating the branch's beginning.

A 2023 investigation led by Nicole Foley compared 241 placental mammal genome assemblies. Relationships inferred from different genomic regions and analytical approaches broadly agreed at many deep branches, although other parts of the tree retained disagreement. Their dated trees placed branching events both before and immediately after the extinction boundary.[2]

A **molecular clock** connects accumulated genetic change with elapsed time. It is not a sequence of calendar dates hidden in DNA. Rates can vary, and fossil-based age constraints help calibrate the reconstruction. Agreement among sequences can strengthen a relationship without eliminating uncertainty over when the split occurred.[2,4]

Fossils themselves can be analysed without treating their first appearances as exact origins. Also in 2023, Emily Carlisle and colleagues used a probabilistic model of the fossil record. Their results favoured a Late Cretaceous origin of placentals, while the crown groups of many living orders originated around or after the boundary.[3]

This offered partial convergence with molecular estimates through a different route. It did not uncover previously unknown Cretaceous placental bones. Instead, the model estimated how much history could precede the preserved occurrences. The pattern of sampling remained part of the answer.[3]

The disagreement remained active in 2026. Sandra Álvarez-Carretero and colleagues compared molecular dating models that allowed an older origin with models forcing placental origins after the extinction. Their tests strongly favoured the older alternative for the datasets, calibrations and rate models examined.[4]

The result is more informative than saying that any date can be obtained by changing assumptions. Competing assumptions can themselves be tested against the data. Yet support within those comparisons does not turn one estimated branching date into an observed birth, or settle the identity of every disputed fossil.

The history therefore contains several overlapping transitions: branches separating, bodies acquiring recognisable features, organisms entering the fossil record and populations expanding into new ecological roles. Compressing them into a single mammalian beginning obscures what each archive contributes.

A branching tree also tells us little by itself about the brains inside those expanding bodies. Did larger mammals immediately become relatively larger-brained animals, or did these changes follow different paths?
