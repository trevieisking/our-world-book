### A BRAIN IN PROPORTION

The inside of a fossil skull preserves a different kind of outline from the outside. Its cavity can constrain the shape of the brain and neighbouring tissues that once occupied it. A digital reconstruction of that space, an **endocast**, lets researchers investigate an organ that has usually disappeared.[2]

The outline is not the organ itself. It contains neither a count of functioning nerve cells nor a record of what the animal learned. Its value lies in the comparisons that surviving boundaries permit, not in an ability to recover a mind from an empty space.

In 2022, Ornella Bertrand and colleagues used scans of Paleocene skulls to investigate early placental brain evolution. Their comparison challenged a simple story of brains steadily enlarging relative to bodies after the extinction. Initially, body mass increased faster, and relative brain size declined.[1]

A falling proportion does not necessarily mean that the brain became smaller in absolute terms. The denominator matters. If a body grows faster than its brain, the brain can constitute a smaller fraction of a larger animal. Confusing the fraction with the organ would create a change that the measurements do not establish.

Later, in the Eocene, several lineages independently developed relatively enlarged brains with increased sensory regions. The researchers proposed that changing ecological opportunities and competition helped explain the sequence. That causal interpretation went beyond the anatomical measurements: the scans did not directly measure ancient competition.[1]

A more focused study examined three species of the Eocene mammal *Trogosus*. Its reconstructed brain cavities retained relatively large olfactory bulbs, associated with smell, and a comparatively small neocortex, the outer cerebral region involved in integrating information.[2]

The combination was consistent with an animal living and feeding on the ground. The authors also proposed that limited expansion of its neocortex might have contributed to vulnerability as competitors and predators changed. These are different claims. Anatomical compatibility with a lifestyle is not proof that one brain feature caused an entire lineage to disappear.[2]

The scale of comparison also needs testing. A study first published in December 2025, with its final volume appearing in 2026, examined brain and body measurements across 4,679 animal species. Joanna Baker and colleagues found that the relationship curved rather than following the commonly assumed straight line after logarithmic transformation.[3]

In other words, increasing body size did not imply the same proportional brain change across the full range of animals. Their modelling connected that pattern with differences among individuals within species. The analysis warned that apparent evolutionary patterns can depend on how size is accounted for, not only on which fossils have been discovered.[3]

This does not make endocasts useless, or erase the differences visible inside skulls. It asks for separate checks on the reconstructed anatomy, estimated body mass and comparison used to call a brain relatively large or small. A statistical description and a behavioural explanation must not silently become the same thing.

Mammalian history therefore offers no single escalator carrying every lineage towards larger bodies, larger brains and eventual survival. Different combinations persisted under different conditions; some branches later ended. To close this chapter, we must ask what those vanished branches contribute to the history, rather than looking only at the ones that reach the present.
