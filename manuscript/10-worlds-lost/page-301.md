### THE FIRST MILLION YEARS

Mammalian success after the impact was built across a boundary that had also eliminated mammals. The survivors entered neither an undamaged world nor a landscape entirely without other animals. Their expansion must be reconstructed from losses and new appearances, with the surviving record tested for what it leaves out.

In 2013, Gregory Wilson examined mammal communities from northeastern Montana using tooth shapes and estimates of body size. His analysis supported disproportionate losses among larger animals with specialised diets, including strict meat-eaters and forms relying heavily on plants.[1]

The pattern suggested that disruption of food production mattered to the extinction. The teeth did not preserve a measured calorie shortage, but they allowed a mechanism to be compared with the kinds of animals lost. The earliest recovery also involved organisms interpreted as immigrants rather than only descendants of the local survivors.[1]

Wilson distinguished increasing species richness from expansion into a wider range of diets and body forms. More named species need not mean more distinct ecological roles. A community can rebuild one kind of diversity before another, so the question of when mammals recovered requires specifying what is being counted.[1]

A broader North American analysis in 2016 exposed a related problem. Nicholas Longrich and colleagues identified only four species crossing the boundary among the 59 Late Cretaceous species in their sample. The survivors tended to be widespread and common, making them relatively easy to find as fossils.[2]

Rare, geographically restricted species were both less likely to survive and less likely to be sampled. Incomplete collecting could therefore hide some of the losses rather than merely exaggerate them. The result was a warning about the record, not a claim that only four mammal species survived worldwide.[2]

Different post-extinction localities also contained different communities. Combining them revealed a regional recovery that a narrow geographical sample could miss. Neither a single depleted assemblage nor a rich one could stand in for the whole continent.[2]

Colorado provided another approach: following plants and vertebrates through the same dated sequence. In 2019, Tyler Lyson and colleagues described fossils spanning the first million years after the impact. Within roughly 100,000 years, mammalian richness had doubled and the largest mammals approached pre-extinction body sizes.[3]

By approximately 300,000 years, larger maximum sizes and more specialised diets coincided with increased plant diversity. Further large mammals appeared by about 700,000 years, alongside the first recorded legumes, the bean family, in the sequence. Several changes also coincided with warming intervals.[3]

The association made food and climate important candidates for explaining the rebuilding. It did not establish that one newly recorded plant caused every change in mammalian size. A first fossil appearance can reflect arrival or preservation as well as evolutionary origin, and several environmental changes may operate together.

These records replace an instantaneous takeover with a sequence that can be investigated. Survival, immigration, diversification and ecological expansion overlap, but they are not the same event. Larger bodies and new feeding roles emerged within recovering communities rather than outside the changing history of plants and climate.[1–3]

One further distinction remains. The date when a group became abundant is not necessarily the date when its evolutionary branch began. To connect these early mammals with the groups alive today, we must compare the relationships recorded in bones with a different historical archive: inherited DNA.
