### TWO DISTURBANCES, DIFFERENT CLOCKS

The Deccan Traps preserve an immense volcanic history in India. Their proximity in time to Chicxulub creates a demanding question, not an automatic answer. Did eruptions help prepare the crisis, cause the sharp extinction pulse, influence recovery—or contribute differently during different intervals?

Two studies published in 2019 approached the problem with different radiometric clocks. Blair Schoene and colleagues used uranium and lead in zircon to reconstruct four high-volume eruptive periods. Their model placed one pulse beginning tens of thousands of years before the impact and extinction.[1]

Courtney Sprain and colleagues used argon isotopes and estimated that about three-quarters of the volcanic volume was emplaced after the boundary. Earlier climatic changes coincided with smaller eruptive phases. That mismatch raised a crucial possibility: the amount of gas reaching the atmosphere need not track the amount of lava reaching the surface.[2]

The distinction prevents a misleading shortcut. A thick lava sequence is a record of erupted rock. To turn it into an atmospheric history, we need evidence about gas content, release and timing. Even a perfectly dated rock does not contain a complete measurement of its environmental effect.

In 2020, Pincelli Hull and colleagues combined reconstructed temperatures with carbon-cycle modelling. Their analysis supported a major episode of volcanic outgassing beginning and ending before the impact, while the sharp extinction coincided with the collision and a profound biological change in carbon cycling.[3]

Their model also suggested that the altered ocean could absorb substantial carbon dioxide after extinction, reducing the warming otherwise expected from later volcanism. A muted temperature response would therefore not necessarily mean that no gas was released. The receiving environment mattered as well as the source.[3]

Chemical evidence kept the earlier disturbance in view. In 2023, Sara Callegaro and colleagues reconstructed sulfur and fluorine budgets in Deccan lavas. Relatively sulfur-rich material occurred before the extinction interval; later sampled basalts were generally poorer in sulfur. The authors proposed repeated, short volcanic cooling episodes as a source of pre-impact ecological stress.[4]

A further test came from land far from the eruptions. Lauren O'Connor and colleagues examined organic temperature indicators and carbon isotopes in two boundary-spanning lignites from the western United States. Lignite is a low-grade coal formed from accumulated plant material. Their 2024 study reconstructed both warming and a shorter cooling episode.[5]

The cooling, approximately five degrees in those records, was correlated with a Deccan eruptive pulse roughly 30,000 years before the boundary. Temperatures then returned towards their previous values before the extinction. The authors argued that volcanic climate change was not the primary cause of the final terrestrial crisis.[5]

That conclusion does not transform two sites into a global census of healthy ecosystems. Temperature recovery is not necessarily ecological recovery, and an earlier stress could leave consequences not captured by that measurement. It does constrain the claim that the particular cooling episode and the boundary extinction were the same event.

The studies therefore distinguish environmental disturbance from the timing of catastrophic loss. Volcanism belongs in the history, but assigning every change near the boundary to it would disregard the impact evidence. Equally, recognising the impact's central role does not erase the eruptions.

Causation becomes clearer when the clocks are kept separate. The next test asks how the losses were distributed among living things. Why did some branches disappear while others crossed the boundary into a changed world?
