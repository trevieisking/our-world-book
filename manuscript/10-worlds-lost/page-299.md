### A FOREST IS MORE THAN GREEN COVER

A landscape can become green again without recovering its former forest. The returning plants may differ in identity, height and abundance, and their relationships with other organisms may have changed. Fossil leaves, pollen and spores allow those possibilities to be tested rather than hidden within the single word recovery.

In 2001, Vivi Vajda and colleagues reported a marked change in New Zealand's boundary record. A diverse assemblage of pollen and spores gave way to one dominated by relatively few fern species. This **fern spike** occurred alongside an iridium anomaly, connecting severe vegetation disturbance far from Chicxulub with the impact boundary.[1]

The signal is microscopic, but its implications concern a landscape. A shift in the material plants released and sediments preserved can reveal a change in vegetation that left no complete standing forest. It is not, however, a photograph from which every surviving tree can be counted.

Tropical South America supplied a longer comparison. In 2021, Mónica Carvalho and colleagues examined extensive collections of fossil pollen and leaves from Colombia. Their reconstruction distinguished relatively open Late Cretaceous forests from later Paleocene forests with more continuous canopies and multiple vegetation layers dominated by flowering plants.[2]

Plant diversity declined substantially across the boundary, and the study placed its recovery at roughly six million years. That interval did not mean the land remained bare until a forest suddenly appeared. It measured the rebuilding of diversity within the investigated record, while vegetation and forest structure could change along a different trajectory.[2]

The later canopy is therefore not simply the old forest repaired. The evidence supports ecological reorganisation: a different arrangement of plants and their surrounding conditions. Resemblance to modern tropical forests does not make every later species or interaction already present in the Paleocene.

Farther south, leaves from Patagonia exposed another ambiguity. Elena Stiles and colleagues compared more than 3,500 fossil leaves from deposits before and after the boundary. Their 2020 study found substantial turnover among species and reduced richness in the younger assemblages.[3]

Yet many broader plant groups persisted. Earlier pollen studies and taxonomic comparisons had indicated continuity at the level of genera and families. This need not conflict with extensive species loss. A family can cross a boundary while many of its constituent species do not.[3]

The authors treated their highest turnover estimate as a maximum, identifying possible geographical, sampling, stratigraphic and climatic biases. Their comparison with the Williston Basin in North America also showed that the regions did not begin with equal diversity or follow identical recovery patterns.[3]

These distinctions change what it means for a forest to survive. Continued presence of a broad lineage, persistence of particular species and restoration of a canopy are different propositions. A single percentage cannot express all three. Nor can a well-studied regional sequence be extended silently to every continent.

The records nevertheless converge on something substantial: the boundary disrupted vegetation at widely separated sites, and subsequent greenery did not erase the losses. Recovery involved survivors and new arrangements, not the reappearance of organisms that had become extinct.[1–3]

The animals inhabiting these landscapes were also part of older histories. Mammals did not begin when the large non-bird dinosaurs disappeared. Before following their expansion through the recovering forests, we must look back at the range of lives their earlier relatives had already explored.
