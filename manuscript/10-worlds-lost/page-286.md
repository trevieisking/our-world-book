### THE SPEED OF AN ERUPTION

The environmental effect of volcanism depends on more than the total amount of molten rock. Timing matters. The same quantity released gradually or in closely spaced bursts can impose different demands on the atmosphere, the oceans and the organisms living within them.

At the end of the Triassic, extensive volcanism formed the Central Atlantic Magmatic Province. Its rocks offer a way to test the relationship between eruptions and another interval of major biological losses. As with the end-Permian crisis, a shared position on a broad timeline is only the beginning of the argument.[1]

In 2013, Terrence Blackburn and colleagues used uranium–lead dates from zircon to refine the chronology. Their results aligned the earliest volcanism with the extinction and resolved four major pulses across approximately 600,000 years. Volcanic activity therefore continued while biological recovery was already under way.[1]

That long interval does not tell us how steadily lava emerged during each pulse. A sequence spanning many thousands of years may contain eruptions far shorter than the gaps between them. Averaging the material across the whole interval could conceal precisely the rates that mattered most to living things.

Evidence of the gases came from much smaller objects. A 2020 investigation led by Manfredo Capriolo examined bubbles within **melt inclusions**, tiny pockets of material trapped in crystals. Raman spectroscopy, which identifies substances through their interaction with laser light, detected carbon dioxide in the enclosed bubbles.[2]

The results supported carbon-rich magmas and indicated a deep source for at least part of the carbon. Estimating the total released into the atmosphere required further calculations and assumptions. A trapped bubble preserves evidence of a gas; it is not a direct measurement of the entire volcanic province's emissions.[2]

The researchers proposed that these emissions contributed to warming and ocean acidification. Another investigation, published in 2024, examined whether the initial eruptions were concentrated enough to produce a different, shorter-lived disturbance as well.[2,3]

Dennis Kent and colleagues compared magnetic directions recorded in successive lava flows from Morocco and northeastern North America. Closely matching directions grouped the initial activity into five pulses. The authors interpreted each as lasting less than a century, with the first four distributed across roughly 40,000 years.[3]

These are groups within the initial activity, not a replacement count for every eruptive episode across the province's longer history. The interpretation uses how the magnetic field changes through time to constrain how rapidly flows accumulated. It does not measure the temperature experienced by nearby animals.[3]

Rapid release of sulfur could create sunlight-reflecting sulfate aerosols and brief volcanic winters. The 2024 authors proposed this as a plausible contributor to losses on tropical land, while allowing longer carbon-dioxide effects to influence marine environments through warming and acidification.[3]

The contrast is therefore not simply cold versus heat. Different emissions can act over different timescales. Establishing their relative contributions requires comparing eruption tempo with environmental changes and biological losses, rather than selecting whichever mechanism produces the most dramatic scene.

By now, dinosaurs were already part of the world undergoing this disruption. Explaining their subsequent expansion requires another kind of evidence: not merely which animals remained, but what they ate and how their relationships changed as communities were rebuilt.
