### THE HOURS AND THE WINTER

Inside the Chicxulub crater, the aftermath accumulated in stone. A drill core recovered roughly 130 metres of melted rock and broken debris that researchers interpreted as having settled during the first day. Above it, much thinner sediments recorded the slower processes that followed.[1]

The sequence is not a simple pile of material falling from the sky. In the reconstruction published by Sean Gulick and colleagues in 2019, seawater flooded the newly formed crater, carried debris across its elevated inner ring and continued to move as waves crossed the basin. Charcoal near the top supplied evidence of burning material transported into the site.[1]

That evidence establishes neither the ignition time of every fire nor a planet burning uniformly. It does, however, connect the collision to a disturbed landscape beyond the crater. Following its consequences farther requires examining material that remained aloft after the heaviest debris had fallen.

In 2023, Cem Berk Senel and colleagues measured fine silicate particles in a boundary deposit in North Dakota. Their size distribution became an input to simulations comparing dust, sulfur and soot. Small particles matter because their behaviour in the atmosphere differs from that of rapidly falling coarse fragments.[2]

The simulations allowed fine dust to persist for about fifteen years and reduced the light available for photosynthesis severely for almost two. These durations describe the modelled atmosphere, not a weather diary recovered from the rock. They nevertheless demonstrate how an impact could damage distant food webs without its blast reaching every habitat.[2]

Sulfur introduced another test. In 2025, Katerina Rodiouchkina and colleagues compared sulfur concentrations and isotope compositions from the impact structure and terrestrial boundary deposits. Their calculation estimated a release of 67 billion tonnes, with an uncertainty of 39 billion tonnes—substantially below some earlier numerical estimates.[3]

Reducing that contribution changes the proposed severity of sulfur-driven cooling. It does not remove the other particles or prove that the winter was harmless. The quantity released, the time it remained aloft and its interaction with radiation are separate parts of the explanation.[3]

A study published in July 2026 investigated a much shorter interval. Brandon Johnson and colleagues argued that fine dust could also strengthen the thermal pulse generated as impact droplets, called spherules, returned through the atmosphere. In their analysis, dust restricted the escape of heat to space and increased radiation directed towards the surface.[4]

This revived the possibility of extensive heating and fire during the earliest hours. It did not turn those calculations into a census of animals killed, or resolve competing reconstructions of how the ejecta travelled. A proposed global mechanism still needs geological and biological tests across different regions.[4]

Heat followed by darkness and cooling is not a contradiction. The energy and materials involved can change their effects as the event unfolds. A surface exposed during the initial pulse, an organism sheltered beneath it and a food web deprived of fresh production would face different hazards.

The catastrophe therefore has more than one timescale. Its first deposits, atmospheric aftermath and biological consequences must be connected without being collapsed into a single scene. Another large disturbance was unfolding around the same boundary: volcanism in India. How can its contribution be separated from the impact?
