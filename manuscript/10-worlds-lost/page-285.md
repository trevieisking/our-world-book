### BARRIERS WITHOUT OCEANS

Continuous land is not necessarily continuous habitat. An animal moving across a supercontinent would still need food, water and conditions it could tolerate. A climatic boundary could restrict a population without leaving the unmistakable geological barrier of an ocean between two shores.

In 2015, Jessica Whiteside and colleagues investigated Late Triassic deposits in western North America, then at low latitudes. They combined pollen and spore records with evidence of wildfire, carbon-isotope changes and reconstructed atmospheric carbon dioxide. The records indicated repeatedly changing plant communities within an unstable, fire-prone environment.[1]

Their explanation connected those changes to the vertebrate record. Large, rapidly growing plant-eating dinosaurs would have required dependable resources. Fluctuating conditions could help explain why these animals were poorly represented while other reptile groups persisted. This was an ecological interpretation of several connected records, not a direct measurement of a dinosaur's daily food supply.[1]

It also concerned scarcity and community composition, not proof that every tropical region was permanently inaccessible. A population might pass through, remain uncommon or occupy a favourable pocket without becoming dominant. Those are different possibilities, and the fossil evidence needed to distinguish them is not identical.

A later study examined the northern end of a proposed dispersal route. In 2021, Dennis Kent and Lars Clemmensen compared the magnetic sequence in East Greenland's dinosaur-bearing rocks with a calibrated record of changes in Earth's magnetic polarity. Their age model placed the local appearance of sauropodomorphs at about 214 million years ago.[2]

That estimate fell within an independently reconstructed decline in atmospheric carbon dioxide. The authors proposed that less extreme climatic contrasts had made northward dispersal easier. The timing supported the hypothesis, but did not preserve a trail taken by particular animals, or show that the local first occurrence was the earliest possible arrival.[2]

An important challenge arrived in 2025. David Lovelace and colleagues described *Ahvaytum bahndooiveche* from the Popo Agie Formation of Wyoming, in a region that had been near the equator. Their analysis assigned it to an early dinosaur branch, with sauropodomorph affinities.[3]

Dating involved another distinction. Zircon grains from higher in the formation supplied maximum depositional constraints. An age–depth model then estimated the older fossil horizon at roughly 230 million years. The estimate was not obtained by directly dating the animal, nor by treating every dated mineral as newly formed when its surrounding sediment accumulated.[3]

The occurrence challenged a prolonged absence of early sauropodomorphs from northern Pangaea. It did not automatically invalidate the Greenland rock chronology, or make the evidence for environmental instability disappear. Rather, the broader dispersal account needed to accommodate an earlier northern population.[3]

This is how a useful climatic hypothesis can change without being replaced by the claim that climate never mattered. The strength, location and duration of a barrier may vary; so may the requirements of different dinosaur lineages. Presence, successful establishment and ecological dominance should not be squeezed into one arrival date.

The Triassic world was therefore neither an empty stage awaiting dinosaurs nor a single uniform habitat through which they spread without constraint. Its histories were regional, and its reconstructed pathways remain open to new evidence. Near the period's end, however, another crisis would alter the conditions under which many of those communities existed.
