### RECOVERY WAS NOT A SINGLE DATE

The return of one species, the rebuilding of a food web and the stabilisation of the environment are different kinds of recovery. They need not happen together. After the end-Permian crisis, an unusually rich fossil bed can reveal what life had regained without proving that the rest of the ocean had regained it too.

The Guiyang Biota, described in 2023 from South China, offers such a window. Its fossils include diverse fishes and crustaceans. Uranium–lead dating placed the assemblage at about 250.8 million years old, approximately a million years after the extinction. The published age uncertainty was measured in tens of thousands of years, not reduced to an exact anniversary.[1]

The variety of organisms supported a marine community with several feeding levels. This was more than a scattering of isolated survivors. A **lagerstätte**, an exceptionally well-preserved fossil deposit, could reveal ecological complexity that less informative rocks had failed to preserve.[1]

Yet the deposit does not contain a complete census of every surrounding habitat. Nor does an early complex community prove that all its lineages originated during the interval immediately preceding burial. Some may represent survivors or immigrants. The fossil assemblage establishes their presence together more securely than the full history of their arrival.

Another window opened in southeastern Idaho. The Paris Biota, described in 2017, preserved an Early Triassic community containing sponges, echinoderms and cephalopods, among other animals, alongside algae. The authors recognised multiple feeding levels and anatomical combinations that had been poorly documented in rocks of this age.[2]

Some fossils belonged to groups with much older known records; others displayed features previously associated with later examples. Such discoveries change the apparent distribution of organisms through time. They do not resurrect an extinct lineage. They expose intervals during which its history had gone unrecorded or unrecognised.[2]

These communities also discourage a picture of the entire Early Triassic as uniformly empty. Local complexity and widespread environmental difficulty can coexist. The question becomes where suitable conditions persisted, how long they lasted and how representative each preserved window really is.

A 2025 modelling study examined a further obstacle to recovery: the possibility that environmental disturbance could keep recurring without a new enormous eruption initiating every episode. Zi-Heng Li and colleagues investigated coupled changes in phosphorus, carbon and oxygen, comparing their behaviour with geochemical records.[3]

In their model, oxygen-poor conditions affected the burial and recycling of phosphorus. Nutrient availability then influenced biological production and oxygen demand. Under some conditions, these interacting processes produced repeated oscillations rather than a smooth return to equilibrium.[3]

This supplies a mechanism by which an initial disturbance could leave the system unstable. It does not demonstrate that later volcanism was absent, or that each fossil community experienced identical cycles. The model's assumptions about nutrients and seafloor conditions remain part of the explanation, not observations that can be omitted from it.

Recovery therefore has several clocks. Diversity may increase before former ecological relationships return; an intricate food web may exist while surrounding environments remain disturbed. No single fossil or chemical curve measures every aspect of rebuilding.

The land record now poses a related puzzle. In some post-extinction assemblages, one kind of vertebrate became extraordinarily common. Was that abundance a return to normality, or evidence of a world still profoundly altered?
