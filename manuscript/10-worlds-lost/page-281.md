### WHEN HABITAT BECAME UNLIVABLE

An extinction mechanism must reach the living organism. Dating an intrusion can establish when magma arrived, but it cannot by itself explain why one animal disappeared while another survived. The next part of the investigation asks what changed in the space between an organism's requirements and its surroundings.

In 2018, Justin Penn and colleagues combined simulations of the Permian–Triassic ocean with information about the oxygen requirements of living marine animals. Their measure, the **Metabolic Index**, estimated the scope for activity supported by oxygen under different conditions. Warming and oxygen loss could reduce that scope together.[1]

The model made a prediction that could be checked against fossils: losses should differ geographically. Its predicted pattern included relatively lower extinction intensity in the tropics. A spatial analysis of the marine fossil record supported that pattern. The researchers calculated that the combined stresses could explain more than half the extinction's magnitude.[1]

That is a conditional result, not a count of animals whose cause of death was observed. Traits measured in modern species inform the reconstruction; they are not measurements taken from Permian animals. Nevertheless, matching a geographical pattern tests more than the broad assertion that a warmer ocean was dangerous.

Ocean chemistry supplies another line of enquiry. A 2015 study used boron isotopes in marine carbonate rocks from what is now the United Arab Emirates, together with a carbon-cycle model, to reconstruct changes in seawater pH. The reconstruction indicated relatively stable pH during an earlier extinction pulse and a sharp decline during a later one. The authors linked that later acidification to losses among heavily calcified organisms.[2]

A 2020 investigation used boron isotopes in fossil brachiopod shells from the Tethys shelf. Its reconstruction placed a substantial pH decline at the onset of extinction in the latest Permian. Combined with modelling, the evidence supported carbon release, acidification and subsequent oxygen depletion as connected processes.[3]

The two accounts cannot simply be merged into one precisely timed acidification event. They use different archives and reconstructions. Their agreement that carbon-cycle disruption could alter marine chemistry is broader than their agreement about the sequence. Establishing that a stress occurred, locating it in time and assigning its share of the losses remain separate tasks.[2,3]

A further model published in 2026 explored why major warming appears to lag the initial carbon-isotope disturbance in the records it combined. Shihan Li and colleagues proposed that increased erosion exposed minerals to weathering, temporarily counteracting some effects of carbon release. At the same time, nutrients carried to the sea could stimulate production and the oxygen consumption accompanying decomposition.[4]

In that scenario, deterioration in marine oxygen availability could develop before the strongest warming. The authors' model reproduces several geochemical trends, but it does not directly measure ancient erosion rates or establish one globally uniform cause of death.[4]

The resulting explanation is a network of testable connections, not a list of interchangeable disasters. Heat, oxygen supply and seawater chemistry could act together, while their timing and severity differed. A successful explanation must account for both the environmental evidence and the pattern of biological losses.

Survival, however, introduces a different question. Once the sharpest losses had passed, what prevented the remaining organisms from immediately rebuilding the world around them?
