### MORE THAN ONE DEVONIAN CRISIS

The phrase “Late Devonian extinction” can conceal a sequence. The Kellwasser crises affected marine communities around the transition between the Frasnian and Famennian divisions of the period. The Hangenberg crisis came later, near the Devonian's end. Placing every observation under one label would erase the time between them.[1–3]

To investigate the earlier losses, we can look beyond the animals' bodies to their disturbance of sediment. Burrows and other traces record activity within the seabed. Their presence, variety and relationship to the surrounding layers help reveal when bottom-dwelling communities could occupy a place.

A 2026 study compared Upper Devonian shale successions at five North American sites. The researchers combined traces with sedimentary and geological evidence to examine episodes of oxygen depletion. Many affected intervals contained relatively simple traces or little evidence of animals mixing the sediment.[1]

Yet the pattern was not uniform. Parts of the inner Illinois Basin retained more varied traces than more openly connected marine settings. The authors interpreted these areas as refuges for bottom-dwelling communities. A widespread crisis could therefore coexist with local persistence; a dark layer in one basin was not a map of conditions everywhere.[1]

Another 2026 investigation approached the problem through a nutrient. Matthew Dodd and colleagues measured phosphate associated with carbonate minerals, comparing Devonian records from Australia, China and Nevada as part of a wider study. Similar changes across those sections supported episodes of increased phosphorus availability in seawater.[2]

More nutrients can support more biological production, yet that need not benefit every inhabitant. When organic material is broken down using oxygen, increased production can increase demand on the oxygen supply. The researchers tested this connection in a model linking nutrient cycles, climate and ocean chemistry.[2]

The agreement was informative but incomplete. The paper discussed differences between model predictions, chemical records and extinction horizons. Phosphorus might contribute to a crisis or prolong difficult conditions without being the first cause of every loss. The chemical signal also did not, by itself, decide whether the extra nutrient originated on land or was recycled within the sea.[2]

At the much later Devonian–Carboniferous transition, another kind of evidence came from land plants. A 2020 study described malformed spores preserved in East Greenland. Their normally patterned outer structures were irregular, sometimes with distorted or fused projections. Some spores also differed in pigmentation.[3]

John Marshall and colleagues interpreted the damage as evidence of unusually strong ultraviolet-B exposure associated with a weakened protective ozone layer. They linked it to the terrestrial disruption around the Hangenberg crisis. The preserved structures establish the abnormalities; the radiation and atmospheric explanation is the authors' reconstruction.[3]

These spores do not supply an ozone sample from the ancient atmosphere. Nor can their proposed explanation simply be carried backwards to account for the earlier Kellwasser losses. A mechanism suggested for one interval must meet the evidence of another before the two can be joined.

The Devonian therefore gives us a warning against seeking one answer to several different questions. Marine oxygen loss, nutrient cycling and damage to terrestrial reproduction concern interacting systems, but their records occupy particular places and times. Explaining the whole sequence requires preserving those distinctions.

The investigation next moves much farther forward, to the end of the Permian. There, an immense episode of magmatism and a severe biological crisis pose a more sharply timed question: did the volcanic history and the losses actually coincide?
