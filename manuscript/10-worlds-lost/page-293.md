### THE CHANGING GREEN WORLD

The plants beneath a dinosaur's feet were not an unchanging background. Yet the appearance of a new plant group, the spread of its members and a transformation in ecosystem function need not occur together. Fossils offer different ways of testing each stage.

An early flowering plant need not resemble a modern garden flower. In 2015, Bernard Gomez and colleagues re-examined *Montsechia*, preserved in Early Cretaceous rocks of Spain. Its reproductive structures enclosed single seeds. Slender branching shoots and other anatomical features supported an aquatic way of life.[1]

The combination placed a flowering plant within an unexpected setting without establishing that all flowering plants originated underwater. A fossil can reveal an early ecological possibility while leaving the broader origin question open. Its importance does not depend on appointing it the first member of an entire group.

Insect associations require another kind of evidence. Early Cretaceous Spanish amber contains thrips carrying abundant gymnosperm pollen. A study published in 2012 described specialised hairs associated with those grains and interpreted them as structures used in pollen collection and transport.[3]

These were relationships with seed plants other than flowering plants. The fossils therefore prevent a simple account in which flowers invented every insect–plant pollination partnership. Their strongest evidence is the physical association between bodies and pollen; the complete sequence of visits that produced it is not preserved.[3]

A contrasting example was described in 2019 from mid-Cretaceous Myanmar amber. The beetle *Angimordella burmitina* carried pollen with three grooves, characteristic of the eudicot branch of flowering plants. Microscopy revealed grains attached to body hairs, while X-ray imaging helped reconstruct mouthparts suited to pollen feeding.[4]

Body form, mouthparts and attached pollen together supported the authors' interpretation of a flower visitor involved in pollination. That combination is stronger than merely finding an insect beside a plant fragment. It still does not show a completed transfer between two flowers or the fertilisation that might have followed.[4]

Reproduction was only part of the changing plant world. Taylor Feild and colleagues investigated fossil leaves in 2011, measuring the length of veins within a given area. Early angiosperm leaves in their sample overlapped the relatively sparse venation of contemporary ferns and gymnosperms; later examples contained denser networks.[2]

More closely spaced veins can bring water nearer the tissues where evaporation occurs. Comparison with living leaves therefore connected the fossil pattern to increased potential for water supply and gas exchange. It did not directly measure photosynthesis in a Cretaceous forest. The authors also cautioned that gaps in sampling could make a gradual increase appear as two separate surges.[2]

The distinction brings us back to the ecosystem. A reproductive innovation does not automatically confer every physiological capacity found in later relatives. Nor does a leaf capable of rapid exchange establish how much vegetation covered an entire continent. Each scale requires additional evidence.

Pollen on an insect and veins in a leaf thus reveal different connections within the same changing world. One concerns reproduction and movement between plants; the other concerns the machinery supporting growth. Together they make the living landscape more than scenery around the animals.

Change, however, is not the same as collapse. To investigate the crisis that ended the Cretaceous, we must turn from these longer transformations to a narrow geological boundary—and ask what crossed it.
