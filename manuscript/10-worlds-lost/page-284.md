### A MAP IS NOT A BIRTHPLACE

The search for the first dinosaurs begins with a problem of recognition. A bone must be identified before its age can extend the history of a group. Even then, the place where it was buried need not be the place where that group originated.

An upper-arm bone and several vertebrae from Tanzania illustrate the difficulty. Described as *Nyasasaurus parringtoni* in a study published online in 2012, the material combined features associated with dinosaurs. Microscopic bone structure also indicated rapid growth. The researchers nevertheless retained two possibilities: a member of Dinosauria, or a very close relative outside it.[1]

That qualification is not a minor footnote to an otherwise certain winner. It controls what the fossil can establish. A specimen outside the group can illuminate the anatomy near its origin without itself becoming the oldest dinosaur. Dating the rock more precisely would not, by itself, settle the identification.

The study placed the Tanzanian succession in the Middle Triassic through comparisons with other fossil assemblages. Its proposed position was therefore supported by both anatomy and geological context, but neither provided an uncertainty-free starting date. More complete specimens could test interpretations that the surviving fragments leave open.[1]

A contrasting discovery came from Zimbabwe. In 2022, Christopher Griffin and colleagues described a Carnian assemblage containing the nearly complete skeleton of *Mbiresaurus raathi*. Its anatomy placed it among sauropodomorph dinosaurs. The surrounding vertebrate assemblage resembled dinosaur-bearing communities known elsewhere in southern Pangaea.[2]

Having much of a skeleton changes the investigation. Researchers can compare combinations across several body regions, rather than ask one fragment to carry the whole identification. Yet completeness does not make an animal ancestral to every later member of its group. A well-preserved branch remains a branch.

Nor does a cluster of discoveries automatically locate a birthplace. A map of fossils combines an ancient biological distribution with the uneven survival, exposure and examination of rocks. An empty area might mean absence, but it might also mean that the relevant evidence has not been sampled.

A 2025 study led by Joel Heath made that unevenness part of its analysis. The researchers modelled the early distribution of dinosaurs and their relatives, tested alternative evolutionary trees and allowed sampling to vary across regions and time. Many results favoured an origin at low latitudes in Gondwana, the southern portion of Pangaea.[3]

Support depended partly on the assumed relationships of silesaurids, animals whose position near early dinosaur branches remains debated. The low-latitude result was strongest when these animals were placed within the early ornithischian branch of dinosaurs. Changing a relationship could therefore change the inferred geography.[3]

The model did not discover a hidden skeleton or identify an exact birthplace. It proposed where an incompletely sampled history might fit. That is useful because it makes the geographical explanation answerable to new finds, while leaving the distinction between a prediction and a discovery intact.

The first dinosaurs thus emerge through several tests, not one triumphant label: anatomical identity, geological age, evolutionary relationship and distribution. Once those questions are separated, another becomes clearer. On a supercontinent, what could limit the movement of animals even where no ocean divided the land?
