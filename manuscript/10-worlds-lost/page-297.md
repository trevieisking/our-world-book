### WHICH BIRDS CROSSED THE BOUNDARY?

Birds carried one branch of dinosaur history beyond the end-Cretaceous catastrophe. But the statement that birds survived conceals another loss: many other bird lineages disappeared. Possessing feathers, or even the capacity for flight, did not make every member of this wider radiation secure.[1]

One explanation begins with the landscape. In 2018, Daniel Field and colleagues combined plant and pollen evidence for widespread forest destruction with reconstructions of ancestral bird lifestyles. Their analysis favoured predominantly ground-dwelling or otherwise non-arboreal ancestors among the lineages crossing the boundary. Tree-dependent ways of life evolved repeatedly afterwards.[1]

This connected a change in habitat with a proposed pattern of survival. It did not recover the daily movements of every ancestral bird. Relationships and comparisons among organisms were needed to reconstruct those lifestyles; the surviving fossils supplied only part of the history.

The distinction matters because the same disturbance can affect organisms differently. Removing a forest changes more than the number of standing trunks. An explanation involving habitat dependence must connect that loss to the requirements of the animals, rather than assume that everything able to fly could simply leave the problem behind.

Diet offers another test. In 2016, Derek Larson and colleagues examined thousands of teeth from bird-like dinosaurs. The range of tooth forms in their sampled groups did not decline significantly towards the boundary. That pattern challenged a simple account of these animals gradually losing their ecological variety before the final crisis.[2]

The authors also proposed that seed consumption could have helped some toothless, beaked lineages survive a disruption in other food supplies. This was a dietary hypothesis, not a seed recovered from the stomach of a bird known to have crossed the boundary. Tooth shape, a reconstructed diet and successful survival remain separate links in the argument.[2]

A new fossil described in September 2026 approached the question through the covering of the body. Jingmai O'Connor and colleagues investigated feathers inside a theropod coprolite from the Hell Creek Formation. Associated bones suggested a hesperornithiform, a flightless aquatic bird outside the group containing all living birds.[3]

One feather preserved a stiff, lightweight shaft with a square outline and an internal filling. Its dense branches supported a water-resistant function. Other feathers retained structures unlike those of living birds. The authors proposed that differences in body plumage, and therefore potentially insulation, might have contributed to extinction during the impact winter.[3]

The preservation is exceptional, but the causal claim remains a proposal. Neither the temperature inside that bird nor its capacity to withstand prolonged cold was directly measured. The coprolite records a bird being consumed before the boundary; it is not a death certificate linking that individual to the impact.

Habitat, food and insulation therefore offer distinct ways to investigate selectivity. They need not compete as mutually exclusive explanations. A trait can matter under one set of conditions without being sufficient under another, and a lineage surviving does not mean that most of its individuals survived.

The emerging history is not a contest won by a universally superior bird. It is an investigation of organisms with different requirements meeting a rapidly altered world. The same question must now be asked beneath the sea: what survived when the foundations of marine food webs changed?
