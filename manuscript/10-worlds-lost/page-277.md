### THE LAST FOSSIL IS NOT THE LAST ANIMAL

Imagine following a kind of shell upwards through a sequence of rocks. It appears in one layer, then another, then no more. Somewhere above your last specimen, the history of that animal continues—or ends. The highest fossil you have found does not automatically mark the death of the last living member of its species.

This distinction is the starting point for investigating extinction. **Extinction** means the disappearance of a lineage, not merely its departure from one place or its absence from one collection. Rocks preserve observations from which that larger event must be reconstructed.

In 1982, Philip Signor and Jere Lipps examined how incomplete sampling could distort the apparent timing of a biological crisis. Suppose several species disappear together, but their final populations leave no fossils that we recover. Their last known specimens will fall at different, earlier points. An abrupt extinction can acquire the appearance of a gradual decline.[1]

This **Signor–Lipps effect** does not prove that every decline conceals a catastrophe. It shows why the pattern of collected fossils cannot, by itself, decide between those possibilities. An uncommon animal, or one whose habitat is poorly represented, is particularly likely to have a misleadingly shortened recorded history.[1]

The rocks can introduce a different distortion. In 2015, Steven Holland and Mark Patzkowsky combined numerical models of evolution, marine habitats and sediment accumulation. Even with a constant extinction rate, their simulated last occurrences clustered at particular kinds of geological surface.[2]

Some clusters formed where deposition stopped or erosion removed part of the record. Others arose because changing water depth altered which organisms were likely to occur at the sampled location. A row of apparent endings could therefore reflect the history of a habitat and its sediments, rather than one simultaneous killing event.[2]

The authors proposed tests involving comparisons between shallower and deeper settings and between deposits representing similar environments. This turns a warning into a method. Instead of accepting every cluster as a crisis—or dismissing every crisis as a sampling problem—we can ask what competing explanations predict.[2]

A study of much younger fossils illustrates the value of that approach. In 1996, Charles Marshall and Peter Ward statistically examined late Cretaceous marine molluscs from the western European Tethys region. Their results distinguished a major loss near the period boundary from earlier, more gradual losses among certain bivalves.[3]

Another change among ammonites could represent either extinction or altered abundance associated with retreating seas. The investigators did not force every disappearance into the same category. One succession could contain background losses, environmental shifts and a larger crisis, while still leaving uncertainty about particular species.[3]

The important lesson is not that we can never know when a world changed. It is that establishing a loss, estimating its duration and identifying its cause are separate tasks. A proposed cause must explain the evidence at the appropriate scale; a local disappearance cannot silently become a global event.

With these distinctions in place, the investigation can move beyond the empty space above a fossil. We will compare biological losses with the physical changes recorded around them. Our first case takes us back before the forests and vertebrate limbs of the previous chapter, to an Ordovician world whose most abundant animal record lies beneath the sea.
