### BEFORE THE OPPORTUNITY

To begin mammalian history at the dinosaur extinction would remove much of the evidence needed to explain what followed. Long before Chicxulub, mammals and their close extinct relatives had occupied more varied roles than the familiar image of a tiny insect-eater hiding beneath giant feet suggests.[1–3]

The broader group, **mammaliaforms**, includes mammals and close relatives outside the group containing all living mammals. This distinction allows a fossil to illuminate early mammalian biology without requiring it to be a direct ancestor of a living species. A branch can reveal an important capability even if that branch later ends.

In 2006, Qiang Ji and colleagues described *Castorocauda* from Jurassic rocks in China. It preserved fur and a broad, flattened, partly scaly tail. Features of the skeleton supported swimming and digging, while its teeth suggested feeding associated with water.[1]

The resulting reconstruction combined structures that invited comparisons with beavers, otters and platypuses. Those comparisons explain function, not identity. The fossil was not an ancient beaver, nor does a similar tail establish a shared origin of every swimming adaptation. Its importance lies in the particular combination actually preserved.

A different route through the environment appeared in Jurassic fossils described in 2017. Qing-Jin Meng and colleagues examined *Maiopatagium* and related material preserving skin membranes alongside the skeleton. Limb and digit proportions supported gliding and life associated with trees.[2]

The shoulder arrangement differed from that of living marsupial and placental gliders. Similar airborne possibilities could therefore emerge through different anatomical configurations. Here, soft-tissue preservation strengthened an inference that limb proportions alone would have supported less directly.[2]

These were not bats performing powered flight. A membrane capable of helping an animal glide does not establish repeated flapping or the ability to climb through the air under its own power. Nor does a fossil limb specify the height of a tree or the length of a particular journey.

Food remains supplied another challenge to the stereotype. In 2005, Yaoming Hu and colleagues reported juvenile *Psittacosaurus* bones among the stomach contents of *Repenomamus robustus*, a mammal from Early Cretaceous China. The consumed animal was a young horned-dinosaur relative, not an insect.[3]

The same paper described a larger species of *Repenomamus*, extending the known body-size range of these animals. But it was the stomach contents in *R. robustus* that supplied the feeding evidence. The larger skeleton and the meal must not be combined into an individual that the researchers never found.[3]

Even the meal has a boundary. It establishes consumption more securely than the circumstances in which the food was obtained. Without additional evidence, we cannot watch an attack, determine how often dinosaurs were eaten or extend a juvenile's fate to large adults.

Swimming adaptations, preserved membranes and swallowed bones thus establish different parts of an already varied history. The examples come from separate places and times; they do not form one reconstructed community or a catalogue of lineages known to survive the final Cretaceous crisis.

That distinction prepares the next investigation. Mammalian expansion after the impact was not the beginning of all mammalian diversity. It followed severe losses within a group that already possessed varied ways of living. Which survivors multiplied, and how did the recovering environment shape what happened next?
