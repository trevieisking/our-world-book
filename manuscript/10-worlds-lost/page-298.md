### SURVIVAL BELOW THE SURFACE

A damaged ocean is not necessarily an empty ocean. Nor does the return of abundant microscopic life mean that its former relationships have returned. To investigate the aftermath of Chicxulub, researchers must distinguish the organisms present from the work they performed within the marine system.

In 2019, Michael Henehan and colleagues analysed boron isotopes in fossil foraminifera, single-celled organisms with mineral shells. Their reconstruction indicated a rapid fall in surface-water pH after the impact. Combined with modelling, the evidence supported acidification as part of the marine crisis.[1]

The same analysis distinguished the production of new organic material from its transfer into deeper water. A modelled reduction of roughly half in global marine primary production could explain the carbon-isotope pattern without requiring a wholly lifeless sea. Production and efficient transport of carbon to depth recovered on different schedules.[1]

Food acquisition adds another distinction. In 2020, Samantha Gibbs and colleagues combined plankton microfossils with ecological and evolutionary models. They argued that an ability to ingest prey helped some algal plankton survive the interruption of sunlight and contributed to subsequent recovery.[2]

These organisms could combine photosynthesis with obtaining food from other cells, a strategy called **mixotrophy**. In the reconstructed aftermath, abundant small prey and reduced pressure from larger consumers favoured this flexibility. The conclusion concerns capacities inferred from fossils and comparisons, not organisms deliberately choosing a new strategy when the sky darkened.[2]

A feeding role also cannot be recovered merely by counting shells. Two communities with comparable numbers of organisms might route energy and carbon differently. Rebuilding abundance, diversity and ecological connections are related processes, but none provides a complete measure of the others.

The crater itself tested an intuitive prediction. If proximity to the impact imposed a lasting environmental barrier, recovery should have been especially slow there. Christopher Lowery and colleagues reported in 2018 that microfossils and traces instead indicated life returning within years, followed by a productive marine community.[3]

The finding did not establish that those organisms had survived the collision in place. Recolonisation is different from uninterrupted residence. It did challenge the assumption that distance from the impact alone determined how long recovery took.[3]

Dates for the wider rebuilding also required scrutiny. In January 2026, Lowery and colleagues used published helium-3 records from six boundary sites to estimate sediment accumulation rates. They avoided assuming that sediment had accumulated at the same rate before and after the catastrophe.[4]

Their recalibration placed the first appearance of one widely used plankton marker between about 3,500 and 11,100 years after the boundary, depending on the site. Other new forms appeared earlier, in some cases within a modelled interval of less than 2,000 years. The analysis thus shortened an important part of the recovery chronology.[4]

First appearance is not a witnessed birth of a species. The authors noted differences between sites and disagreements over which fossil forms deserve separate names. Evolution, dispersal and preservation all influence the record used to reconstruct that sequence.[4]

The ocean's return therefore cannot be assigned one date. Chemical conditions, carbon transport, local communities and new lineages each leave different records. Their histories can overlap without proceeding together. Ashore, the question is equally demanding: did recovering vegetation rebuild the old forests, or establish something different?
