### THE LIVES OF A SURVIVOR

Abundant fossils can conceal a difficult life. A community dominated by one kind of animal need not be a community in which individuals lived long or conditions were favourable. To understand the post-extinction success of *Lystrosaurus*, researchers have looked beyond the number of skeletons to the histories recorded inside them.

This beaked plant-eater belonged to the synapsid branch of vertebrate evolution, which also contains mammals. It was not a dinosaur, nor does its position among mammalian relatives establish that it was our direct ancestor. Its fossils offer evidence about a successful lineage in its own right.[1,3]

A 2016 investigation combined bone microstructure and body-size information from South African therapsids, the wider group to which *Lystrosaurus* belonged. Patterns of growth and size were consistent with shortened development and increased mortality among post-extinction species. Small bodies could therefore reflect abbreviated lives as well as differences in the size a fully grown animal could reach.[1]

Population models explored what might help such animals persist. Earlier reproduction reduced extinction risk under the unstable conditions tested. The authors proposed shortened generation times as part of the explanation for survival. The fossils did not preserve a record of mating ages: that part of the argument combined growth evidence, comparisons and modelling.[1]

A different record lay inside the tusks. In 2020, Megan Whitney and Christian Sidor compared growth marks in *Lystrosaurus* from Antarctica and South Africa. The Antarctic samples showed repeated intervals of stress that differed from the steadier growth in the comparison material.[2]

The researchers interpreted these patterns as preliminary evidence of **torpor**, a reduction in metabolic activity during unfavourable conditions. Antarctic animals faced strong seasonal changes in daylight, even though their environment was unlike the continent's modern ice-covered interior. A capacity to reduce activity could have helped them endure those changes.[2]

The comparison is not a preserved scene of hibernation. Growth disturbances record a physiological response more directly than the exact behaviour producing it. The authors acknowledged the small, fragmentary sample and the difficulty of controlling every alternative source of variation. Extending the interpretation to all populations would go beyond the evidence.

In 2026, a study of three very young South African specimens opened another part of the life cycle. One small skeleton was tightly curled, lacked tusks and retained an incompletely joined lower jaw. Scanning revealed smooth, unfinished bone margins rather than a gap produced by breakage.[3]

The investigators argued that this combination supported an embryo that had died before hatching. Curling alone was insufficient: older animals in the same regional record can also be preserved curled up. The developmental anatomy supplied the additional test.[3]

No calcified eggshell survived. A soft covering remained a proposed explanation, not a material identified in the specimen. Comparisons using the reconstructed egg size suggested relatively well-developed hatchlings, but any advantage of that reproductive strategy in surviving environmental disruption remained a further hypothesis.[3]

Together, these studies replace the vague idea of a hardy survivor with questions about growth, seasonal stress and reproduction. Their answers have different levels of certainty. Abundance establishes neither comfort nor immortality; survival can coexist with repeated individual hardship.

Other branches were also diversifying across the changing Triassic world. The investigation now turns to dinosaurs—not as the inevitable successors to these survivors, but as one emerging group among several.
