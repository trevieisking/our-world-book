### THE BRANCHES THAT ENDED

A history built only from living mammals would leave out an important part of the world after Chicxulub. Some lineages crossed that boundary, diversified and later disappeared. Their extinction does not make their earlier biology a failed rehearsal for the animals that remain.

Multituberculates provide a particularly useful example. In 2012, Gregory Wilson and colleagues compared their teeth and reconstructed body sizes through time. The range of dental forms broadened well before the end-Cretaceous extinction, alongside increasing diversity and a wider range of body sizes.[1]

Greater tooth complexity and larger maximum sizes supported increased plant eating. The expansion continued across the boundary, linking the group's history to changing ecological opportunities rather than one instantaneous release from dinosaurs. That continuity does not establish that every population survived or that every food supply remained unchanged.[1]

Their eventual disappearance posed another question. Could competition with rodents explain why a once-diverse mammal group ended? Superficial similarity would not be enough. The explanation needed a mechanism connecting the bodies of the proposed competitors to their use of resources.

A 2019 investigation compared digital skull models of four living rodents with *Kryptobaatar*, a Cretaceous multituberculate. Computer simulations tested feeding stresses and mechanical performance. The rodents generated bite forces effectively but experienced greater skull stresses, whereas the multituberculate model resisted some loads better.[2]

The authors argued that the rodent balance of these properties could have favoured competition. The comparison supplied a possible functional mechanism, not an observed replacement. Its models represented selected animals from different periods, rather than the complete set of competitors present during the later extinction.[2]

Another investigation approached the question geographically. Benjamin Burger's study, published online in 2025 and in a 2026 journal issue, compared North American Eocene multituberculate occurrences with plants and six contemporary rodent families. The reconstructed forest associations differed between the groups.[3]

Multituberculates were especially associated with wet forests containing dawn redwoods and swamp cypresses. Burger favoured the later loss of these habitats over direct rodent competition as the main explanation for their disappearance. Spatial association, however, does not preserve a meal or directly measure competition.[3]

The two investigations therefore examine different links in a causal argument. A skull can possess a useful feeding mechanism without proving which resources an animal actually contested. A distribution can track a habitat without independently isolating the reason a population vanished. Combining those scales is the challenge, not choosing whichever image seems more persuasive.

This is also why an extinct branch belongs at the chapter's end. A surviving lineage does not represent every possibility that existed beside it, and surviving one catastrophe does not guarantee survival through later changes. The present is a selective sample of the history, not its intended destination.

Across the evidence examined here, disappearance has required more than a last fossil, and recovery has required more than renewed abundance. Dates, anatomical structures, chemical traces and models become most informative when their separate claims can be tested against one another.

The next chapter follows one branch through that larger history. Its eventual human members will not be treated as a target towards which every earlier body was moving. We begin instead with a practical question: what surviving evidence allows us to recognise an early primate?
